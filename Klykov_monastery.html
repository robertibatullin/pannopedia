Klykov monastery

Klykov monastery (Zgurian and Deglian: Klykov monastyr; Glagolitic: ⰍⰎⰬⰍⰑⰂ ⰏⰑⰐⰀⰔⰕⰬⰓ) is a Catholic monastic complex in Former Upper Pannonia, occupying pillar rocks above the South Drava valley.

The Klyci ("Fangs") are three perfectly vertical sandstone pillar rocks, each roughly 20 metres in diameter and 350–400 metres tall. They stand in a row, separated by 5–10 metre gaps from each other and from the steep, pinewood-covered slope of Skull Mountain (Lubovincza Gora). The rocks are connected by rope bridges only. From the mountain outward, the pillars are named the Son's, the Father's, and the Holy Spirit's.

Foundation

St. Geonus the Stylite, a Byzantine hermit monk, is said to have been the first to settle on the Holy Spirit's rock in the sixth century. A monastic community formed over the following centuries. The original rules forbade all instruments; the early monks carved their living niches and footholds by hand alone, though wooden and stone tools were permitted much later. The original dietary rules were stricter still: monks ate only what the rocks provided — moss, insects, and bird droppings. These rules, too, were eventually relaxed.

Middle Ages

In the tenth century the monastery became a sanctuary for clerics of the Glagolitic tradition, which had arrived in Pannonia with Slavic communities displaced from Great Moravia. When St. Tibor, the first bishop of Bregovo, imposed the Latin rite in 1040 and prohibited Glagolitic worship, Klykov remained the sole exception. It claimed perpetual autonomy under the Decretum Ioannis, a purported ninth-century papal grant. The bishops of Bregovo disputed its authenticity for centuries, but local barons hostile to the Bregovo see aligned with the monastery and kept the Slavic rite alive. By the eleventh century the complex had grown into a large system of artificial caves housing dozens of monks: carved labyrinths approximately 500 metres in total length, with cells, chapels, churches, dormitories, refectories, and storage rooms. Benedictine rules were adopted as late as 1060.

St. Nectarius (lived 1267–1339, abbot since 1310) was renowned as a manuscript collector, encouraging scribal activity; local legend remembered him as a great magician capable of commanding demons. The monastery was materially supported by local peasants and received donations from kings and barons. It never ceased to function, though at times — especially during the Ottoman era — the number of monks was reduced to one.

Glypholatry

To justify their adherence to Glagolitic, Klykov monks developed what they called the "glyphic discipline" — an esoteric teaching resembling Kabbalah. Each Glagolitic character was said to represent an aspect of God, and their combinations to carry mystical meaning. The discipline prescribed specific prayers consisting of character names (az, bowky, and so on) recited in seemingly meaningless sequences. Combined with strict asceticism, this practice was believed to develop miraculous powers and lead to sanctity. A papal decree of 1509 condemned the teaching as glypholatry — character worship — but it continued to be practiced at Klykov well into the nineteenth century, if not later.

Later periods

Enjo Zslutovodyj's research brought wider attention to Klykov, which he promoted as a site of Slavic national heritage. From the mid-nineteenth century the monastery was patronized by Slavic Revival enthusiasts. Vilmosz Komarovicki, an outstanding composer, spent his final years there as a monk.

Under Communist rule, the number of monks was gradually reduced, as no ordination of new monks was permitted. By 1990, only two elderly monks remained. The monastery was nonetheless treated as a national heritage site: extensive research was conducted and the entire manuscript collection was published through 1960s and 1970s.

Present state

The library holds approximately 500 volumes, mostly liturgical. Legends of secret rooms containing forbidden glypholatric literature have not been confirmed. The monastery currently houses ten monks, recognizable by their black Benedictine habits and rosaries with Glagolitic characters. By Klykov rule, ordained monks may not leave the rocks and are therefore rarely seen up close. Tourism is not encouraged; only religious pilgrimage is permitted, and strictly limited.
