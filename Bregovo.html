Bregovo

Bregovo (Glagolitic: ⰁⰓⰅⰃⰑⰂⰑ, from breg "bank, shore") is a former town of Former Upper Pannonia.

Bregovo stood on the left bank of South Drava, 40 km east of Vronovica, opposite Gowbkaslava, originally enclosed by a near-circular river bend. After the construction of the Gowbka hydroelectric power plant raised the river level, what remained of the town drowned; only the ruins of the former bishop's castle survive, now completely surrounded by water.

Middle Ages

Inhabited from prehistoric times, the site held a Celtic settlement, a Roman castellum, and an early medieval Slavic fort with an important pagan sanctuary. Pre-Christian worship continued until 1040, when St. Tibor from Esztergom and his followers destroyed the temple and founded a Christian church; Tibor was ordained the first Bishop of Upper Pannonia, subordinate to the Archbishop of Esztergom. The new see waged a sustained struggle against the Slavic liturgy and Glagolitic tradition centered at Klykov monastery.

In 1115, Hungarian king Béla II granted the Bregovo bishops the title of Prince Bishop (Pannonian: knjuz biskup), conferring some secular power over Upper Pannonia. They pursued authority over the lesser lords of the Pannonian territory and often maintained semi-official concubines whose children — nominally nephews or pupils — they regularly appointed as heirs.

The see produced a succession of notable incumbents. Miklosz (mid 12th c.) built a fortified castle, fiercely fought for sovereignty over the local lords, and personally commanded in battles. Janosz (late 12th c.) was dismissed by the Archbishop of Esztergom, refused to obey, launched an armed revolt, and won. Tamasz (mid 13th c.) was seized in Bregovo by invading Mongols but survived the siege. Benedict "the Butcher" (mid 14th c.) became notorious for harsh treatment of his subordinates. Peter "the Beardless" (early 15th c.) was widely believed to be a daughter, not a son, of the previous bishop — a legend unconfirmed, though he certainly had male partners. Djordji "the Slaver" (mid 15th c.) fabulously enriched himself by selling his subjects into Ottoman slavery, and rebuilt the Bregovo cathedral in the Florentine Renaissance style.

The late 15th century brought the most turbulent succession. Brothers Boleslav and Lukas, sons of Djordji, fought a ten-year armed conflict over the inheritance, both bribing Pope Alexander Borgia. In 1505, Pope Julius II dismissed and excommunicated both brothers and installed an Italian bishop with no connection to the dynasty; the newcomer was promptly poisoned by Boleslav's son Maczasz, who then seized the seat.

Ottoman era

Maczasz "the Renegade" (early 16th c.) proved the undoing of the see: after the Ottoman victory at Mohacs, he offered the Sultan to surrender Bregovo and convert to Islam in exchange for sovereign rule over Upper Pannonia as an Ottoman vassal. He let the Turks enter the city and castle in 1529, but they pillaged and burned it, and impaled Maczasz himself by order of Sultan Suleiman. The latter despised the traitor and had no need of his service, having already established a strong hold on former Hungary.

An Ottoman commander, Haqim Abaz Bey Kuzgun, initially planned to make the castle a stronghold. He learned by divination, however, that the site was cursed; instead, he founded Kuzgun Qalesi — the future Vronovica. From this time, the city and the castle became abandoned, the episcopal seat titular. Descendants of the late prince bishops, the Bregovi family, served Hungarian and Austrian monarchs.

Later periods

After the Austrian recapture of Upper Pannonia, some property was restored to the Bregovi family — the castle ruins among them — though not the episcopal seat. In the 1820s and 1830s, Gergej Gabriel Bregovi partly reconstructed the castle in neo-Gothic style and made it the family residence once again. His grandson Adalfrid Attila, a renowned traveller and collector, turned it into a repository for his extensive ethnographic holdings. The castle remained in the Bregovi hands until 1944; the former city below had by then dwindled to a village inhabited mainly by the castle's servants.

In the autumn of 1944, SS forces occupied the castle as a defensive position, using its river-bend situation to cover South Drava crossings. Soviet forces shelled it for several weeks; a tower collapsed and the main hall was gutted. Retreating German troops removed the most portable pieces of Adalfrid Attila's collection; Soviet forces stripped the remainder. The Bregovi survived the fighting and remained in the castle through the liberation. They were expelled and the property nationalized after the Communist government consolidated its hold.

Current state

When the South Drava dam raised the water level in the 1950s, the ruins of the former town drowned completely, and the castle's lower floors disappeared below the waterline. The castle is now almost inaccessible: the only entrance lies underwater, the walls high and vertical. It is rumored to be a cursed place inhabited by ghosts; lights are sometimes reported, and voices heard. It belongs to the Zgurian state and has been on sale for €1 since 1998, on condition of repair within ten years. No buyer has come forward.
