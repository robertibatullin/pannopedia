Pannonian language

The Pannonian language (Zgurian: pannonska rjecz; Deglian: pannonska rёč) is a Slavic language spoken in the territory of Former Upper Pannonia. It is genealogically descended from the West Slavic dialects of Great Moravia. Following the southward displacement of its speakers during the Hungarian conquest of the late 9th and early 10th centuries, it underwent extensive phonological and lexical convergence with neighbouring South Slavic varieties — particularly Slovenian and the Serbo-Croatian (BCMS) group. The result is a language in a transitional position within the Slavic family: typologically closer to the western South Slavic languages than to its genealogical relatives Czech and Slovak, yet preserving numerous archaic features lost by most other members of either branch.

Modern Pannonian exists in two mutually intelligible official varieties — Zgurian (zgurska rjecz) and Deglian (deglänska rёč) — corresponding to the two unrecognised successor states of Former Upper Pannonia. The varieties are grammatically and lexically nearly identical; their principal distinction is orthographic. The unified language spoken and written before the 1992–1995 civil war was recorded exclusively in a local variant of the Glagolitic script; both post-war varieties employ Latin alphabets of differing conventions.

Terminology and political status

The designation "Pannonian language" was the official and widely used term throughout the successive political entities of the region: the Upper Pannonian Banate of Hungary (1850–1918), the (First) Republic of Upper Pannonia (1918–1934), the (First) Kingdom of Upper Pannonia (1934–1940), the (Second) People's Republic of Upper Pannonia (1945–1990), the (Third) Democratic Republic of Upper Pannonia (1990–1992), and the short-lived (Second) Kingdom of Upper Pannonia (1994).

Since the dissolution of Upper Pannonia the term has come to be regarded as outdated on political grounds. The two unrecognised successor states each maintain their own language standard — Zgurian and Deglian — insisting that these constitute distinct languages, despite their grammars and vocabularies being virtually identical. For simplicity, this article uses the term Pannonian to refer collectively to pre-1992 Pannonian, Zgurian, and Deglian, and employs Zgurian orthography throughout.

Writing system

Pannonian has been written in three distinct scripts across its documented history: the traditional Glagolitic alphabet of unified Pannonian, and the two Latin-based alphabets adopted by the successor varieties after 1992.

Glagolitic. The Pannonian Glagolitic tradition is claimed to be a genuine heritage of Cyril and Methodius's mission in Great Moravia and therefore a significant marker of pre-split Pannonian national identity. Its use in Upper Pannonia is attested from the medieval period, then revived by the 19th c. National Enlightenment movement, and survived until 1992. Following the Zguro-Deglian armed conflict and the political discrediting of a unified "Pannonian" identity, Glagolitic fell out of everyday use and is now largely unreadable by younger generations.

Zgurian Latin alphabet. Zgurian orthography avoids diacritics entirely, representing complex sounds by means of digraphs modelled partly on Polish conventions: cz [tʃ], sz [ʃ], dj [dʒ], zs [ʒ], cs [ʃtʃ]. Besides being a part of dj digraph, the letter j serves a dual function: as the consonant [j] in most positions, and — when it follows a consonant before a non-front vowel — as an explicit marker of consonant palatalisation (e.g. nj, kj).

Deglian Latin alphabet. Deglian orthography employs a broader set of diacritics, including characters familiar from other South Slavic traditions (č, š, ž, đ) alongside Deglian-specific forms. Palatalisation not indicated by a following vowel is marked by an acute accent written on the consonant (e.g. ŕ, ń).

The letters i and y form a complementary pair analogous to Russian и and ы: i appears after palatalised consonants, y elsewhere. The iotated vowel letters (ja, je, jo, ju) follow the same distributional logic as the Cyrillic iotated series, denoting both the preceding palatalisation and the vowel quality.

Phonology

Pannonian phonology reflects its dual inheritance: a West Slavic structural base reshaped by prolonged South Slavic contact. The most salient features, described relative to Old Church Slavonic (OCS), are set out below.

Consonantism

Retention of kv-, gv-, xv- clusters. Pannonian preserves the Proto-Slavic consonant clusters kv-, gv-, and xv- before front vowels without undergoing second palatalisation — a diagnostic West Slavic isogloss: kvjet "flower" (contrast BCMS cvijet/cvjet), gvjezda "star" (contrast BCMS zvijezda/zvezda).

West Slavic reflexes of Proto-Slavic *tj and *dj. Where OCS shows шт and жд (the South Slavic outcome of Proto-Slavic *tj and *dj), Pannonian has cz [tʃ] and dj [dʒ], in line with the West Slavic branch rather than the South.

Full hard/soft consonant opposition. Pannonian has developed a systematic palatalised/non-palatalised consonant opposition comparable in scope to Russian or Polish — a feature absent from BCMS. Under this system t palatalises to cz, d palatalises to dj [dʒ], and all other consonants acquire a palatalised counterpart (k → kj, n → nj, l → lj, etc.). The opposition is phonemically distinctive and is reflected fully in the writing systems of all three scripts.

Vocalism

Nasal vowels. Proto-Slavic nasal vowels are preserved in a conditioned split driven by stress. In stressed syllables ę diphthongises to ew and ǫ diphthongises to ow: pewcz "five" (< PS *pętь), rowka "hand" (< PS *rǫka). In unstressed syllables both nasals simply denasalise: ę > e, ǫ > o — pamecz "memory" (< PS *pamętь).

Yat ѣ. The front vowel ѣ merges with e but leaves a palatalisation trace on any immediately preceding consonant, yielding a phonemically distinct outcome from inherited e: bjely "white" (< OCS бѣлъ), where b- is palatalised by the preceding yat; contrast vesti "to lead" (< OCS вести) where original e triggers no such effect. In word-initial position and after a vowel, where no consonant is available to receive the palatalisation, yat regularly gives je: jesti "to eat".

Yer reduction. The development of the reduced vowels (yers) reflects both West Slavic and South Slavic influence:

  • Strong ъ > u, a unique innovation within Slavic: sun "sleep" (< OCS сънъ).
  • Strong ь > e, consistently palatalising the preceding consonant: prjevy "first" (< OCS прьвъ).
  • Weak ь, before disappearing, palatalises the preceding consonant: djenj "day" (< OCS дьнь, where d is palatalised to dj); czma "darkness" (< OCS тьма, where t is palatalised to cz).
  • Both weak yers surface as e — rather than disappearing — when they stand between two stop consonants, breaking up the resulting cluster: vidjeti "to see" (< OCS видьти, where weak ь between d and t both palatalises d to dj and surfaces as e).

Liquid diphthongs → syllabic liquids. Proto-Slavic liquid diphthong sequences (TorT, TolT, TerT, TelT) developed the metathesis like West and South Slavic: vron "raven" (< PS *vornъ), grod "castle" (< PS *gordъ), mleko "milk" (< PS *melko).

Vowel quantity and tone. Vowel quality is broadly unchanged from OCS. Length distinctions and tonal contrasts survive in conservative registers but are no longer phonemically contrastive in the standard language and are not indicated in writing.

Prosody. Stress regularly falls onto the first long vowel, or onto the first vowel when no vowel is long. Endings are almost never stressed.

Grammar

Pannonian grammar is conservative by Slavic standards, preserving seven grammatical cases, dual number, and three grammatical genders from Old Church Slavonic. In the verbal system, the OCS aorist and imperfect are retained alongside the perfect, as in Bulgarian, and dual number is fully preserved in verb paradigms.

Word stems that end in cz, sz, zs, dj or any palatalized consonant are called "soft" stems, while the remainder are "hard". When endings begin with -o-, this vowel usually becomes -e- after a soft stem. This happens in many noun and adjective declensions, and also in some verbs.

Nouns are marked for case and number. There are 7 cases (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, vocative, locative, and instrumental) and 3 numbers (singular, dual, and plural). Plural is used instead of dual for nouns that represent things that usually come in pairs (such as body parts, socks, twins, and parents), except if one wants to stress that the noun relates to both parts and after words that signify a pair (such as oba "both", etc.) Pannonian nouns are divided into 3 genders (masculine, feminine, and neuter). Each gender has different declension patterns, for a total of 10 declension forms.

Verbs are conjugated for 3 persons and 3 numbers (singular, dual, and plural). There are 4 tenses (present, imperfect, aorist, and future), 3 moods (indicative, imperative, and conditional) and 2 voices (active and passive). Numerals between 20 and 100 are systemically 20-based, which is highly unusual for Slavic languages (appears in the Resian dialect of Slovene only) and is hypothetically influenced by pre-Slavic Pannonian Romanized Celtic substratum.

Lexicon

When early Slavic settlers from Great Moravia established themselves in Cisdanubian Pannonia, they encountered a remnant Proto-Romance-speaking population and absorbed a layer of cultural vocabulary from them — for instance, kostur "city" (< Latin castrum), polac "palace" (< Latin palatium), where a > o transition indicates early borrowing. During the medieval and early modern periods the language accumulated several hundred loanwords from Hungarian, Latin, and German.

The 19th-century Slavic Revival movement pushed back against this foreign stratum, demanding a return to a purely Slavic vocabulary. The nationalist government of the First Republic carried out a sweeping "lexical purification" reform (slovny poczist), replacing most established loanwords with newly coined Slavic equivalents — for instance, Ministeria o finance "Ministry of Finance" was replaced by Naprava o skarbe.

During the Fascist dictatorship of Field Marshal Drogan Glovacz, then King Drogan I (1931–1940), and the National-Communist dictatorship of Generalissimo Severyn Gowbka (1945–1990), usage of most non-Slavic words was considered "unpatriotic" and in certain contexts illegal. Post-Communist democratization brought a more relaxed approach, but Slavic purism remains the enforced policy of the linguistic authorities in both de facto successor states.

Onomastic

The oldest stratum of Pannonian personal names consists of dithematic Slavic compound names built from elements such as blag- "kind," vlod- "rule," mir- "peace," drog- "dear," and slav- "glory." Associated with 19th-century Slavic Revival activists and the First Republic, these are now considered archaic. Abbreviated forms — Miro, Ljubo, Vojko and others — became independent names in their own right and remain common across generations.

Christian baptismal names entered through Hungarian mediation and bear the traces of that phonological filter: Janosz "John," Sandor "Alexander," Djordji "George," Zsuzsana "Susan." This Hungaricized stratum was dominant before 1900; after 1918 the choice of Hungaricized names carried madjaron connotations, they were legally banned between 1945 and 1990, and have partially revived since. The currently dominant category consists of Latinized or Germanized equivalents of the same Christian names — Jan, Stefan, Georg, Elena — which became standard after 1918.

Hereditary surnames emerged gradually, first among the nobility as toponymic references marked by the Hungarian -y ending, e.g. Bregovy "from Bregovo." Another mark of Hungarian domination was the placement of surname before given name — an order that persisted in official use until the country's independence. Until 1787, when Joseph II made surnames mandatory for all Austrian subjects, the majority of peasants still lacked them. Most registered by patronymic — father's given name plus -ov/-ova or -ev/-eva — or by village name plus -ski/-ska. These Josephine-stratum surnames remain the most common in the language today.

Russian influence, prominent within the pan-Slavic movement, introduced the patronymic — a middle name derived from the father's given name and marked by the suffixes -ov/-ova (or -ev/-eva after a palatal consonant). It was adopted into the official naming system under the First Republic and remains formally required, though dropped in ordinary speech.

The country's ethnic minorities have their specific naming patterns. So-called "Turks" (in fact, Muslim Slavs) use given names from Islamic stock and surnames almost exclusively derived from patronymics, e.g. Mehmet Umarov. Serbs, Slovenes and Croats retain their characteristic -icz surnames.

The official name order — given name, patronymic, surname — reflects the 1920s reform. In everyday speech the patronymic is omitted, and hypocoristics and nicknames are widespread.

Titles and addresses

Pre-modern Pannonian possessed no standardized courtesy title. The 19th-century Slavic Revival movement introduced pan and pani on the model of Polish and Czech practice, treating them as markers of a shared Slavic cultural identity in deliberate contrast with the Hungarian úr and asszony that then dominated official usage under the Banate. The First Republic formally adopted the pair as the state's standard forms of address in 1918.

The Communist authorities abolished pan/pani after 1945 as titles of bourgeois provenance and mandated the genderless tovarics — modelled on the Russian товарищ — as the universal form of address in official, educational, and public life. The compulsion was uneven in practice; within families and informal settings the old forms persisted quietly. After democratisation in 1990, pan/pani reasserted itself in public usage within months; tovarics is now encountered almost exclusively in nostalgic or ironic registers.

The current system uses pan (masculine singular nominative; vocative pane) and pani (feminine singular, invariable across nominative and vocative, and used regardless of age or marital status). The plural forms are pany for masculine and pane for mixed or exclusively feminine groups. Both titles precede the surname in formal address and may stand alone — pane or pani — as equivalents of English sir and ma'am.

In military usage, kamrad/kamradka preceding a rank designation is the established title, e.g. kamrad sutnik "Comrade Captain". This word is not Communist by origin, but traces its roots to Austro-Hungarian military tradition and survived all Slavic purification attempts.

Pronoun address follows the T-V distinction familiar from Russian and BCMS: the informal second-person singular ty governs address to family members, close friends, and peers in informal contexts, while the polite vy — morphologically a second-person plural — is required for strangers, superiors, and any setting demanding social distance. Uninvited use of ty towards a stranger or official is considered rude; an unsolicited offer of ty from a superior signals genuine personal warmth.
