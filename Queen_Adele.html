Queen Adele

Adelj Glovacz (born Etelka Leonardova Baumgarten; 12 April 1903 – 3 September 1952) was the consort of King Drogan I of Upper Pannonia and, from 1936, Queen consort and designated heir to the throne. Born into a theatrical family of mixed Deglian, Zgurian, Hungarian, and Jewish ancestry in Vronovica, she pursued a film career in Weimar Germany under the stage name Adele Verger before returning to Upper Pannonia in 1930. Her marriage in 1933 to General Drogan Glovacz provoked a social scandal within Pannonian military circles. She became a widely admired public figure through philanthropic work and private advocacy for political prisoners, and as Queen from 1936 was the humane face of an otherwise brutal regime.

Following the Hungarian invasion of 1940 and King Drogan's assassination, she fled to London, established a government-in-exile, and led Pannonian monarchist resistance throughout the war. Abandoned by her British allies at Yalta, she remained the focal point of anti-Communist Pannonian emigration until her death in 1952, almost certainly poisoned by agents of the NOON secret police.

Early Life (1903–1924)

Etelka Baumgarten was born on 12 April 1903 in Vronovica. Her father, Leonard Baumgarten, managed the Vronovica Municipal Theatre; her mother, Veselina (née Meljnik), was a stage actress of Zgurian-Deglian descent. Even by the standards of multi-ethnic Pannonian society the family's origins were mixed: Veselina's background was Slavic, Leonard's family Hungarian-assimilated Jews resident in Vronovica for three generations.

Of Vronovica's three theatres, the conservative Royal performed in German and Hungarian only, the nationalist Slavic Theatre in Pannonian only, and the Municipal was apolitical and offered all three. Etelka grew up backstage, performed minor roles by ten, and learned Hungarian and German simultaneously. Her formal education was intermittent; she attended the Vronovica Girls' Gymnasium for three years before withdrawing. She did not associate herself with any of the competing ethnic identities. Asked in a 1931 interview to describe her background: "Pannonian. Everything else is my parents' business."

Silent Film Career (1924–1930)

Arrival in Germany

In early 1924, aged twenty, Etelka Baumgarten travelled to Berlin under the stage name Adele Verger — chosen to sound fashionable while remaining pronounceable across Central European markets. She found initial employment through a Pannonian theatrical contact and was taken on by UFA (Universum Film AG) as a contract extra within months.

Film Roles

Her early appearances were uncredited. Production records list her as an extra in Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927) and briefly on F.W. Murnau's Tartüff (1925), both bringing her close to the major figures of German cinema.

Her first credited role was in the now-lost Das Lied der Fremde (The Song of the Stranger, 1927, dir. Hanns Schwarz), a foreign dancer with four minutes of screen time. The film earned her a notice in the Film-Kurier: "Fräulein Verger, of unknown East European origin, possesses a melancholy photogenic quality that the camera finds naturally."

Her most substantial credit was a supporting role in Die Verbannten (The Exiles, 1928, dir. Richard Oswald), a melodrama set among Central European émigrés in postwar Vienna; two critics singled out her performance as the most natural element.

Personal Life in Berlin

During her Berlin years, Adele frequented the Romanisches Café and UFA social circles. She was romantically linked with cinematographer Karl Hoffmann — who had shot Der Golem (1920) — for approximately two years; the relationship ended without apparent acrimony. She was briefly noted in Die Dame in 1927 in connection with the actor Anton Walbrook, though the nature of the association was never elaborated.

More consequential was her friendship with director G.W. Pabst, whom she met during Die Büchse der Pandora (Pandora's Box, 1929), working as a set assistant after her contracted role was cut. Pabst reportedly considered her for Tagebuch einer Verlorenen (1929) but did not cast her; no correspondence survives.

Return to Upper Pannonia (1930–1933)

The transition to sound ended her German career; her Slavic accent, undetectable on a silent screen, made her unusable in dialogue pictures. "The cinema is finished for me here," she wrote to her mother.

Adele returned to Vronovica in mid-1930, to a city where the Great Depression was accelerating social tensions and Glovacz's National Empowerment Movement was gaining strength. Within weeks she was cast in Zarja nad Dravoj (Dawn Over the Drava, 1930), the first Upper Pannonian sound film — a period drama in which she played a noblewoman heroically resisting the Turkish invaders. The Vronovica press dubbed her the "Pannonian Greta Garbo", a comparison she found flattering and slightly absurd. Two further Pannonian films followed in 1931 and 1932, making her the country's foremost screen actress.

Marriage to Drogan Glovacz (1933)

She met General Drogan Glovacz in autumn 1931, soon after his September 1 seizure of power. For Adele, it was probably not a matter of choice: few women would dare to say "no" to the Supreme Commander. For Glovacz himself, this relationship turned into something more serious than his many previous affairs with easily available women.

In early 1933, Glovacz announced their marriage. Officers of the old Austro-Hungarian tradition filed resignation petitions; some took early retirement. Glovacz accepted these with equanimity. Adele handled the transition with a composure that disarmed many critics. She legally changed her given name from the Hungaricized Etelka to Adelj (Zgurian; Deglian: Adeĺ; Glagolitic: ⰀⰄⰅⰎⰠ), signalling Slavic self-identification.

As First Lady (1933–1934)

In the years between her marriage and King Drogan's formal enthronement, Adelj built the popular reputation that would sustain her for the rest of her life — working within the constraints of a brutal dictatorship without challenging its structures.

Her principal public activities were charitable. She founded the Pannonian Women's Relief League in 1934, organizing food distribution in Depression-affected districts, funding orphanages, and establishing literacy classes for rural women. Less publicly, she interceded with her husband for political prisoners — never seeking the release of prominent figures but documented to have secured commutation of at least eleven death sentences. Word circulated in the way such things do in closed societies: widely known, officially unacknowledged, and more powerful for it.

Queen of Upper Pannonia (1936–1940)

In 1934, Glovacz was enthroned as Krol Drogan I; in 1936, he enthroned Adelj as queen (Krolynja Adelj) and, citing their childless marriage, designated her his successor. The designation generated less controversy than the marriage had three years earlier; Adele had established a genuine popular following and the military offered no public resistance.

As Queen, Adele became the regime's principal representative at cultural events and patronized Pannonian visual arts and music. At the royal residence she maintained a salon that attracted the intellectual class regardless of political alignment — a rare space of relative freedom. The Relief League expanded its programmes across the country by 1938.

Flight to England and Government in Exile (1940–1945)

The Invasion and Escape

In September 1940, the Hungarian advance combined with an internal coup brought the kingdom to an end. King Drogan was assassinated; Queen Adele, reportedly warned hours in advance, escaped Vronovica by car assisted by her personal secretary Sir Randall Massey — an Englishman in royal service since 1938, widely believed to be a British intelligence officer. She was transported through neutral Yugoslavia to the Adriatic, where a British naval vessel collected her.

She arrived in London in October 1940. The British government's initial interest was strategic: a government-in-exile for an occupied Central European state represented useful diplomatic capital. She declared herself Queen of Upper Pannonia in Exile, established a court at a rented Kensington residence, and appointed an émigré government of former parliamentarians, diplomats, and military figures.

In 1941, Queen Adele gave birth to a daughter, Ljudmila, described as the posthumous daughter of King Drogan. The official record dates the birth to June 10, nine months after the King's death; suspicious details suggest the actual delivery may have occurred two months later. Speculation regarding Randall Massey as the biological father was never confirmed or addressed by either party.

BBC Broadcasts and the Monarchist Resistance

Working with the BBC's European Service, Queen Adele delivered regular broadcasts in Pannonian to the occupied country. Listeners, who faced severe penalties for tuning to foreign broadcasts, gathered around concealed radios throughout the occupation years. Monarchist guerrilla units — never militarily significant but symbolically important — were sustained in part by knowledge that the queen remained active.

She also arranged the evacuation of Pannonian nationals, documented occupation atrocities for post-war tribunals, and maintained Upper Pannonia's formal allied status — which carried practical consequences for the treatment of Pannonian prisoners of war.

Yalta and Abandonment

By the time of the Yalta Conference, Soviet forces had already occupied Upper Pannonia and the Gowbka government was a functioning fact on the ground. Stalin demanded the British cease support for Queen Adele, calling the monarchist movement a "reactionary fascist formation" — a characterization the British were in no position to contest.

Churchill briefly argued for some accommodation, then conceded. In late 1945, the United Kingdom officially recognized the Gowbka government; Adele's government lost its formal standing, British funding, and diplomatic access. The Kensington court continued as a private organization. Adele kept her title, but was now essentially a private person without a state.

Final Years and Death (1945–1952)

Loss of official recognition transformed rather than ended her political activity. The court became the hub of the anti-Communist Pannonian diaspora, maintaining contact with networks inside the country and supporting refugees. She corresponded with Western politicians and journalists, engaged exile committees of other Eastern European countries, and kept the Pannonian question in public view through press interviews and émigré addresses.

Her circumstances were strained. With British subsidies gone, the court depended on émigré donations and occasional American Cold War support. Randall Massey — whose official status the British government never acknowledged — remained her constant companion, understood in émigré circles and at NOON's foreign directorate alike as both her partner and a continuing British intelligence asset.

NOON had maintained a file on her activities from the late 1940s and made at least two documented attempts to insert agents into her social circle. Whether these produced actionable intelligence or constituted preliminary work for what followed is unknown.

On 3 September 1952, Queen Adele died at her Kensington residence following a brief illness presenting as acute gastroenteritis. She was forty-nine. British forensic examination concluded natural death; retrospective analysis in the 1990s, following partial opening of NOON archives, found evidence of a slow-acting thallium compound consistent with NOON's documented methods. No perpetrator was ever identified or prosecuted.

Her supporters proclaimed the eleven-year-old Princess Ljudmila Queen in exile. The child was transferred to Pannonian émigré organizations in the United States. She would return to Upper Pannonia, briefly and tragically, forty-two years later.

Legacy

Queen Adele occupies a singular position in Pannonian historical memory. In nationalist and monarchist tradition she is venerated as the embodiment of dignified resistance — a woman who turned personal misfortune into political purpose and maintained Pannonian sovereignty at considerable personal cost. Streets, a hospital, and a secondary school were named in her honour in the post-Communist period.

From a leftist or critical perspective she is more ambiguous: the consort of a fascist dictator whose humanitarian work occurred within, and to some extent legitimized, a repressive system. This critique was developed in official Gowbka-era historiography and retains some currency despite that historiography being broadly discredited.

The question of Princess Ljudmila's parentage remains unresolved. Randall Massey died in London in 1991 without public comment. It persists as a topic of interest in Pannonian popular history and is genuinely contested within the monarchist tradition, where it carries dynastic implications.
