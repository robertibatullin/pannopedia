Queen Adele of Upper Pannonia

Adelj Glovacz (born Etelka Leonardova Baumgarten; 12 April 1903 – 3 September 1952) was the consort of King Drogan I of Upper Pannonia and, from 1936, Queen consort and designated heir to the Pannonian throne. Born into a theatrical family of mixed Deglian, Zgurian, Hungarian, and Jewish ancestry in Vronovica, she pursued a film career in Weimar Germany under the stage name Adele Verger before returning to Upper Pannonia in 1930. Her marriage in 1933 to dictator Field Marshal Drogan Glovacz provoked a social scandal within Pannonian military circles. She went on to become a widely admired public figure through philanthropic work, quiet personal dignity, and private advocacy for political prisoners. Enthroned as Queen in 1936, she became the humane face of an otherwise brutal regime.

Following the Hungarian invasion of 1940 and the assassination of King Drogan, Adele fled to London, where she established a government-in-exile and led Pannonian monarchist resistance throughout the war. After being abandoned by her British allies at the Yalta Conference, she remained the focal point of anti-Communist Pannonian emigration until her death. She was poisoned in London in 1952, almost certainly by agents of the Communist regime's secret police, NOON.

Early Life (1903–1924)

Etelka Baumgarten was born on 12 April 1903 in Vronovica, the administrative capital of the semi-autonomous Upper Pannonian Banate. Her father, Leonard Baumgarten, managed the Vronovica Municipal Theatre. Her mother, Veselina (née Meljnik), was a stage actress of Zgurian-Deglian descent who had worked across the Austro-Hungarian theatrical circuit. Even by the standards of multi-ethnic Pannonian society, the family's origins were mixed: Veselina's background was Slavic; Leonard's family were Hungarian-assimilated Jews who had lived in Vronovica for three generations.

Of the three Vronovica theatres at the time, the Municipal Theatre was the most cosmopolitan. The conservative Royal Theatre performed in German and Hungarian only; the nationalist Slavic Theatre in Pannonian only; the Municipal Theatre was apolitical and offered all three. Etelka grew up backstage. She performed minor walk-on roles by the age of ten and learned Hungarian and German simultaneously. Her formal education was intermittent — she attended the Vronovica Girls' Gymnasium for three years before withdrawing. She did not associate herself with any of the country's competing ethnic identities. Asked in a 1931 interview to describe her background, she replied: "Pannonian. Everything else is my parents' business."

Silent Film Career (1924–1930)

Arrival in Germany

In early 1924, aged twenty, Etelka Baumgarten travelled to Berlin under the stage name Adele Verger, chosen to sound fashionable while remaining pronounceable across Central European markets. Berlin's film industry in the mid-1920s was at the peak of its Expressionist period and drew aspiring performers from across Europe. Adele found initial employment through a Pannonian theatrical contact and was taken on by UFA (Universum Film AG) as a contract extra within months of her arrival.

Film Roles

Her early appearances were uncredited: crowd scenes and background work in several major productions of the period. Production records list her as an extra in the underground worker-city sequences of Fritz Lang's Metropolis (1927), employed for three weeks during shooting. She also worked briefly on F.W. Murnau's Tartüff (1925) in a similar capacity. Neither role was remarkable, but both brought her close to the major figures of German cinema.

Her first credited role came in 1927 in a now-lost romantic melodrama, Das Lied der Fremde (The Song of the Stranger, dir. Hanns Schwarz), in which she played a mysterious foreign dancer with four minutes of screen time and a single intertitle card. The film received modest distribution and earned her a small notice in the Film-Kurier: "Fräulein Verger, of unknown East European origin, possesses a melancholy photogenic quality that the camera finds naturally."

Her most substantial credit was a supporting role as a displaced Balkan noblewoman in Die Verbannten (The Exiles, 1928, dir. Richard Oswald), a melodrama set among Central European émigrés in postwar Vienna. The film received respectful if unenthusiastic reviews; two critics singled out Adele's performance as the production's most natural element.

Personal Life in Berlin

During her Berlin years, Adele frequented the Romanisches Café and the social circles around UFA. She was romantically linked with Austrian cinematographer Karl Hoffmann — who had shot Der Golem (1920) — for approximately two years. The relationship ended without apparent acrimony; both parties declined to discuss it. She was also briefly noted in the gossip columns of Die Dame magazine in 1927 in connection with the actor Anton Walbrook, though the nature of the association was never elaborated.

More consequential, and more discreet, was her friendship with director G.W. Pabst. She met him during the production of Die Büchse der Pandora (Pandora's Box, 1929), where she worked as a set assistant after her contracted supporting role was cut in editing. Pabst reportedly considered her for a role in Tagebuch einer Verlorenen (Diary of a Lost Girl, also 1929) but did not cast her. The nature of their connection was a subject of retrospective speculation; no correspondence survives.

Return to Upper Pannonia (1930–1933)

The transition to sound film ended Adele Verger's German career. Her Slavic accent — undetectable on a silent screen — made her commercially unusable in dialogue pictures. She departed Berlin in the spring of 1930. "The cinema is finished for me here," she wrote in a letter to her mother recovered after the war.

Adele returned to Vronovica in mid-1930. The Great Depression was accelerating social tensions. Field Marshal Glovacz's National Empowerment Movement (Dviga Drezsavnoj Zmoczi), closely modelled on Italian Fascism, was gaining strength. The First Republic's brief democratic experiment was visibly failing. Within weeks of her return, she was cast in the leading role of Zarja nad Dravoj (Dawn Over the Drava, 1930), the first Upper Pannonian sound film — a technically primitive but locally celebrated period drama in which she played a Pannonian noblewoman inspiring a revolt against Turkish invaders. The Vronovica press dubbed her the "Pannonian Greta Garbo", a comparison she found flattering and slightly absurd. She made two further Pannonian films in 1931 and 1932, becoming the country's foremost screen actress and a recognized figure in Vronovica high society.

Marriage to Drogan Glovacz (1933)

In 1932 she became the mistress of Field Marshal Drogan Glovacz, who had seized power the previous year. Glovacz was a widower and a committed social conservative. He was well aware that the military-aristocratic establishment on whose loyalty his regime depended would consider a liaison with an actress scandalous.

In early 1933, Glovacz announced their marriage. The reaction within Pannonian military circles was severe. Officers of the old Austro-Hungarian tradition, for whom marriage to a performer was professionally dishonourable, filed resignation petitions; some took early retirement. Glovacz accepted these with equanimity. Adele handled the transition from mistress to dictator's wife with a composure that disarmed many of her critics. She legally changed her given name from the Hungarized Etelka to Adelj (Zgurian spelling; Deglian: Adeĺ, Glagolitic: ⰀⰄⰅⰎⰠ), signalling a Slavic self-identification and association with the country as a whole rather than with any specific ethnicity.

As First Lady (1933–1934)

The years between her marriage and King Drogan's formal enthronement were when Adelj built the popular reputation that would sustain her for the rest of her life. She worked within the constraints of a brutal dictatorship, establishing herself as its public face without challenging its structures.

Her principal public activities were charitable. She founded the Pannonian Women's Relief League in 1934, which organised food distribution in Depression-affected districts, funded orphanages, and established a network of literacy classes for rural women. Less publicly, she interceded with her husband on behalf of political prisoners. She did not challenge the regime's core operations and apparently never sought the release of prominent political figures. But she is documented to have secured commutations of death sentences in at least eleven individual cases and improved conditions for others. Word of this practice circulated in the way such things do in closed societies: widely known, officially unacknowledged, and more powerful for it.

Queen of Upper Pannonia (1936–1940)

In 1934, Drogan Glovacz was formally enthroned as king (Krol Drogan I). In 1936, he similarly enthroned Adelj as queen (Krolynja Adelj, officially spelled "Adele" in foreign-language publications). Citing the couple's childless marriage after several years together, he designated her as successor to the throne. The designation generated less controversy than the marriage had three years earlier. Adele had by this point established a genuine popular following, and the military establishment, whatever its private reservations, offered no public resistance.

As Queen, her public role expanded. Adele became the regime's principal representative at cultural and ceremonial events and patronized Pannonian visual arts and music. At the royal residence she maintained a salon that attracted the country's intellectual class regardless of political alignment — a rare space of relative freedom in an increasingly closed society. Her charitable work expanded in scale under the auspices of the Relief League, which by 1938 operated programmes across the country.

Flight to England and Government in Exile (1940–1945)

The Invasion and Escape

In April 1940, a Hungarian military advance combined with an internal coup by Hungarian-aligned officers brought the kingdom to an abrupt end. King Drogan was assassinated by his guardsmen. Queen Adele was reportedly warned hours in advance. She escaped Vronovica by car, assisted by her personal secretary, Sir Randall Massey — an Englishman who had entered royal service in 1938 and was later widely believed to be a British intelligence officer. Adele was transported through Yugoslavia to the Adriatic coast, where a British naval vessel collected her.

She arrived in London in May 1940, during the most precarious weeks of British resistance. The British government's initial interest was strategic: a potential allied government for an occupied Central European state represented useful diplomatic capital. She declared herself Queen of Upper Pannonia in Exile, established a formal court at a rented residence in Kensington, and appointed an émigré government comprising former parliamentarians, diplomats, and senior military figures who had escaped the invasion or been abroad at the time.

In 1941, Queen Adele gave birth to a daughter, Ljudmila, described as the posthumous daughter of King Drogan. The official record dates the birth January 10, less than nine months after the King's death. Some suspicious details indicate potential forgery, suggesting the actual delivery may have occurred two months later. Speculation regarding Randall Massey as a possible biological father — who remained at the queen's side throughout her London years and into the postwar period — was never confirmed or addressed by any party.

BBC Broadcasts and the Monarchist Resistance

Working with the BBC's European Service, Queen Adele delivered regular broadcasts in Pannonian to the occupied country. Her well-known voice proved an effective instrument. Listeners, who faced severe penalties for tuning to foreign broadcasts, nonetheless gathered around concealed radios to hear her throughout the occupation years. Within Upper Pannonia, monarchist guerrilla units — never militarily significant but symbolically important — were sustained in part by knowledge that the queen remained present and active.

She also worked through diplomatic channels to arrange the evacuation of Pannonian nationals, to document atrocities committed during the Hungarian occupation for post-war tribunals, and to maintain Upper Pannonia's formal status as an allied nation, which carried practical consequences for the treatment of Pannonian prisoners of war.

Yalta and Abandonment

By the time of the Yalta Conference in February 1945, Soviet forces had already occupied Upper Pannonia. The Soviet-backed government under Severyn Gowbka was a functioning fact on the ground. Stalin demanded that the British cease all support for Queen Adele's government in exile, calling the monarchist movement a "reactionary fascist formation." The British were in no position to contest the characterization given the military realities of Eastern Europe.

Churchill, recorded in the conference minutes as having briefly argued for some accommodation for the queen, ultimately conceded. In late 1945, the United Kingdom officially recognized the Gowbka government. Adele's émigré government lost its formal standing, its British funding, and its diplomatic access. The Kensington court continued as a private organization. Adele kept her title, but her political position was now essentially that of a private person without a state.

Final Years and Death (1945–1952)

The loss of official recognition transformed rather than ended Adele's political activity. The monarchist court became the hub of the anti-Communist Pannonian diaspora in Western Europe, maintaining contact with networks inside the country and supporting refugees fleeing the new regime. She corresponded with Western politicians and journalists, maintained contact with the exile committees of other Eastern European countries, and kept the Pannonian question in public view through press interviews and addresses at émigré gatherings.

Her personal circumstances were strained. With British subsidies gone, the court's finances depended on donations from the Pannonian émigré community and occasional support from American Cold War organizations. Randall Massey, whose official status the British government never formally acknowledged, remained her constant companion. Within émigré circles and at NOON's foreign operations directorate alike, he was widely understood to be both her partner and a continuing British intelligence asset.

NOON had maintained a file on her activities from the late 1940s and made at least two documented attempts to insert agents into her social circle. Whether these produced actionable intelligence or constituted preliminary work for what followed is unknown.

On 3 September 1952, Queen Adele died at her Kensington residence following a brief illness presenting as acute gastroenteritis. She was forty-nine. British forensic examination at the time concluded natural death. Retrospective analysis in the 1990s, following the partial opening of NOON archives, found evidence suggesting a slow-acting thallium compound consistent with NOON's documented methods. No perpetrator was ever formally identified or prosecuted.

Her supporters immediately proclaimed the eleven-year-old Princess Ljudmila as Queen in exile. The child was transferred to the custody of Pannonian émigré organizations in the United States for her safety. She would return to Upper Pannonia, briefly and tragically, forty-two years later.

Legacy

Queen Adele occupies a singular position in Pannonian historical memory. Within the nationalist and monarchist traditions she is venerated as the embodiment of dignified resistance. She is seen as a woman who turned personal misfortune into political purpose and, at considerable personal cost, maintained the continuity of Pannonian sovereignty through occupation and exile. Streets, a hospital, and a secondary school in the post-Communist period were named in her honour.

From a leftist or critical perspective, she is a more ambiguous figure: the consort of a fascist dictator whose humanitarian work occurred within, and to some extent legitimized, a repressive system. This critique, developed systematically in official Gowbka-era historiography, retains some currency despite that historiography being broadly discredited.

The question of Princess Ljudmila's parentage remains unresolved. Randall Massey died in London in 1991 without public comment on the matter. The question persists as a topic of interest in Pannonian popular history and is genuinely contested within the monarchist tradition, where it carries dynastic implications.
