Enjo Zslutovodyj

Enjo Zslutovodyj (Zgurian; Deglian: Enö Žlutovody; Glagolitic: ⰅⰐⰦ ⰆⰎⰖⰕⰑⰂⰑⰄⰬ; Latin: Eugenius Aquaflavus; 1823–1851) was a poet, scholar, and publisher, widely considered the founding father of the Pannonian Slavic Revival.

Early life and education

Zslutovodyj was born in Zsluty Vody (lit. "yellow waters"), a settlement at sulphuric hot springs. His family belonged to the lower gentry; his ancestors had served as Grenzer commanders on the Habsburg military frontier. The settlement was home to both Zgurian and Deglian communities, and Zslutovodyj never stated which he considered himself. The question has never been resolved and remains a hot topic in Zguro-Deglian discussions.

He enrolled at the Vronovica Academy of Sciences — later the Old University — where he studied classical languages and Catholic theology. As a student he took part in a circle debating whether Slavic vernaculars or Latin were the proper medium for education and literature. He argued consistently for Slavic. This drew him toward the Glagolitic tradition; he visited the monastery of Klykov twice and spent several months working in its scriptoria. From that research he compiled An Inventory of the Glagolitic Manuscripts of Klykov Monastery1, published in 1845. The volume covered approximately half the collection. A projected second part was never completed.

Service with Bregovi

After graduating in 1846, Zslutovodyj was employed as a home teacher by Lajosz Bregovi, a wealthy nobleman who would later become the first Ban of Upper Pannonia. In Bregovi's home, Zslutovodyj fell in love with the nobleman's young daughter Ilona. She did not return his feelings, and he never pressed the matter; he expressed himself in verse instead. The poems he addressed to Ilona over the following years, published posthumously, were the first works of secular literature in the Pannonian language. In 1847, Ilona was given in an arranged marriage to a Hungarian magnate of the Esterházy family. Zslutovodyj entered a prolonged depression, and hostility to Hungarian cultural and political dominance became a defining element of his subsequent work.

Publications and the Enlightenment Society

Bregovi continued his patronage through this period and used it to fund Zslutovodyj's scholarly projects. In 1847 Zslutovodyj published A Grammar of the Pannonian Language2, the first systematic description of the language. A Dictionary of the Pannonian Language3 followed in 1848. Both were printed in Glagolitic script with parallel Latin translation and circulated mainly among ecclesiastical and scholarly readers.

Also in 1847, an anonymous treatise was published, proving decisively that the so-called Decretum Ioannis — a ninth-century papal document granting the Klykov monastery perpetual liturgical autonomy — was in fact an eleventh-century forgery. Zslutovodyj is widely believed to be the author, having chosen to conceal his authorship to avoid discrediting Klykov and its Glagolitic tradition4.

In 1848, Zslutovodyj launched The Slavic Dawn (Slavjanska Zarja), a monthly magazine and the first periodical in the Pannonian language, and co-founded the Enlightenment Society (Zadruga prosvjeta) with fellow Slavophile scholars Gostyvoj Bluxa, Dometius Bobov, and others. They launched a Sunday school where they taught Glagolitic literacy for free; classes never exceeded five students.

The Slavic Dawn published original verse, collected folklore from Pannonian-speaking villages, and ran philological articles on the Glagolitic tradition. Zslutovodyj used it to stake out a position on the revolutions of 1848. He supported cultural and administrative autonomy for Slavic peoples within the Habsburg framework, and opposed Hungarian independence as hostile to Slavic interests. This put him at odds with Bluxa who backed the Hungarian cause, expecting it to carry Slavic liberties along with it.

Zslutovodyj was equally skeptical of the Illyrian movement and its Zagreb proponents, who advocated a unified South Slavic cultural identity. In a series of articles in late 1848, Zslutovodyj argued that Pannonians were a distinct Slavic people, more closely related to Czechs and Slovaks than to the southern Slavic nations the Illyrian project claimed to unify. He did not deny common Slavic origins, but contended that adopting a literary standard built on a southern dialect foreign to Pannonian speakers would merely substitute one cultural imposition for another. This put him at odds with Bobov who was at this time an Illyrism proponent. Despite political disagreements, Zslutovodyj remained a friend and a figure of authority for Bobov and Bluxa alike.

Suppression and death

In mid-1849, following the Habsburg suppression of the Hungarian revolution, the imperial authorities extended their crackdown to vernacular publishing and cultural associations across the empire. Zadruga prosvjeta was dissolved; the Sunday language school closed; the periodical banned. Zslutovodyj made no public response. He stopped writing and withdrew from public life.

The bans were partially lifted in 1850, after Bregovi's appointment as Ban gave him sufficient influence to negotiate exceptions. Zslutovodyj was by then already ill with tuberculosis. He died in the spring of 1851, aged twenty-eight. The Enlightenment Society was refounded by Bluxa and Bobov the following year.

Zslutovodyj's argument that Pannonians formed a distinct Slavic people — neither culturally Hungarian nor part of the South Slavic world — became the foundation of Pannonian national identity and held for over a century. The Zguro-Deglian civil war of the 1990s dissolved it: both communities ceased to identify primarily as Pannonian, and the common identity Zslutovodyj had helped define fractured along the ethnic lines. Nevertheless he remains a highly respected figure for Deglians and Zgurians alike. His name is given to the principal avenue of Vronovica's Second District, to the square at its head, and multiple other locations across Former Upper Pannonia.
