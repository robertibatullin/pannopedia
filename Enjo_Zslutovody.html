Enjo Zslutovody

Enjo Zslutovody (Zgurian; Deglian: Enö Žlutovody; Glagolitic: ⰅⰐⰦ ⰆⰎⰖⰕⰑⰂⰑⰄⰬ; 1823–1851) was a poet, philologist, and publisher, widely considered the founding father of the Pannonian Slavic Revival.

Early life and education

Zslutovody was born in Zsluty Vody (lit. "yellow waters"), a settlement of sulphuric hot springs. His family belonged to the lower gentry; his ancestors had served as Grenzer commanders on the Habsburg military frontier. The settlement was home to both Zgurian and Deglian communities, and Zslutovody never stated which he considered himself. The question has never been resolved and remains a hot topic in Zguro-Deglian discussions.

He enrolled at the Vronovica Academy of Sciences — later the Old University — where he studied classical languages and Catholic theology. As a student he took part in a circle debating whether Slavic vernaculars or Latin were the proper medium for education and literature. He argued consistently for Slavic. This drew him toward the Glagolitic tradition; he visited the monastery of Klykov twice and spent several months working in its scriptoria. From that research he compiled A Catalogue of the Glagolitic Manuscripts of Klykov Monastery1, published in 1845. The volume covered approximately half the collection. A projected second part was never completed.

Service with Bregovy

After graduating in 1846, Zslutovody was employed as a home teacher by Lajosz Bregovy, a wealthy nobleman who would later become the first Ban of Upper Pannonia. In Bregovy's home, Zslutovody fell in love with the nobleman's young daughter Ilona. She did not return his feelings, and he never pressed the matter; he expressed himself in verse instead. The poems he addressed to Ilona over the following years, published posthumously, were the first works of secular literature in the Pannonian language. In 1847, Ilona was given in an arranged marriage to a Hungarian magnate of the Esterházy family. Zslutovody entered a prolonged depression, and hostility to Hungarian cultural and political dominance became a defining element of his subsequent work.

Publications and the Enlightenment Society

Bregovy continued his patronage through this period and used it to fund Zslutovody's scholarly projects. In 1847 Zslutovody published A Grammar of the Pannonian Language2, the first systematic description of the language. A Dictionary of the Pannonian Language3 followed in 1848. Both were printed in Glagolitic script with parallel Latin translation and circulated mainly among ecclesiastical and scholarly readers.

Also in 1848, Zslutovody launched The Slavic Dawn (Slavjanska Zarja), a monthly magazine and the first periodical in the Pannonian language, and co-founded the Enlightenment Society (Zadruga prosvjeta) with fellow Slavophile scholars Gostyvoj Bluxa, Dometius Bobov, and others. In early 1849 the monthly converted to a weekly under the same name.

Suppression and death

In mid-1849, following the Habsburg suppression of the Hungarian revolution, the imperial authorities extended their crackdown to vernacular publishing and cultural associations across the empire. Zadruga Prosvjeta was dissolved; both publications were banned. Zslutovody made no public response. He stopped writing and withdrew from public life.

The bans were partially lifted in 1850, after Bregovy's appointment as Ban gave him sufficient influence to negotiate exceptions. Zslutovody was by then already ill with tuberculosis. He died in the spring of 1851, aged twenty-eight.

The Enlightenment Society was refounded by Bluxa and Bobov the following year. Zslutovody's name was later given to the principal avenue of Vronovica's Second District, to the square at its head, and multiple other locations across Upper Pannonia.
