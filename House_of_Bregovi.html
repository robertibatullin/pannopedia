House of Bregovi

The House of Bregovi (Dom Bregovi) descends from Boleslav and Lukas, rival contenders for the title of Prince Bishop of Bregovo in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries; it was the most influential noble family in Former Upper Pannonia during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

After the treason of Maczasz the Renegade, both pretenders to the Hungarian crown — Ferdinand I of Habsburg and János Zápolya — abolished the title of Prince Bishop and declared it permanently extinct. The sons of Lukas and the younger sons of Boleslav fought against the Ottomans in Habsburg or Zápolya service. Their noble status was recognized, but no princely title. Habsburg King Maximilian confirmed that no descendant of the "sinful and cursed breed of Bregovo" would ever hold the title of Prince, Duke, Count, or any equivalent dignity.

Heads of the House since the late seventeenth century

1. Ferenc (1662–1737) served under Eugene of Savoy in the Great Turkish War, commanding a cavalry regiment at the Battle of Zenta in 1697. He later led the suppression of Rákóczi's rebels in the Pannonian theater and ended the Austro-Turkish War of 1716–1718 as a major general after taking part in the capture of Belgrade. For his service in the Great Turkish War Ferenc was granted Bregovo and numerous other properties in the recaptured territory in 1696, making him the richest landowner in Upper Pannonia. He held senior judicial office there for many years. A fanatical Catholic, he fiercely persecuted Muslims and Protestants and oversaw mass executions of alleged witches and werewolves.

2. Janosz (1692–1763) was commissioned as an ensign in the final campaigns of the War of the Spanish Succession and took part in the capture of Belgrade under his father in 1717. He led a regiment at the disastrous Battle of Grocka in 1739 during the Russo-Austrian-Turkish War. At Dettingen in 1743, during the War of the Austrian Succession, he commanded a brigade, finishing a five-war career as a major general. He converted the former Sancak Bey summer residence, the Yeşil Konak north of Vronovica, into a Baroque mansion called Grünhof. As a landlord, he reduced large numbers of his dependants to serfdom and raided lesser landowners' properties with impunity.

3. Karol (1720–1788) fought as a colonel during the Seven Years' War at Leuthen in 1757 and at Hochkirch in 1758, and served as a major general in the War of the Bavarian Succession. Through systematic over-exploitation of his serfs he accumulated great wealth and rebuilt Grünhof as a lavish Rococo complex.

4. Lipot (1752–1819) took part in the suppression of the Transylvanian peasant uprising of 1784, becoming notorious for the atrocities committed in that campaign. He commanded an infantry regiment at the siege of Belgrade in 1789 during the Austro-Turkish War, then led it into the Italian theater in 1796 during the War of the First Coalition, rising to major general before the armistice. Known as a liberal, a freethinker, and a libertine, he maintained a harem of young serf women despite the formal abolition of serfdom in 1785; in practice, debt bondage continued throughout the period.

5. Jozsef (1782–1849) served in the Napoleonic Wars as a hussar officer: captured at Austerlitz in 1805 and released on parole, he fought at Wagram in 1809 and at Leipzig in 1813, ending the wars as a major. In 1815, he petitioned for the restoration of the princely title, a request Emperor Franz rejected. A nonconformist thinker of a mystical cast, he was a Freemason and Rosicrucian who assembled a collection of magical and alchemical manuscripts, made the first scholarly exploration of the Klykov monastery library, and undertook the initial restoration of Bregovo castle.

6. Lajosz (1810–1868) participated in the suppression of the Kraków Uprising of 1846 and in the Italian campaigns of 1848–49, fighting at Custoza in July 1848 and at Novara in March 1849. Promoted to major general thereafter and appointed Ban of Upper Pannonia in 1850, he patronized the early Slavic Revival and petitioned again — without success — for the restoration of the princely title. In 1862 he was dismissed from the Banship for his suspected pro-Slavic and anti-Hungarian stance. He subsequently partly reconstructed Bregovo Castle in neo-Gothic style, making it the family's principal residence.

7. Antal (1837–1904) served in the Austro-Prussian War as a cavalry officer, surviving the rout at Königgrätz in 1866, before turning to travel and collection. His expeditions took him to New Guinea, Africa, and Mecca — the last undertaken disguised as a Muslim pilgrim, the city being forbidden to non-Muslims on pain of death. Desperate to acquire an Austrian title, he travelled to the Empire of Brazil in 1871 and legally purchased the title of Count of Pindamonhangaba. Franz Joseph responded by explicitly forbidding "Antal Bregovi, Count of Pindamonhangaba" from styling himself "Count Bregovi." Antal took this as a personal insult and became an increasingly active supporter of the Slavic movement, including its political wing.

8. Prince Ostrovit (1872–1946) commanded an infantry regiment on the Galician front in 1914–15 and on the Isonzo front until 1917, then served in the Pannonian National Army during the conflicts of 1918–19. During the First Republic he was a non-partisan politician who served as Minister of War in several 1920s cabinets. He endorsed the coup of Drogan Glovacz in 1931 but withdrew from politics thereafter. In 1934, soon after proclaiming the monarchy, King Drogan I granted the Bregovi family the long-desired princely title. Ostrovit survived the 1944 battle of Bregovo castle and died in emigration.

9. Prince Svjetovit (1899–1973), a sculptor, survived the 1944 battle of the castle and emigrated with the rest of the family. In 1946 he petitioned Gowbka for permission to return, abdicating his princely title and donating all family property to the state — property that had already been nationalized; the petition was granted. He returned to become a devoted Gowbka supporter and the most celebrated official sculptor of the People's Republic, serving as lifelong chairman of the PRUP Academy of Fine Arts. He authored Gowbka's equestrian monument and the entire sculptural program of the Alley of Heroes and Pannonian Women's Avenue (see Sixth District of Vronovica). He donated most of his royalties to Miljana Gowbka's orphanages.

10. Blazsen (1929–2007) was an artist whose work, unlike his father's, was avant-garde and nonconformist, at odds with the Socialist Realist canons of the People's Republic. He was never persecuted, but his dissident reputation made him popular and sought-after in the West; his works entered the collections of the Museum of Modern Art in New York and the Centre Pompidou in Paris. After 1990 he emigrated to Vienna, where he was awarded the Austrian Grand State Prize in 1998 and lived as a prosperous artist with no involvement in politics.

11. Michael (born 1960), living in Vienna, is the current head of the House of Bregovi. He serves as an external consultant to the UN Quadrilateral Commission on Former Upper Pannonia, a Vienna-based body that appoints the UN High Commissioner.
