Second District of Vronovica

The Second District (Vtory okrug) district of North Vronovica is widely regarded as the best part of the city. Administratively it belongs entirely to the Neutral Zone.

History overview

The district's central axis is Enjo Zslutovody avenue, informally known as Main Street. The name was coined by FUPFOR soldiers who could not pronounce the avenue's proper name; it has since spread into general Vronovica usage across all languages. As the principal segment of the Vronovica-Budapest road, the avenue served in the Habsburg era as the city's most prestigious address, known then as King Franz Joseph avenue (Ferenc József király út in Hungarian). The local nobility and the wealthiest commercial families lived in the adjacent quarters.

During the First Republic this district was the city's centre of pro-Hungarian sentiment. Roughly seventy percent of its residents voted for union with Hungary in the 1922 plebiscite. It was the stronghold of the madjarony faction and the conservative opposition to the Republic and then to the Droganist monarchy. Field Marshal Glovacz, whose base lay in the Third District, responded by downgrading the avenue's ceremonial primacy in favour of Slavjanska jezda.

The Communist government, arriving in 1945, treated the district's inhabitants as presumptive collaborators. Arrests, confiscations, and apartment reassignments replaced the original social elite with party functionaries, NOON officers, and military staff. This substitution of one privileged class for another proved durable. The district retains its elitist character, reinforced by its incorporation into the Neutral Zone.

Topography

Principal buildings and landmarks, moving north-east from Enjo Zslutovody Square:

The City Administration building (Kosturska naprava), completed in the 1890s in a neo-Renaissance manner, was the seat of Habsburg municipal government. Since 1995 it has housed the City Management Office appointed by the UN High Commissioner.

The Theatre Square (Divadlov Trug) is the site of the two oldest and most respectable city's theatres. The Opera House, completed in 1881 and modelled on the Viennese Staatsoper, continues to function under UN Cultural Management authority.

The National Theatre (Drjezsavno Divadlo) — the Royal Theatre under the Habsburgs — occupies an earlier Italianate building of 1878. Its programme has historically been more varied than the opera house. Under socialism it became home to the State Theatrical Ensemble No. 1. It remains the Neutral Zone's most active performing venue.

The History and Fine Arts Museum, in a purpose-built neo-Baroque structure of 1886, situated further north-east, holds the principal collection of pre-twentieth-century Pannonian cultural heritage. The historical section contains the Hall of Ottoman Occupation, the hall of Austrian Occupation, the Hall of Hungarian Occupation, and the Hall of Soviet Occupation.

The Stock Exchange building, completed in 1890 in a neo-Classicist style, has not functioned as a trading floor since nationalization in 1946. Under socialism it became the People's Club (Narodny Zbor), a subsidized venue for lectures, concerts, and approved theatrical productions. Since 1990 it has stood vacant; UN-funded restoration began in 2003.

The Sedelmeier Passage, completed in 1903 to designs by the architect Jan Hofmann, was built for the arts patron and textile merchant Moritz Sedelmeier. It was the city's first purpose-built four-story arcade of retail galleries under a glass roof, and a showpiece of the local Sezession idiom. The Passage was nationalized in 1946, renamed "Universal Store No. 1", designated a protected monument in 1974, and restored between 1997 and 2003. It now operates as a boutique gallery for the Neutral Zone's international and professional clientele.

The Modern Art Gallery, housed in a Sezession building of 1910 also funded through Sedelmeier's patronage, holds the principal collection of twentieth-century Pannonian painting and sculpture, including the largest surviving assemblage of works from the interwar avant-garde.

The Upper Pannonian University, or the Old University (Stary Unyversytet) in common usage, stands east of the avenue, on University Street (Unyversytecka ulica). Founded by Maria Theresa, it taught exclusively in Latin for its first century; Hungarian was reluctantly added after 1867. Through the late Habsburg era it remained a citadel of conservatism and Catholic orthodoxy. Under the First Republic it was subordinated to the New University by state decree and substantially reorganized. It closed in 1991 amid fierce Zguro-Deglian student debates. Reopened in 1996 as an independent institution under UN administration, it enforces ethnic diversity through mandatory mixed Zguro-Deglian classes. This policy has led most Pannonian students to boycott it, the most progressive excepted. Its combination of minimal tuition and residual European prestige draws its student body predominantly from African countries. Instruction is in English.

The residential streets adjacent to the avenue retain their architectural character: late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century apartment buildings and private mansions. Their occupants have changed several times — original owners, Communist-era party elite, post-1995 international organizations and investors — while the buildings have not.
