Pannonian medieval chronicles

Sources on the medieval history of Former Upper Pannonia are scarce. Foreign writers rarely mentioned this remote corner of the Kingdom of Hungary. Local authors were few, and most of their work was lost during Ottoman rule.

Bregovo Episcopal Chronicle

From the mid-13th century, the court of the prince-bishops of Bregovo kept an official chronicle in Latin, known as the Deeds of the Prince-Bishops of Bregovo (Gesta principum episcoporum Bregovensium). The original manuscript was destroyed when the Ottomans took the city in 1529. The text survives only in two late redactions, the Venetian and the Olesczan. They differ considerably, and neither is considered reliable.

The last episcopal chronicler, a monk named Innocentius, survived the fall of the city, was taken captive, and ended up as an oarsman in the galleys of Hayreddin Barbarossa. At the Battle of Preveza in 1538, when the Ottoman fleet met the Christian coalition, his galley was wrecked. Innocentius survived once again: he escaped the sinking ship, swam to a Venetian galleon, and surrendered. He settled in Venice, where he worked for the Republic as a translator from Slavic languages. In 1547 he published the Bregovo Episcopal Chronicle in Venice, reconstructed entirely from memory. In the preface, he admitted that his memory was weak and imperfect, all the more so after his years as a Turkish slave, when he had been beaten about the head constantly. He apologized for the gaps and for errors he knew the text contained, but argued that, as far as he knew, no other copy of the chronicle existed, and without at least this version several centuries of Upper Pannonian history would be lost for good. Indeed, wherever his text can be checked against other sources, it is riddled with wrong dates and names, and it tends to inflate the role of the Bregovo bishops. For example, it misdates the Mongol invasion of Hungary to 1240 instead of 1241 and credits the retinue of Prince-Bishop Tamasz with a significant part in the Battle of Mohi, although no other source mentions it at all.

In 1772, the Pannonian priest Emmanuel Olesczan — younger brother of Count Viktor Olesczan, future Bishop of Vronovica, and at the time a keen bibliophile and manuscript collector — published a previously unknown version of the chronicle, now called the Olesczan redaction. According to Father Emmanuel, he had found the manuscript, a half-rotten, mouse-eaten copy from the early 16th century, in the neglected archive of the parish church at Czigra. The manuscript itself has not survived, and some doubt that it ever existed. In 1779, at the height of yet another feud between the Olesczans and the Bregovis, unknown raiders attacked the Olesczan estate at Gajnovo and burned its library. The published Gesta principum episcoporum is half again as long as the Venetian redaction and differs substantially in content. In his preface, Father Emmanuel offered a plausible explanation: Innocentius had written from memory. In the new version, Innocentius's errors are gone, the dates are corrected, and many facts are added, mostly unflattering to the Bregovis and flattering to the Olesczans. Under the communist regime of Gowbka, the Olesczan redaction was treated as unquestionably genuine, since it alone mentions the Pannonian peasant uprising of 1436 — an episode the Marxist view of history could not do without — and no doubts about its authenticity were tolerated. Modern scholars tend to be more skeptical.

Klykov Paschalion

In the Middle Ages, Klykov Monastery had its own distinctive tradition of Glagolitic writing in Old Church Slavonic. Its manuscripts are almost exclusively liturgical, and the monks kept no annals. The closest thing to a chronicle is the Klykov Paschalion, a table of calculated Easter dates from 1303 to 2898. The Glagolitic manuscript was produced in 1302, and over the following generations monks wrote brief notes on events in the blank spaces beside individual years. The Paschalion is far less informative than the Bregovo Episcopal Chronicle, but it contains a good deal of information absent in the latter, along with many discrepancies in dates and names.

The Paschalion is best known to the general public for recording several events that have not yet happened. These entries were added by a single anonymous author in the late 18th century. They are prophecies based on the eschatology of the Opisthonomics and probably on some astrological calculations. Among them: 1949 — the Jews will return to Jerusalem and rebuild Solomon's Temple; 1998 — the Antichrist will be born; 2028 — the Antichrist will begin preaching false doctrine and corrupt the Christian faith, after which only the Jews will keep faith in the one God; 2201 — the Jews will be carried off into slavery in Egypt; 2598 — a new Flood will destroy humankind, and Christ will appear to the few righteous survivors and restore the Kingdom of God on Earth; 2898 — God will take the whole created world into the Kingdom of Heaven, and its history will end. The Paschalion was discovered by Enjo Zslutovodyj, who listed it in his catalog, but it was not published in full until 1874, by Gostyvoj Bluxa. Bluxa was also the first to link the anonymous Klykov prophet's predictions to Opisthonomic teaching.
