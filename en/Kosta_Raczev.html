Kosta Raczev - DRAFT

Konstantyn Dominikov Raczev (Zgurian: Konstantyn Dominikov Raczev; Deglian: Konstantyn Dominikov Račev; Glagolitic: ⰍⰑⰐⰔⰕⰀⰐⰕⰬⰐ ⰄⰑⰏⰅⰐⰬⰍⰑⰂ ⰓⰀⰝⰅⰂ; diminutive Kosta; 1950–1994) was an Upper Pannonian politician. [to be continued once the main article is finished]

Early years (1950–1970)

Kosta was born on 23 June 1950, the second and last child of Dominik and Boleslava Raczev. The family of the People's Republic's chief writer enjoyed no shortage of privileges, but the boy barely saw his father, who was wholly absorbed in his work and spent his days and nights in the production director's office of the writers' sharashka at Beksziczi. When father and son did talk, the conversations were, as a rule, unpleasant for both of them.

Kosta attended the privileged School No. 41 in the Second District of Vronovica, where his rebellious streak showed early. He later recalled:

It all started simply enough. In literature class we covered my father's novels — one a year — and, naturally, we despised them with a passion. To avoid becoming a target myself, I had to prove I despised them more than anyone. I did it with flair, wit, and inventiveness. That's how my nonconformism began — out of conformism, of all things.

Kosta's nonconformism did not stop at mocking his father's books: having cast himself as a debunker of authority, he played the part in everything. His school diary, now kept in the family's apartment museum in South Vronovica, is thick with red ink: "Behaved defiantly," "Made cynical, ideologically unsound remarks," "Sang bourgeois songs." Beneath these teachers' notes stands his father's resolution, "Measures taken" — which usually meant a beating.

These corrective measures did no good. By his final years of school, Kosta's rebellion had taken on an openly political edge. He refused to join the Young Fighters' League (the Communist youth organization) — formally permissible, but unheard-of scandal for the son of a great Communist writer and member of the Party's Central Committee. Among close friends, over a glass of rakia, Kosta would openly call himself an anti-Communist, curse the Party in every possible way, and share what he knew about the goings-on at the Beksziczi sharashka (closed by then). He was picked up by the police more than once, including for a drunken public performance of the banned nationalist anthem "Krasnej sulna", which threatened serious trouble. Dominik Raczev had to use his influence to keep the case from going anywhere politically — and did so only under pressure from his wife: left to himself, he would have shipped his no-good son off "for re-education" without a second thought.

After finishing school, Kosta was drafted into the army, and thanks to his family's privileged standing he landed a cushy posting: a border unit at a railway station, where his whole service amounted to checking documents and cargo. The elder Raczev could have gotten his son out of service altogether, but chose not to, hoping the army would "beat the shit out of him" and "make a man of him." It didn't. Over his two years of service (1968–1970), Kosta's teenage defiance hardened into a settled, deliberate, ideological hatred of the system.

Dissidence and the "Trial of the Eighteen" (1970–1975)

After the army, Raczev enrolled in the history department at Vronovica University, choosing museum studies as his major: "No careerists there, but plenty of pretty girls from the provinces." At first, he kept his head down, dutifully taking notes in the required lectures on scientific Marxism-Gowbkaism and steering clear of public controversy. The one exception came in 1971, when he caused a scene at the Writers' House, throwing punches at the celebrated novelist Ljubodar Vloszek. In his statement to the police, Raczev claimed the attack had been over "personal reasons": Vloszek had recently "cruelly jilted" his sister Ursula. To friends, he told a different story — the divorce was merely a convenient pretext for publicly humiliating a "known NON informer" (NON being the Ministry of People's Security). When Dominik Raczev died in 1972, Kosta did not attend the funeral, which was its own small scandal.

Avoiding loud public demonstrations as much as his temperament allowed, Raczev spent his student years gathering a circle of free-thinking young people around himself. It began innocently enough — evenings in smoke-filled kitchens, unguarded conversation, foreign shortwave broadcasts, and readings and discussions of banned books drawn from Dominik Raczev's well-stocked library. All of this stayed within tolerated limits, but the circle soon crossed them, taking up samizdat distribution and attempting to make contact with the Queen Ljudmila Foundation (QLF). At this point the group came to NON's attention. An agent provocateur was planted among them, posing as a QLF emissary, the necessary evidence was gathered, and in May 1974 — at the height of the university exam session — the most prominent members, Raczev included, were arrested.

The investigator broke him easily. Raczev had been the group's obvious leader and organizer, and he alone had held actual working discussions with the supposed QLF emissary about setting up an information channel. The other members could be charged only with distributing subversive literature (up to ten years' imprisonment); Raczev faced espionage and treason, which meant a bullet in the head. It was blackmail, and Raczev caved. He struck a deal with the investigation and gave up everyone he knew. All eighteen members of the circle were arrested. At the trial — a show trial attended by foreign journalists — Kosta repented, wept, and begged for leniency. He even stooped to calling himself "unworthy of his great father." Leniency was granted: the indictment made no mention of any contact with the QLF, and the members of the "Group of the Eighteen" were charged only with "forming an illegal political organization" and "distributing subversive literature." Everyone but Raczev received five to ten years in the camps; Raczev, in recognition of his sincere repentance, got one year, with time served in pretrial detention counted toward it. The sentence was handed down in October 1974; he was released in May 1975.

Reassessment (1975–1976)

Raczev came out of prison to complete isolation. Every friend who hadn't been imprisoned had turned their back on him; acquaintances refused to shake his hand; the girl he loved, a fellow member of the "Group of Eighteen," was serving a ten-year sentence; and his already cool relations with his mother and sister had warmed not at all. Many people in his position drank themselves into the ground or took their own lives, but Kosta's pride and will to live were too strong for so inglorious a surrender. "I felt I had to rethink my whole path and find myself again," he recalled years later.

Raczev was allowed to sit his final exams and qualify as a museum worker. He took an obscure, poorly paid, dead-end job as a junior researcher at the Museum of Folk Art and Peasant Life. There he was assigned to catalogue the museum's unsorted holdings — among other things, sorting artifacts as Zgurian or Deglian. He started the work out of boredom, but it soon drew him in:

I'd never given a thought to my Deglian roots before. But the job forced me, at every turn, to answer the question: what exactly separates Deglian from Zgurian? The ethnography handbooks and folk-art guides didn't always have an answer. So I had to look for it in myself.

It was through this solitary work with the museum's collections and his reading of ethnographic literature that Raczev came to see himself as a Deglian, and his worldview shifted from vague anti-Communist opposition to Deglian nationalism. The shift was probably inevitable. Having betrayed his comrades, he needed some way to justify that betrayal to himself. To salvage his self-respect, Kosta had to convince himself that he had betrayed them not out of cowardice but out of deep philosophical disagreement — one he may not have fully recognized at the time. His ethnographic work, in this anguished search for self-justification, pointed him toward an answer rooted in ethnic identity. In 1976 Raczev wrote the treatise "Unity or Zgurification?" (Jednoŝ aĺ pozgureńё?), setting out that answer with clarity and force.

"Unity or Zgurification?" (1976–1977)

In the treatise that would go on to become the Bible of Deglian nationalism, Raczev tore the idea of a single Pannonian nation to shreds. No such nation existed, he argued — it was a Zgurian invention, dating to the 19th century when Zgurians seized control of the movement for independence from Hungary. Under the fraudulent banner of "Pannonian unity," the Zgurian majority was trying to assimilate the Deglian minority, stamp out its national consciousness, and erase the distinctiveness of its language and culture — even though it was the Deglians, not the Zgurians, who were the true heirs of Great Moravia and its cultural riches. Raczev's real target was less the People's Republic's state ideology, in which Pannonian unity ranked alongside socialism itself, than his former comrades from the dissident underground. In his view, they were either Zgurians themselves or had simply swallowed the Zgurian lie of a single nation. They dreamed of overthrowing the Communist regime, only to replace it with the same "united Pannonian" democratic republic, where Zgurians would go on dominating by sheer force of their democratic majority. Raczev brushed aside any charge of separatism and instead proposed a federal republic with cultural and linguistic autonomy for the Deglians, insisting that nothing in this contradicted Communist ideology — and pointing to the national autonomies of the USSR, Yugoslavia, and the PRC as precedent.

For more than a year, no one read the manuscript at all. Raczev tried showing it to Deglian colleagues who shared his interest in their ethnic roots, but they only shied away, taking him for a NON provocateur. So Kosta gambled on a desperate move: he typed up several dozen copies and, in February 1977, mailed them out to newspaper and magazine editors, the Ministry of Culture, the Party Central Committee, and NON itself. The NON men duly searched his apartment, confiscated the remaining copies and his typewriter, and warned him of the consequences — but no criminal case followed. The gamble paid off. No one published the treatise, of course, but dozens of people read it, and in some of them it struck a chord. Someone secretly copied the text of "Unity or Zgurification," and it made its way into samizdat circulation.

The seed fell on prepared ground. The People's Republic officially proclaimed equality among its nationalities, and by and large honored it, at least where Deglians were concerned (other minorities — Hungarians, Jews, Roma, and Muslim Slavs — faced real discrimination, but that is another story). Even so, Deglians, especially those who had moved from villages to the cities, regularly ran into negative stereotypes, mocked for their dialect and manners, and this bred resentment and a sense of being slighted. Raczev was the first to give these strong but inarticulate feelings a clear ideological voice. He had written his treatise upon recognizing himself as a Deglian. Deglians who read it recognized themselves too.
