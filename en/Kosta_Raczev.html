Kosta Raczev

Konstantyn Dominikov Raczev (Zgurian: Konstantyn Dominikov Raczev; Deglian: Konstantyn Dominikov Račev; Glagolitic: ⰍⰑⰐⰔⰕⰀⰐⰕⰬⰐ ⰄⰑⰏⰅⰐⰬⰍⰑⰂ ⰓⰀⰝⰅⰂ; diminutive Kosta; 1950–1991) was an Upper Pannonian politician. Born into a high-ranking Communist family, Raczev's rebellious streak turned him into an implacable enemy of the regime; he survived prison and a psychiatric hospital to become the ideologist and spiritual leader of Deglian nationalism, and, after the regime's fall, its political leader — probably contributing more than anyone else to igniting the Zguro-Deglian conflict. He died in murky circumstances at the height of a political crisis his own radicalism had helped create, a death that fixed nothing and brought reconciliation no closer. Deglians regard him as the heroic founding father of their republic; Zgurians, as a deranged fanatic chiefly to blame for the civil war.

Early years (1950–1970)

Kosta was born on 23 June 1950 in Vronovica, the second and last child of Dominik and Boleslava Raczev. The family of the People's Republic's chief writer enjoyed no shortage of privileges, but the boy barely saw his father, who was wholly absorbed in his work and spent his days and nights in the production director's office of the writers' sharashka at Beksziczi. When father and son did talk, the conversations were, as a rule, unpleasant for both of them.

Kosta attended the privileged School No. 41 in the Second District, where his rebellious streak showed early. He later recalled:

It all started simply enough. In literature class we covered my father's novels — one a year — and, naturally, we despised them with a passion. To avoid becoming a target myself, I had to prove I despised them more than anyone. I did it with flair, wit, and inventiveness. That's how my nonconformism began — out of conformism, of all things.

Kosta's nonconformism did not stop at mocking his father's books: having cast himself as a debunker of authority, he played the part in everything. His school diary, now kept in the family's apartment museum in South Vronovica, is thick with red ink: “Behaved defiantly,” “Made cynical, ideologically unsound remarks,” “Sang bourgeois songs.” Beneath these teachers' notes stands his father's resolution, “Measures taken” — which usually meant a beating.

These corrective measures did no good. By his final years of school, Kosta's rebellion had taken on an openly political edge. He refused to join the Young Fighters' League (the Communist youth organization) — formally permissible, but an unheard-of scandal for the son of a great Communist writer and member of the Party's Central Committee. Among close friends, over a glass of rakia, Kosta would openly call himself an anti-Communist, curse the Supreme Leader Gowbka in every possible way, and share what he knew about the goings-on at the Beksziczi sharashka (closed by then). He was picked up by the police more than once, including for a drunken public performance of the banned nationalist anthem “Krasnej sulna”, which threatened serious trouble. Dominik Raczev had to use his influence to keep the case from going anywhere politically — and did so only under pressure from his wife: left to himself, he would have shipped his no-good son off “for re-education” without a second thought.

After finishing school, Kosta was drafted into the army, and thanks to his family's privileged standing he landed a cushy posting: a border unit at a railway station, where his whole service amounted to checking documents and cargo. The elder Raczev could have gotten his son out of service altogether, but chose not to, hoping the army would “beat the shit out of him” and “make a man of him.” It didn't. Over his two years of service (1968–1970), Kosta's teenage defiance hardened into a settled, deliberate, ideological hatred of the system.

Dissidence and the “Trial of the Eighteen” (1970–1975)

After the army, Raczev enrolled in the history department at Vronovica University, choosing museum studies as his major: “No careerists there, but plenty of pretty girls from the provinces.” At first, he kept his head down, dutifully taking notes in the required lectures on scientific Marxism-Gowbkaism and steering clear of public controversy. The one exception came in 1971, when he caused a scene at the Writers' House, throwing punches at the celebrated novelist Ljubodar Vloszek. In his statement to the police, Raczev claimed the attack had been over “personal reasons”: Vloszek had recently “cruelly jilted” his sister Ursula. To friends, he told a different story — the sister's divorce was merely a convenient pretext for publicly humiliating a “known NON informer” (NON being the Ministry of People's Security). When Dominik Raczev died in 1972, Kosta did not attend the funeral, which was its own small scandal.

Avoiding loud public demonstrations as much as his temperament allowed, Raczev spent his student years gathering a circle of free-thinking young people around himself. It began innocently enough — evenings in smoke-filled kitchens, unguarded conversation, foreign shortwave broadcasts, and readings and discussions of banned books drawn from Dominik Raczev's well-stocked library. All of this stayed within tolerated limits, but the circle soon crossed them, taking up samizdat distribution and attempting to make contact with the Queen Ljudmila Foundation (QLF). At this point the group came to NON's attention. An agent provocateur was planted among them, posing as a QLF emissary. Raczev later recalled:

This cheerful, charming fellow handed me several QLF publications — priceless to us — and asked for a favor in return. “We're interested in anything out of Pannonia,” he said, “and I mean anything that doesn't make it into the papers. Accuracy doesn't matter, wild rumors are fine, the more absurd the better. They say an alien ship crashed near Tepelje? And is it true the Gowbkaslava Automobile Plant is developing a flying submarine?” I took it for a good joke and laughed it off, telling him I hadn't heard anything about a flying submarine, but I'd be sure to find out.

The necessary evidence was gathered, and in May 1974 — at the height of the university exam session — the most prominent members, Raczev included, were arrested. The investigator broke him easily. Raczev had been the group's obvious leader and organizer, and he alone had held actual working discussions with the supposed QLF emissary about transfer of classified information. A flying submarine had, in fact, been in development at the Gowbkaslava Automobile Plant. The other members could be charged only with distributing subversive literature (up to ten years' imprisonment); Raczev faced espionage and treason, which meant a bullet in the head. It was blackmail, and Raczev caved. He struck a deal with the investigation and gave up everyone he knew. All eighteen members of the circle were arrested. At the trial — a show trial attended by foreign journalists — Kosta repented, wept, and begged for leniency. He even stooped to calling himself “unworthy of his great father.”

Leniency was granted: the indictment made no mention of any contact with the QLF, and the members of the “Group of the Eighteen” were charged only with “forming an illegal political organization” and “distributing subversive literature.” Everyone but Raczev received five to ten years in the camps; Raczev, in recognition of his sincere repentance, got one year, with time served in pretrial detention counted toward it. The sentence was handed down in October 1974; he was released in May 1975.

Reassessment (1975–1976)

Raczev came out of prison to complete isolation. Every friend who hadn't been imprisoned had turned their back on him; acquaintances refused to shake his hand; the girl he loved, a fellow member of the “Group of Eighteen,” was serving a ten-year sentence; and his already cool relations with his mother and sister had warmed not at all. Many people in his position drank themselves into the ground or took their own lives, but Kosta's pride and will to live were too strong for so inglorious a surrender. “I felt I had to rethink my whole path and find myself again,” he recalled years later.

Raczev was allowed to sit his final exams and qualify as a museum worker. He took an obscure, poorly paid, dead-end job as a junior researcher at the Museum of Folk Art and Peasant Life. There he was assigned to catalogue the museum's unsorted holdings — among other things, sorting artifacts as Zgurian or Deglian. He started the work out of boredom, but it soon drew him in:

I'd never given a thought to my Deglian roots before. But the job forced me, at every turn, to answer the question: what exactly separates Deglian from Zgurian? The ethnography handbooks and folk-art guides didn't always have an answer. So I had to look for it in myself.

It was through this solitary work with the museum's collections and his reading of ethnographic literature that Raczev came to see himself as a Deglian, and his worldview shifted from vague anti-Communist opposition to Deglian nationalism. The shift was probably inevitable. Having betrayed his comrades, he needed some way to justify that betrayal to himself. To salvage his self-respect, Kosta had to convince himself that he had betrayed them not out of cowardice but out of deep philosophical disagreement — one he may not have fully recognized at the time. His ethnographic work, in this anguished search for self-justification, pointed him toward an answer rooted in ethnic identity. In 1976 Raczev wrote the treatise “Unification or Zgurification?” (Pojednenё aĺ pozgurenё?), setting out that answer with clarity and force.

“Unification or Zgurification?” (1976–1977)

In the treatise that would go on to become the Bible of Deglian nationalism, Raczev tore the idea of a single Pannonian nation to shreds. No such nation existed, he argued — it was a Zgurian invention, dating to the 19th century when Zgurians seized control of the movement for independence from Hungary. Under the fraudulent banner of “Pannonian unity,” the Zgurian majority was trying to assimilate the Deglian minority, stamp out its national consciousness, and erase the distinctiveness of its language and culture — even though it was the Deglians, not the Zgurians, who were the true heirs of Great Moravia and its cultural riches. Raczev's real target was less the People's Republic's state ideology than his former comrades from the dissident underground. In his view, they were either Zgurians themselves or had simply swallowed the Zgurian lie of a single nation. They dreamed of overthrowing the Communist regime, only to replace it with the same “united Pannonian” democratic republic, where Zgurians would go on dominating by sheer force of their democratic majority. Raczev brushed aside any charge of separatism and instead proposed a federal republic with cultural and linguistic autonomy for the Deglians, insisting that nothing in this contradicted Communist ideology — and pointing to the national autonomies of the USSR, Yugoslavia, and the PRC as precedent.

For more than a year, no one read the manuscript at all. Raczev tried showing it to Deglian colleagues who shared his interest in their ethnic roots, but they only shied away, taking him for a provocateur. So Kosta gambled on a desperate move: he typed up several dozen copies and, in February 1977, mailed them out to newspaper and magazine editors, the Ministry of Culture, and the Party Central Committee. The NON men duly searched his apartment, confiscated the remaining copies and his typewriter, and warned him of the consequences — but no criminal case followed. The gamble paid off. No one published the treatise, of course, but dozens of people read it, and in some of them it struck a chord. Someone secretly copied the text of “Unification or Zgurification,” and it made its way into samizdat circulation.

The seed fell on prepared ground. The People's Republic officially proclaimed equality among its nationalities, and by and large honored it, at least where Deglians were concerned (other minorities — Hungarians, Jews, Roma, and Muslim Slavs — faced real discrimination, but that is another story). Even so, Deglians, especially those who had moved from villages to the cities, regularly ran into negative stereotypes, mocked for their dialect and manners, and this bred resentment and a sense of being slighted. Raczev was the first to give these strong but inarticulate feelings a clear ideological voice. He had written his treatise upon recognizing himself as a Deglian. Deglians who read it recognized themselves too.

Second dissidence and psychiatric confinement (1977–1989)

Letters from readers began arriving. Raczev answered them eagerly, and with some correspondents the exchange grew into a real acquaintance. A circle started forming around him again — but this time its members came from a different world than his old friends, the fractious intelligentsia of the capital. They knew nothing of his reputation as a traitor; to them he was an intellectual authority, a man who had opened their eyes. Raczev recalled:

Some of them were, to put it mildly, odd. Self-taught peasant philosophers. Conspiracy theorists among retired military men. Thoughtful Dynamo fans looking for an ideological basis for their hatred of Sokol fans. One dreamed of reviving Slavic paganism; another went further still and wanted to revive the Dacian Kingdom and the cult of Criocephalus. One kept my work, hand-copied in a calligraphic bobovica, on a hidden bookshelf at home, wedged between the Protocols of the Elders of Zion and Zdjeslav Bluxa's The Venetic Race. Yet another, a ninety-year-old invalid, was very nearly the last living veteran of the Legion, with an Anabasis Medal received from Glovacz's own hands; under the First Republic he had been a DDZ militant, taken part in the pogroms of 1933, spent the whole war fighting with the monarchist Resistance, and served some twenty years in the camps afterward. “All my life I believed you had to cut down Communists,” he told me, “but you opened my eyes — it's Zgurians you have to cut down. I don't have long left, but I can still drag ten or so along with me to the other side; just tell me who.” He was, of course, a NON provocateur, but his life story, as far as I could tell, was true.

Raczev had learned caution. With people he barely knew, he stayed strictly within legal bounds, made no attempt to build an organization, and avoided anything that could be read as a hint of foreign contact. His only concern now was spreading his ideas. NON kept him under watch, hauled him in once or twice for “preventive talks,” and demanded the occasional report — but nothing more. State security probably did not see much of a threat in what Raczev was doing, and may even have found it useful, splitting dissident-minded citizens along ethnic lines.

But in the 1980s the economy began to deteriorate, popular discontent grew louder, and word of the strikes in Poland — and then of perestroika in the USSR — reached the country despite its isolation. NON grew far more jittery in this uneasy climate. From 1985 onward it launched a sweeping crackdown on every dissident group of any visibility at all, however quiet and harmless. Raczev's arrest was only a matter of time, and he hastened it himself by releasing a samizdat memoir, “Confession” (Spovёđ), in 1986. In this book — quoted several times above — Raczev not only laid out the story of his betrayal with total candor but also sketched a political program for the Deglian movement after the fall of the Communist regime, writing of that fall, quite coolly, as an event certain to arrive within the next few years. That was too much. In early 1987, Raczev was committed to a psychiatric hospital against his will.

For almost three years he was “treated” for an invented case of “paranoid schizophrenia” with the harshest tools in the arsenal of Socialist punitive psychiatry: electroshock, insulin-coma therapy, heavy antipsychotics. The terrifying ordeal cost him dearly in health, but it saved his reputation. The memory of his betrayal faded; in dissident circles Kosta Raczev was now seen as a martyr, and martyrdom atoned for the old sin. When he was released in November 1989, along with other political prisoners, almost immediately after Gowbka's death, a crowd was waiting outside the hospital with flowers to greet him — among them members of the “Group of Eighteen,” who had forgiven him everything.

Deglian People's Front (1990)

Raczev left the hospital with his health permanently broken: at forty he looked sixty, plagued by headaches, sleep disorders, and metabolic problems. His cognitive faculties had suffered too, and so had his temperament — both visible in his writing: his articles from 1990–1994, set beside “Unification or Zgurification?” and “Confession,” read noticeably harsher and cruder in argument, stripped of irony and thick with compulsive repetition. Yet his energy hadn't dimmed, his knack for winning people over and carrying them along stayed intact, and his sense of mission only grew stronger.

In December 1989, on the wave of headlong democratization, Upper Pannonia saw parties and private newspapers spring up everywhere. Most of the parties amounted to a dozen friends and acquaintances, and most of the newspapers folded before their third issue. Against this chaotic backdrop, the Deglian People's Front (Deglänski narodny front, DNF) and its newspaper, the Deglian Herald (Deglänski vёstaŕ), both founded by Raczev, drew little notice at first. But unlike most of their contemporaries, they not only survived — they began rapidly gaining popularity.

The euphoria of the first days after Communism's fall did not last long. Freedom had arrived, but the economic troubles only deepened. Social structures crumbled, old norms lost their grip, and ordinary people, unable to make sense of what was happening, found themselves lost amid an unfamiliar babel of opinions. The Communist Party had discredited itself beyond repair — renaming itself Socialist didn't help, nor did the timid promise, made at an extraordinary 17th Congress in December 1989, to “reassess Gowbka's legacy.” The democratic parties fared no better: by year's end they had already delivered on the whole of the opposition's program — freedom of speech, an end to repression, demilitarization, a multi-party system — and found no agreement on what should come next. The democrats fell to squabbling almost immediately, took to publicly slinging mud at one another, and soon lost what respect they'd had.

Nationalism filled the ideological vacuum. The Deglians were the first to succumb to its pull, being a minority who felt their ethnic distinctiveness more keenly than most. Where Raczev's treatise had once “opened the eyes” of a narrow circle of samizdat readers, his newspaper now did the same for a far wider Deglian audience. The success exceeded even Raczev's own expectations. By spring 1990, people were queuing up to join the party in Deglian districts, the Deglian Herald's print run grew with every issue, and the paper soon grew rich enough to open its own printing house — the first in the country to print in bobovica. At this stage the DNF's program still stopped short of demanding a separate Deglian state, calling only for territorial autonomy within a federal Upper Pannonia and for privatization carried out along ethnic quotas.

Rising Deglian nationalism provoked a reaction: Zgurian nationalism. Until then, Zgurian ethnic identity had been comparatively weak: a March 1990 poll found that most Zgurians identified themselves as Pannonians first and Zgurians only second (for Deglians, it was the other way around). But Zgurian sentiment began to shift under the pressure of Deglian grievances, often voiced aggressively. The Socialist Party split at its April conference, precisely over Zguro-Deglian disagreements. Two factions broke away: the Movement for the Defense of Zgurians, led by Ljubovan Jazvjec, and the Deglian Socialist Party (Degljanska Socialystycka Partya, DSP), led by Emilj Vcziski. The party's remaining core, which held to an internationalist line — the Socialist Party for a United Pannonia (Socialystycka Partya su Jednenow Pannoniju, SPJP) — saw its already modest popularity collapse rapidly.

The two Deglian parties — the DNF, made up mostly of sincere but politically inexperienced activists, and the DSP, of former Party functionaries rebranded as nationalists — competed with each other at first, but gradually drew closer. For Raczev, who remained a committed anti-Communist, this was an unpleasant but unavoidable compromise: the DNF, with its largely urban electorate, stood no chance in parliamentary elections without the DSP's administrative reach in the countryside. By autumn the two parties had agreed to divide up constituencies and ran in the election as a single bloc, with Vcziski second on the list behind Raczev. In the country's first multi-party elections, held on 14 October, the DNF–DSP bloc won an overwhelming majority in Deglian districts (as high as 90 percent, on turnout of about 75 percent) and took 42 of the 99 seats in the Legislative Assembly. Most Zgurians voted for the DZZ, but turned out less reliably and split their votes more widely, leaving the DZZ with 47 seats — short of an absolute majority. The remaining 10 seats went to the liberal Pannonian Democratic Renovation Party (PDRP), led by Ljubodar Vloszek, largely on the strength of votes from ethnic minorities and the segment of Zgurians who still held to a united Pannonian identity. The SPJP failed to clear the 5-percent threshold and, like a good hundred minor parties, did not make it into parliament.

As leader of the parliamentary faction, Raczev became known for thunderous speeches, radicalism, and intransigence. Neither nationalist party controlled a majority on its own, and both needed a coalition with the PDRP to form a government or pass necessary legislation. Vloszek and Vcziski were ready to cooperate; Raczev was flatly against it. He scuttled the first attempt at an agreement, and when the PDRP went ahead and formed a coalition with the Zgurian faction instead, he took his supporters into the streets: “We won't let the Zgurians and the liberals sell out the Deglians again!” The resulting protest, the “Deglian March” of 5 December 1990, drew Dynamo fans along with Deglian sedmaki and spremniki, and descended into vandalism and looting — the first mass act of political violence in post-Socialist Upper Pannonia. The coalition collapsed, the government once again failed to form, and the paralysis of power dragged on and deepened. Raczev's standing among Deglian voters had never been higher.

Personal life

Kosta Raczev was a man of ideas, thoroughly indifferent to money and material comfort. His father left no inheritance to the son he never loved: his copyrights all passed to his widow, and his property to her and his daughter. Boleslava bought Kosta a one-room flat in the Sixth District, where he lived out his life, turning it into a cluttered bachelor's den heaped with books. Raczev had charisma and knew how to make an impression, and in his youth he attracted plenty of romantically inclined young women. He himself set little store by romantic attachments and treated women as disposable — an affair with one of the “Group of Eighteen” hadn't stopped him from giving her up along with everyone else. In 1980 Raczev married Desanka Zslutovodska (1950–2021), a colleague at the Museum of Folk Art, and acquaintances noted that for the first time in years he was decently dressed and his flat was in order. The couple had no children. Not long after Raczev's release from the psychiatric hospital, his wife left him. “He'd become a different person,” she said, defending herself against accusations of abandoning her husband in his hour of need. “Kosta could be unbearable before, but now he was simply frightening.” Other acquaintances likewise noted how much his temper had soured after the “treatment.” He had no friends during this period, and the people who worked closely with him at the newspaper and the party rarely lasted long.

Death (1991)

On the evening of 6 January 1991, in heavy snowfall, Raczev drove out of Vronovica toward Gowbkaslava, where a conference of the local DNF branch was scheduled for the next day. He never arrived. At the eighteenth kilometer of the South Highway, in a stretch of empty woodland, his Volkswagen Golf collided head-on at high speed with an unidentified heavy vehicle, probably a truck. His driver was killed instantly; the Golf spun out on the slippery road and went into the ditch, while the other vehicle, undamaged, fled the scene. Raczev survived, though badly hurt, and managed to climb out of the car. Instead of heading for the road, he set off into the woods for some reason — perhaps he had lost his eyesight or his bearings. A few steps in, he walked face-first into a sharp branch jutting from a pine trunk. It killed him. Rescuers found his body the next day, pinned to the tree. The snow had covered every trace, leaving no way to tell whether Raczev had died by accident or with someone's help.

Whatever the truth, the accident looked far too much like murder. Deglians blamed the Zgurians; Zgurians blamed the moderate wing of the DNF–DSP, for whom Raczev's intransigence had become an obstacle. The investigation went nowhere, and the key piece of evidence — the vehicle that struck him — was never found. Theories have multiplied ever since, but the truth is unlikely ever to be settled: Kosta Raczev had gotten in too many people's way. Leadership of the bloc passed to moderates under Vcziski, who soon merged the two parties, but Raczev's death only inflamed the standoff and made further compromise harder still. The chief troublemaker was gone, but that did nothing to halt the slide toward catastrophe.
