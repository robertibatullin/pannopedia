Pannonian names

Pannonian personal names reflect several historically distinct layers of given names and surnames, each shaped by a different phase of the country's political and linguistic history.

Given names

Slavic dithematic names

Dithematic names were revived by 19th-century Slavic Revival activists and reached their widest use during the First Republic, but are now considered archaic. Unlikely for people born before 1850 or after 1960; most typical for those born between 1918 and 1940.

Hungaricized Christian names

These names are most typical for characters born before 1900. For those born after 1918, this choice signals madjaron sympathies of the parents; after 1945 such names were legally banned and bearers required to adopt Latinized equivalents. After 1990 they began to recover ground and are now relatively common, though not dominant.

Surnames

Earliest stratum

Toponym + -i/-j pattern

Surnames modelled on Hungarian toponymic forms, used by 18th-century Pannonian nobility. Example: Bregovi “from Bregovo,” Zslutovodaj “from Zsluty Vody” (Yellow Waters). They indicate patrician origin and are correspondingly rare and prestigious.

Ethnicity

Another old pattern common to both nobility and commoners. These surnames do not necessarily indicate foreign origin: a person might earn the nickname Njemec from blonde hair or a command of German, which nickname would then pass to their children.

Nickname-derived

A common and varied pattern: any word denoting a physical or character trait could generate a nickname that passed into a hereditary surname. Examples: Gowbka (lit. “small lip”), Glovacz (lit. “big-headed”)

Josephine stratum

In 1787, Joseph II required all his subjects to register surnames. Most Pannonian peasants still lacked them, and most registered either by patronymic — father's given name plus -ov(a)/-ev(a) — or by their village name plus -ski/-ska (the aristocratic -y being reserved for established families). This is the most common surname pattern from 1787 to the present. The patronymic suffix is -ov(a) after hard consonants or -o, and -ev(a) after soft (palatalized) consonants or -e. Examples:
