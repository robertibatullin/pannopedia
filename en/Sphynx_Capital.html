Sphynx Capital — DRAFT

Sphynx Capital (Zgurian and Deglian Sfinks Kapital) was a fraudulent financial company that operated in Former Upper Pannonia (FUP) in 1997–1998. More than half of FUP's population fell victim to it, and its collapse set off acute political crises in both unrecognized states on the country's territory, the Deglian and Zgurian Republics.

The company was founded in 1991 by Vardan Rutny (1955–1999), a dwarf who performed in a travelling circus. In 1990 he had played a bit part, [ПРИДУМАТЬ РОЛЬ], in [ПРИДУМАТЬ НАЗВАНИЕ], an experimental production by the Vronovica postmodernist director Berislav Zslutovodski. Zslutovodski had close family ties to the capital's elite. That same year his sister Desanka divorced the prominent dissident Kosta Raczev to marry Prime Minister Vaclav Bluxa, while another sister, the actress Lydya, had once been married to Vaclav's brother Adam Bluxa, a well-known lawyer. The director himself was married to Hanna Jadenova, daughter of the head of the Economic Intelligence Directorate at the Ministry of People's Security. Rutny's memorable turn in a scandalous production apparently won him friends in high places, who later proved useful in business.

Through the civil war and the first post-war years, Sphynx Capital kept a low profile; officially, it was engaged in “financial intermediation.” It operated in both warring halves of the country and probably helped both sides get around international sanctions. By 1997 things had changed: many of the old schemes no longer worked, the business was under threat, and Rutny turned to building a financial pyramid. He had some starting capital, enough to launch a Ponzi scheme that paid out enormous dividends in order to draw in as many new investors as possible. At first he relied on multi-level marketing, then still a novelty in Eastern Europe. Investors were given ranks from “Scarab” to “Pharaoh” according to how many new investors they had brought in, and the rank determined the interest rate. For a Pharaoh, it reached 150% a month.

Payouts arrived reliably and on time, and investors started coming — but still too slowly, so in the summer of 1997 Rutny launched a massive advertising campaign. On some days he bought up all of prime time on television in both republics, and Sphynx billboards covered the streets of both halves of Vronovica as well as the Neutral Zone, where the company had its head office. The response exceeded all expectations. The office was crammed with cardboard boxes stuffed with cash; boxes were also how the cocaine arrived for the weekly corporate parties. By then, pyramid schemes had already collapsed in Albania, bringing down the country's economy and central government with them. Asked the obvious question — where exactly did Sphynx's profits come from? — Rutny would reply: “You don't understand how capitalism works. Money goes to money.” Which was perfectly true.
