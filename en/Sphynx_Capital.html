Sphynx Capital

Sphynx Capital (Zgurian and Deglian Sfinks Kapital) was a fraudulent financial company that operated in Former Upper Pannonia (FUP) in 1991–1998. More than half of FUP's population fell victim to it, and its collapse set off acute political crises in both unrecognized states on the country's territory, the Deglian and Zgurian Republics.

Ascension

The company was founded in 1991 by Vardan Rutny (1955–1999), a dwarf who performed in a travelling circus. In 1990 he had played Karl Marx in "A Feast of Philosophers", an experimental production by the Vronovica postmodernist director Berislav Zslutovodski. Zslutovodski had close family ties to the capital's elite. That same year his sister Desanka divorced the prominent dissident Kosta Raczev to marry Prime Minister Vaclav Bluxa, while another sister, the actress Lydya, had once been married to Vaclav's brother Adam Bluxa, a well-known lawyer. The director himself was married to Hanna Jadenova, daughter of the head of the Economic Intelligence Directorate at the Ministry of People's Security. Rutny's memorable turn in a scandalous production apparently won him friends in high places, who later proved useful in business.

Through the civil war and the first post-war years, Sphynx Capital kept a low profile; officially, it was engaged in “financial intermediation.” It operated in both warring halves of the country and probably helped both sides get around international sanctions. By 1997 things had changed: many of the old schemes no longer worked, the business was under threat, and Rutny turned to building a financial pyramid. He had some starting capital, enough to launch a Ponzi scheme that paid out enormous interests in order to draw in as many new investors as possible. At first he relied on multi-level marketing, then still a novelty in Eastern Europe. Investors were given ranks from “Scarab” to “Pharaoh” according to how many new investors they had brought in, and the rank determined the interest rate. For a Pharaoh, it reached 50% a month.

Zenith

Payouts arrived reliably and on time, and investors started coming — but still too slowly, so in the summer of 1997 Rutny launched a massive advertising campaign. On some days he bought up all of prime time on television in both republics, and Sphynx billboards covered the streets of both halves of Vronovica as well as the Neutral Zone, where the company had its head office. The response exceeded all expectations. The office was crammed with cardboard boxes stuffed with cash; boxes were also how the cocaine arrived for the weekly corporate parties. By then, pyramid schemes had already collapsed in Albania, bringing down the country's economy and central government with them. Asked the obvious question — where exactly did Sphynx's profits come from? — Rutny would reply: “You don't understand how capitalism works. Money goes to money.” Which was perfectly true.

Financial regulation in both republics and the Neutral Zone was rudimentary at best, after the fall of socialism, Vaclav Bluxa's hasty and largely failed market reforms, and a ruinous war. Almost anything could pass for a security. Sphynx certificates, known as “papyri,” traded briskly on the informal open-air exchange on Railway Station Street (Kolostanska ulica), and banks — most of them fraudulent as well — accepted them as collateral. A bigger factor still was the financial illiteracy of a population that had only just come out of survival mode, scraped together some savings, and caught the get-rich-quick bug. Most people had no idea what was going on. A minority understood perfectly well but counted on cashing out in time. Both groups poured everything they had into papyri: they sold cars, flats, houses and land, and ran up huge debts, some of them to gangsters. People hauled Deutschmarks and zlotniks to Sphynx offices by the sackful and queued for days and nights to buy a coveted papyrus at face value rather than at the speculative market price. By February 1998, by rough estimates, up to 60% of FUP's population (both republics and the Neutral Zone included) held papyri, and about 25% had sunk a large share of their assets into them.

In February 1998 the inflow of deposits slowed, and Rutny raised the interest rate — the first warning sign. The market grew nervous. To create the appearance of a real business, Rutny began buying up bombed-out houses and mined plots of land, promising to sell them at a huge profit once they had been restored. It helped, but not for long. The governments of both republics realized they had to act, rather than just keep cycling public money through the papyrus market for their own profit. An uncontrolled collapse of Sphynx threatened Albanian-style chaos, but any government that shut the company down would be committing suicide. If the Zgurian government did it, for instance, Zgurian investors would blame it for their ruin, and Deglian investors would blame the Zgurians. Most people were still armed, and bloodshed would be all but certain. The other government, meanwhile, would keep its hands clean. In this duel, whoever fired first lost.

Resolution

The best outcome for both republics was for the ban to come from the UN mission, UNOMFUP — all the more so since Sphynx's head office stood on territory under its jurisdiction. The Zgurian prime minister, Milan Jezsek, and his Deglian counterpart, Marko Kazsenec, travelled to the Neutral Zone to win over the UN High Commissioner, Bjørn Ellingsen. On the evening of 3 March the leaders of the two unrecognized states met in the waiting room of the Ban's Palace. Jezsek's memoirs give a vivid account of what followed:

We greeted each other with curt nods. There was no love lost between us. We had both fought in the war. Only three years earlier our batteries had faced each other near Komarovica, lobbing 76 mm shells across the Drava. Each of us had personal scores to settle with the other. But men who have been to war sometimes find it easier to understand each other and to see where their common interest lies. And we saw it clearly: to spare both our nations another catastrophe. Quickly, almost without words, we agreed to ask for a joint audience: arguments from two hostile sides would carry far more weight.

Ellingsen received us, probably out of sheer curiosity. I spoke first:

“Mr. High Commissioner, unlike us, you don't answer to voters. You can afford the luxury of an unpopular but absolutely necessary decision. Yes, you will be blamed for everything, but ruined investors are not going to storm the Neutral Zone with FUPFOR troops guarding it.”

And if they do, I thought, it will only unite Zgurians and Deglians, which is more or less the High Commissioner's job anyway. Naturally, I kept that quip to myself. All the same, Ellingsen had no wish to be the scapegoat. He began to wriggle:

“I'm not sure I have any authority in financial matters. The Lausanne Agreements say nothing about it. We simply register companies; we don't interfere in their business. A decision this explosive, without the firmest legal basis…”

I was about to urge the High Commissioner to think like a politician rather than a bureaucrat dodging responsibility, but at that point Kazsenec joined in. With crude, cynical, thoroughly Deglian bluntness, he said:

“Look, Mr. High Commissioner, let's cut the crap. We all know a thing or two about life, or we wouldn't be sitting where we are, and of course we've all got a bit of dough riding on this Sphynx. No need for any official decrees. Just dump all your papyri at once, in one bundle, and the market will get the message. All that matters is that the signal comes from the Neutral Zone. Sure, you'll take a loss, my colleague and I will lose even more, but at least we'll stay alive…”

Ellingsen, a respectable Norwegian not yet used to Balkan ways of doing things, went white, jumped to his feet and practically stamped:

“Are you drunk, Prime Minister? I will not listen to these insinuations! This conversation is over! Both of you, get out at once!”

Once we were outside, Kazsenec turned to me in an almost friendly tone:

“Well, comrade, looks like any day now we'll have to get the hell out of the country. What's your second passport? What do you mean, none? Not even a Paraguayan one? No, that won't do. Here's the number of a guy who can help. He'll do the whole family in a day for a very modest price. You can even pay in papyri while they're still worth something…”

I never used that number, and I never owned any papyri either, whatever my enemies may say. Dejected, I went back to my office, where a scheduled meeting with Mixal Bregovi awaited me at the end of the working day. It was meant to be about the return of the museum treasures that had been evacuated from the country. But Bregovi and I had long been on good, trusting terms, and I told him what was worrying me most.

“You're right, of course,” said Bregovi. “The ban has to come from the High Commissioner. I promise nothing, but I'll try talking to Bjørn and see if I can change his mind.”

I had no great hopes for that conversation. Imagine my surprise when, the next morning, the radio announced the High Commissioner's order for a financial audit of Sphynx Capital and the suspension of its operations until the results were in.

Collapse and aftermath

The High Commissioner's order of 4 March had consequences that were predictable but no less disastrous for that. The papyrus had already started falling before the announcement, evidently thanks to an insider leak; once the news was out, it crashed to a few percent of face value within an hour or so. Investors stormed the Sphynx offices, which staff and guards had hastily abandoned. Some found leftover cash inside, and fights broke out over it at once; 15 people were killed on the first day alone. Rutny tried to flee the country hidden in a suitcase but was found at the border and arrested.

As the scale of their losses sank in — for many, it meant losing everything they owned or being saddled with debts they could never repay — investors took to the streets in all three zones of FUP. The protests gradually lost steam by the end of March and passed without serious violence. The investors organized fairly quickly and put forward two demands: their money back, and the High Commissioner's resignation. They got the second: Ellingsen stepped down of his own accord on 9 March. That spring, the UN Quadrilateral Commission on FUP appointed a new High Commissioner, Anitha Samarasinghe, and gave her broader powers over financial regulation.

The Zgurian and Deglian cabinets survived at first by promising to compensate investors for their losses. They partly kept that promise, but only by running the printing presses flat out. The inevitable result was hyperinflation in both currencies introduced the year before, the Zgurian and the Deglian zlotnik. An Albanian-style nightmare was avoided, but the protests soon flared up again, and by the autumn of 1998 both Jezsek and Kazsenec had been forced out. From then on the two republics went separate ways. In Zguria, President Vloszek appointed a new cabinet under Vaclav Szarojanov without holding elections, leaving the ruling ZDS in power. In Deglia, the protests were more radical and forced a snap election. It was won by European Path, a fiercely anti-government opposition party that promised not only to return people's savings but also to send war criminals to The Hague. Influential veterans took a dim view of this, and in 1999 they staged a military coup, setting up a dictatorship that remains in place to this day.

That same year, Rutny died in solitary confinement in a Neutral Zone remand prison while still under investigation, at the age of 44, in circumstances that have never been explained. What became of Sphynx's cash — by some estimates, up to a billion dollars at 1998 prices — remains a mystery. Much of it was probably spirited away by major insider investors, but nobody knows who they were or how much they took.
