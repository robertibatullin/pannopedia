Pannonian epic poetry

Pannonian epic poetry is a genre of folk song in Former Upper Pannonia. Historical records show that in Ottoman times and the early years of Austrian rule the country was roamed by wandering singers, the gowdlari (sing. gowdlarj). They performed songs and ballads to the gowdlo, a bowed instrument somewhat like a mandolin or the Serbian gusle. In the early 18th century the Pannonian Church turned against these singers, who in its view lured the common people away from church services and troubled their souls. Counts Beatus, Julius and Viktor Olesczan, who ruled various parts of Upper Pannonia in the 18th century, were zealous Catholics and persecuted the gowdlari energetically. As a result, the tradition survived only among a small Muslim minority, the so-called “Turks” (in fact Slavic Muslims). Among the Christian majority it was stamped out completely, and no texts have come down to us.

Interest in the folk song tradition revived in the 19th century, carried by national Romanticism and the Slavic Revival. In 1857 Dr. Gostyvoj Bluxa toured the Muslim villages of Skorczin district and took down several dozen lyric and epic songs from the last surviving gowdlari. He published them in 1859 in two collections, The Pannonian Calliope and The Pannonian Euterpe. In the preface to the first, which contained the epic songs and ballads, Bluxa made no secret of his disappointment: the material was far too Muslim in flavor. Ballads extolling the deeds of heroes named Ali Oglan or Skander Gazi in battle against the infidels were hardly fit to serve as a Pannonian Kalevala. Only the tale of Djirdjis (Saint George) offered something of interest, possibly of pre-Islamic origin. Upper Pannonia, it says, is the land where Djirdjis slew the dragon. The dragon's carcass lies rotting underground, poisoning the land with its miasma and breeding monsters and every kind of misfortune, and so it will remain until the end of time, when a new Flood washes the accursed land clean. The other texts aroused little interest outside academic circles and had no real influence on the emerging Pannonian literature.

Meanwhile the gowdlarj tradition was dying out among the Pannonian Muslims as well, and early 20th-century ethnographers considered it extinct. Nevertheless, in 1955, under the socialist regime, the young folklorist Borivoj Zslutovodski visited the Muslim villages of Skorczin district and found an aged blind singer named Alisza Braximov, the last keeper of the tradition. Braximov claimed to be a hundred and twenty years old and to have met Gostyvoj Bluxa in his youth and sung for him. These claims rest on the word of Braximov, or perhaps of Zslutovodski, since the folklorist is the only source for them. Zslutovodski recorded a large number of Braximov's songs and ballads on subjects entirely unknown to earlier ethnographers. The greatest sensation was a cycle about the Kucko rebellion, at once declared a national classic. But if Bluxa had met the young Braximov and recorded his songs, why had he not recorded the Kucko Epic? Zslutovodski had a simple answer: Habsburg censorship would never have allowed verses glorifying a peasant revolt against feudal lords and churchmen into print.

Alisza Braximov died in 1966, showered with every conceivable state honor. Whether or not he composed all the songs attributed to him, the gowdlarj tradition ended with him for good: he left no pupils.
