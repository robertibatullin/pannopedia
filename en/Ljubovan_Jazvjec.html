Ljubovan Jazvjec

Ljubovan Petrov Jazvjec (Zgurian; Deglian: Lübovan Petrov Jazvёc; Glagolitic: ⰎⰣⰁⰑⰂⰀⰐ ⰒⰅⰕⰓⰑⰂ ⰡⰈⰂⰟⰜ; 1935–1991) was an Upper Pannonian politician. He began his career as a Communist Party functionary and, after the collapse of socialism, quickly reinvented himself as a Zgurian nationalist and one of the leading political figures of the country's collapse and disintegration era. His untimely death spared him a role in the civil war — though it may also have helped bring the war closer.

Career in the People's Republic (1935–1989)

Jazvjec was born into a poor peasant family — the standard formula of official Communist biographies — and belonged to the Zgurian people. During the Hungarian occupation, his father was conscripted into the Hungarian Royal Army, fought on the Eastern Front, and went missing at Stalingrad: a fact official biographies passed over in silence. The rest of the biography followed the same well-worn pattern: village school, conscript service in the People's Liberation Army, work as a construction laborer in Vronovica, grassroots activism, membership in the Communist Party of Upper Pannonia (CPUP) from 1959, and entry-level party work in the committee of his construction enterprise.

In 1965, Jazvjec attended the CPUP's Twelfth Party Congress as a delegate from his local party organization, where he caught the eye of Supreme Leader Severyn Gowbka himself. His career took off from that moment. Malicious gossip attributed his rise to his appearance — short and prematurely bald — which Gowbka allegedly found endlessly amusing; according to this account, the generalissimo could never resist a grin at their meetings, or the chance to give Comrade Jazvjec's bald head a resounding slap.

More substantial reasons probably played a part as well. In 1964, another attempt — real or fabricated — was made on Gowbka's life, followed by the usual wave of repression, this time a sweeping purge of the party apparatus's upper and middle ranks. The Supreme Leader was shedding his old wartime comrades and replacing them with younger men of his own choosing. The Twelfth Congress marked a turning point in this process, and Jazvjec's rapid rise fit the pattern neatly.

Moreover, Ljubovan evidently had some genuine talent for administration, since he was entrusted with serious posts: chairman of the construction workers' trade union Party committee, head of the housing-construction section of the Vronovica city Party committee, chairman of the district committee of the Seventh District (during the peak of its large-scale development), and finally, in 1981, chairman of the Vronovica city Party committee itself. As party boss of the capital, he held one of the highest offices in the CPUP, with an accompanying seat on the Politburo.

As the capital's mayor, in effect, Jazvjec earned a reputation as a businesslike, pragmatic administrator who competently handled the mounting problems of the socialist economy's slow decline. Gifted with a sharp tongue and an easy, informal manner, he had no fear of unscripted encounters with ordinary people and could field pointed questions with disarming wit. His popularity grew, and with it his political weight. After 1985, whispered gossip in the Central Committee's smoking rooms increasingly held that the aging Gowbka would step down at the Seventeenth CPUP Congress, planned for 1990, and hand power to Jazvjec, who would then carry out the long-overdue political and economic reforms.

In 1988, however, he fell out of favor after criticizing Miljana Gowbka, the Supreme Leader's wife, for allegedly “pushing her fellow Deglians into every position.” The remark was made in a small private circle, but it reached the generalissimo's ears quickly. Jazvjec was removed from his post “for health reasons” and expelled from the Politburo without delay; Gowbka's death on 10 November 1989 may well have saved him from worse consequences.

Collapse of the Communist regime (late 1989)

By this point Jazvjec had accumulated considerable political capital: he was known as an effective and popular mayor, his fall from Gowbka's favor only burnished his reputation, and he bore no responsibility for the regime's repression, censorship, militarization, or failed foreign policy. Among Zgurians, his earlier criticism of Miljana's Deglian sympathies earned him further credit. The day after Gowbka's death, the Politburo held a secret session, reinstated Jazvjec, and offered him the party leadership. He turned it down, recognizing the CPUP for the sinking ship it was. He also declined to keep his post as head of the Vronovica city Party committee, arguing that the city's leader ought to be chosen by its citizens in an election — he expected to win that election outright and secure a mandate stronger than anything the unpopular Politburo could offer. During the unrest that gripped Vronovica, he personally addressed the angry crowds and calmed them, averting attacks on government buildings. Though still formally a Party member, he was rapidly becoming an independent political force in his own right, with a following of his own — made up mostly of Zgurians.

In December 1989, the Legislative Assembly announced a program of political reform: multi-party politics, freedom of the press, the release of political prisoners, and preparations for a market transition, beginning with the immediate legalization of private enterprise. Multi-party elections were set for October 1990, giving parties time to organize. That same month, an extraordinary Seventeenth CPUP Congress renamed the party the Socialist Party and adopted a reform program of democratization, a reassessment of Gowbka's legacy, and a shift toward a moderately market-based economy. At that congress, Deglians for the first time openly raised the issue of language rights and a switch of the alphabet to bobovica, meeting resistance from Zgurians, who insisted on a single language and bluxovica — the moment the ethnic divide first surfaced openly in politics. Jazvjec spoke forcefully on the Zgurian side.

Movement for the Defense of Zgurians (1990–1991)

In April 1990, the Socialist Party held its pre-election conference — its first, and, as it turned out, its last. Delegates gathered against a backdrop of deepening ethnic polarization and ideological crisis: support for the former Communists had never been lower, the newly legal democratic parties had already splintered into infighting, while the radical-nationalist Deglian National Front (DNF), led by the charismatic dissident Kosta Raczev, was gaining ground fast. At the conference, Jazvjec declared: “We should stop thinking about national unity — there is none left — and start thinking about defending Zgurians,” drawing thunderous applause along with indignant jeers. The party split three ways: a dwindling internationalist core, the Socialist Party for a United Pannonia (SPUP); the Movement for the Defense of Zgurians (MDZ), led by Jazvjec himself; and the Deglian Socialist Party (DSP), which ran on a joint slate with the DNF that autumn and soon merged with it. The MDZ platform called for transforming Upper Pannonia into a Zgurian Republic, on the grounds that Zgurians made up a majority of the population, for making bluxovica the sole official alphabet, and for formally equal rights for minorities, including Deglians: print in bobovica was permitted, but every Deglian organization was to be banned outright, and autonomy was off the table. The platform was rounded out with populist pledges — halt inflation, end unemployment, carry out privatization “fairly,” and join NATO and the EU within five years.

To everyone's surprise, including Jazvjec's own, the MDZ took about 45 percent of the vote in Zgurian districts in the October elections, and the DNF roughly the same in Deglian ones; the SPUP, unable to shake off the CPUP's legacy, failed to enter parliament at all, while the liberal Pannonian Democratic Renovation Party (PDRP) took around 7 percent. Jazvjec led a faction that fell just short of a majority, but the parliament was paralyzed by the Zgurian-Deglian split and could not form a government: any coalition between either nationalist party and the PDRP instantly triggered street protests from nationalists on the opposing side. The country was run by a weak-mandated “caretaker technical government” that barely controlled events, while the economy collapsed and the state apparatus decayed. Political violence escalated through 1991. In local elections for county, city, and district councils, the MDZ swept the Zgurian districts; Jazvjec, who controlled the party apparatus and its candidate lists, became the de facto ruler of the Zgurian half of the country. The DNF likewise consolidated power in the Deglian districts, while in areas of mixed population — including every major city — the two nationalist parties fought for control by any means available, with little regard for the law.

Personal life

Jazvjec's first wife, Jarka, an unremarkable housewife who bore him two children, filed for divorce immediately after his fall from favor in 1988 — evidently out of fear, and well-founded fear at that: two of her close relatives had already ended up in the camps as wives of enemies of the people. Jazvjec took the divorce hard, a second brutal blow coming right after his dismissal. He cut off all contact with his ex-wife and never saw the children again, even after Jarka tried to reconcile following 1989. The fact that she was Deglian may have played some part in pushing him toward nationalist politics.

In April 1990, just as his career as MDZ leader was taking off, Jazvjec began dating the journalist Svjetana Jozsefova. Their acquaintance started with a heated on-air argument. They married in September, on the eve of the elections; Svjetana became a loyal political partner, running the party's media operations from 1991 onward. The couple had no children. Curiously, both of Jazvjec's wives were taller than average — perhaps why he tended to avoid being photographed next to them.

Those who knew Jazvjec remember him as good-natured despite his occasionally aggressive rhetoric, open and easy to talk to, and, at the same time, almost pathologically tidy, unable to tolerate the smallest disorder around him. His office was not only immaculately clean but arranged with near-geometric precision, everything on his desk lined up as if with a ruler. Unlike most CPUP functionaries — and most Pannonians generally — he neither smoked nor drank to speak of; and, as befit a proper Zgurian, he was a devoted supporter of Sokol Vronovica.

Death and political legacy

On 28 August 1991, Jazvjec, vacationing with his wife at the resort of Zorzsinski Dubri, died in front of her in what was ruled an accident; for details, see the article on the Zorzsin Tunnel. Political opponents tried to accuse Svjetana, the only witness to his death, of murdering her husband, but forensic examination confirmed her account of the accident. The loss of the MDZ's leader threw the party into turmoil and infighting. Svjetana attempted to seize the leadership herself, but stood no real chance amid escalating violence and the party's transformation into an armed paramilitary group. Had Jazvjec lived, he probably could not have stopped this process — but his death certainly hastened it.

Although Jazvjec did not live to see the Zgurian Republic declare independence, he is regarded there as one of its founding fathers — revered all the more for never having had the chance to stain his record with war crimes. His responsibility for the pre-war violence of 1990–1991, the pogroms and political killings, has never been proven. The MDZ leader likely made a genuine effort to stay within civilized bounds: while demanding ruthlessness against Deglian “militants and terrorists,” he condemned attacks on civilians. None of this stops the Deglian Republic's official propaganda today from casting him as an arch-villain, the architect of genocide, and the man chiefly responsible for the civil war.
