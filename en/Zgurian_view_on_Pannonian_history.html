Zgurian view on Pannonian history

This article summarizes the Zgurian version of Former Upper Pannonia's (FUP) history, at the points where it diverges from the Deglian version. This account appears in textbooks published in the Zgurian Republic, underpins its official historical memory, and is shared by most Zgurians apart from a small number of nonconformists.

Origins of the Pannonian peoples

Zgurians are the indigenous population of Upper Pannonia, an authentically Slavic people related to the Scythians of ancient sources. They were the dominant nation in Great Moravia and the medieval Pannonian principalities, and the language of the medieval Glagolitic manuscripts is Zgurian.

During the Ottoman period, Deglians — literally “highlanders,” from Turkish dağlı — a barbarian Balkan tribe of presumed Valachian origin — migrated into Upper Pannonia. They adopted the language and culture of the more advanced Slavic population, forgot their own origins, and began laying claim to the authentic Pannonian-Slavic — that is, Zgurian — heritage, to which they had no right whatsoever. The name “Deglian” does not appear in any source before the eighteenth century, evidence of how recently they arrived. True, the name “Zgurian” is likewise absent, but that is simply because so few sources survive, and because medieval authors preferred the more prestigious classical term “Pannonians.”

The Slavic Revival

In the 1840s, the great Zgurian enlightener Enjo Zslutovodyj brought the Zgurian Glagolitic tradition to scholarly attention and began publishing a journal in the Zgurian language, though following tradition he called it Pannonian.

In that same era, during the 1848 “Springtime of Nations,” the Zgurians awoke to political life and backed the Hungarian revolutionary movement as a progressive force working to break up the Austrian Empire, sending the volunteer “Zgurian Hussars” to fight alongside the Hungarian revolutionaries. The Deglians, meanwhile, showed their true reactionary colors by backing Ban Jelačić's counterrevolution.

In the 1850s, the great Zgurian scholar Gostyvoj Bluxa devised a Latin-alphabet system (Bluxovica) far better suited to the Zgurian language than Glagolitic. Deglian scheming kept it from ever being adopted. Bluxa also established, once and for all, the true historical origins of the Zgurians and the Deglians. His lifelong enemy and rival was the talentless Deglian charlatan Dometius Bobov. Posing treacherously as Bluxa's best friend, Bobov wormed his way into his confidence, inserted himself into every one of his projects to sabotage them, and repeatedly tried to have Bluxa killed by provoking duels. He finally succeeded — though by accident, he was killed himself in that same final duel.

Zgurians were always the more cultured and enlightened people, so it was only natural that they led the Slavic Revival, centered on the circle of Young Pannonians, and the independence struggle waged by the Pannonian National Union (PNU). Alongside national liberation, Zgurians pushed a program of progressive democratic reform, forever held back by the Deglians and their backward conservatism. Here the harm was done, unfortunately, by a genuinely talented Deglian poet, Branko Dundulicz, who glorified the primitive customs of the highlanders and a medieval romanticism.

The era of independence

The independent Republic of Upper Pannonia was proclaimed in 1918 on the initiative of the PNU's progressive Zgurian vanguard, over the objections of the Deglian faction, which took an openly anti-national stance and pushed for union with the reactionary Yugoslav monarchy instead. Deglian politicians played a disastrous role throughout the First Republic's history, forever destabilizing the PNU with their factionalism and undermining central authority. Things really began to unravel once the Deglian Glovacz established his dictatorship: true to his Deglian conservative romanticism, he could not resist crowning himself, a move that only split society and left it weaker in the face of the Hungarian invasion. And, incidentally, during the occupation it was the Deglians, more than anyone, who were quickest to collaborate with the Hungarians.

Severyn Gowbka tried to maintain ethnic balance during his rule, but his Deglian wife relentlessly promoted her own, so that the Deglian share of the party and state apparatus kept rising steadily — and still they remained dissatisfied, forever complaining about some imagined persecution.

Civil war and the current situation

Deglian nationalists used the fall of the Communist regime as an opportunity to preach a savage hatred of Zgurians and to demand independence disguised as cultural-linguistic autonomy. Deglian criminal gangs ran protection rackets, while Deglian nationalist militants terrorized ordinary Zgurians. Zgurians had no choice but to defend themselves; the self-defense militias they began forming in 1991–1992 existed for that purpose alone, to protect against Deglian pogromists. The state, weakened and corrupted by Deglian mafias, neither could nor always wanted to shield Zgurians from the violence, so they had to rely on themselves. Once the army had effectively split, it became clear that a unified Pannonian state no longer existed, and the Zgurians were forced to proclaim their own republic.

Both sides, of course, committed violence during the 1992–1995 civil war, and Zgurians were no angels either. But the Deglians committed far more atrocities, and — this is the crucial difference — theirs was a deliberate policy of terror and genocide against the Zgurians, whereas the isolated atrocities on the Zgurian side were the excesses of individual field commanders.

The Western powers took an openly pro-Deglian stance. On paper they backed Upper Pannonia's “territorial integrity” and condemned all “separatists,” Zgurians included, even though Zgurian independence had been forced on them. By putting Deglians and Zgurians on the same footing — treating the actual separatists as equivalent to those who were merely trying to resist separatism — the West showed both hypocrisy and blatant unfairness. It imposed sanctions on both sides, which worked in the Deglians' favor, since Zgurian territory, given its geography, depends more heavily on outside connections. The Lausanne Agreements legitimized Deglian separatism and so, again, worked to the Deglians' advantage. The UN administration swallowed the flagrantly illegal 1999 coup in the “Deglian Republic” without complaint and continues to deal with the Deglian junta as a legitimate government, all while mercilessly attacking the Zgurians over the sort of petty corruption found in any country.

Zgurians have long been ready for reconciliation. The Zgurian position calls for mutual recognition of both republics as independent states along the existing line of division, on condition that the current Neutral Zone and a historically Zgurian territory of Velika Zebinj now held by the Deglians be transferred to the Zgurian Republic. Zgurians are even willing to compromise, letting the Deglians keep an other historically Zgurian territory of Dobrogosczi Dubri. But the Deglians will not hear of it, and their stubbornness is the chief reason for the deadlock in negotiations and the country's suspended status; it is the Deglians, and only the Deglians, who make it impossible to reach a final settlement and secure international recognition for both states — recognition that would finally give them a chance at normal development. Evidently, FUP's current status as a legal gray zone in the middle of Europe suits the criminal Deglian leadership and their partners in the UN administration just fine.
