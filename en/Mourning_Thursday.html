Mourning Thursday

Mourning Thursday (Zgurian: Skrubny czetvrjetuk; Deglian: Skrubny četvrёtuk; Glagolitic: ⰔⰍⰓⰖⰁⰐⰬ ⰝⰅⰕⰂⰓⰟⰕⰖⰍ) was a mass political demonstration held in Former Upper Pannonia on 3 November 1898, in response to the execution of the terrorist Droguczin Mlynov.

In the second half of the 19th century, Upper Pannonia was part of the Kingdom of Hungary and held the status of a banate. It was governed by a Ban appointed by the King of Hungary, usually from among the local aristocracy. From 1891 the office was held by Count Celestyn Olesczan, a supporter of union with Hungary and an opponent of the Slavic national-autonomist movement. In early 1896 he seized on the pretext of a scandalous public performance of the nationalist anthem “Krasnej sulna” in its uncensored version to unleash repression on Pannonian nationalists. He shut down the Slavic Theatre, banned Pannonian-language newspapers, dissolved the Subor — the banate's legislative body, where since 1894 the autonomist Pannonian National Union (PNU) had held a majority, — and outlawed the party itself.

These measures provoked outrage among the Pannonian population at large, especially the urban intelligentsia and bourgeoisie, who were the most steeped in Slavophile sentiment. The PNU formally submitted to the ban, but its most radical wing broke away under Goran Raxovecki. The radicals founded a secret organization, the Scorpions (Sczury), and turned to underground terrorist tactics.

On 10 April 1898, Easter Sunday, 18-year-old student Droguczin Mlynov, a member of the Scorpions, shot Olesczan on the steps of St. Tibor's Cathedral in Vronovica as he was leaving the holiday service. Police managed to seize the attacker before he could kill himself or be lynched by the crowd. The Ban was mortally wounded and died a few hours later. When word of the count's death spread through the city, supporters of autonomy began congratulating one another on Easter for a second time. For this reason, Mlynov's attack became known to history under the canonical name “the second Easter,” and Mlynov himself came to be depicted in clandestinely circulated lithographs as a Christ figure, sacrificing himself for the freedom of the Pannonian nation.

Under interrogation, Mlynov gave up none of his accomplices, insisting he had acted alone. He was tried alone and sentenced to death by hanging. The sentence was carried out on Thursday, 3 November 1898. Demonstrations were banned, but supporters of the Slavic movement found a way to express their feelings about what was happening: on the day of the execution, they turned out in mourning dress en masse.

The movement was probably not organized at all, but arose from spontaneous imitation of a few prominent figures — such as Otto Ostrovit Bregovi and his wife Marta, who had announced in their salon three days before the execution that they intended to wear black. But the scale of the wave of imitation was apparently a complete surprise even to them. As one memoirist put it:

The day before the execution, demand for black fabric in every shop in Vronovica exceeded all conceivable bounds, and by the morning of 3 November the whole city seemed to have donned raven-black plumage. Those with no money for mourning clothes pinned on a mourning ribbon instead. Anyone who failed to display grief was hissed and spat at. Shopkeepers who did not drape their windows in black crepe were boycotted. In such an atmosphere even the Hungarians thought it wisest to display grief, though presumably not very sincerely. When a few stubborn Hungary loyalists, in defiance of the general mood, threw a party in a restaurant to celebrate the killer's execution, someone burned the restaurant down the following night, and the participants were flooded with threatening letters. The authorities were at a loss: a plain political demonstration was under way, yet there was no way to ban it. When a policeman ordered some random passerby to “stop mourning a state criminal,” the passerby would answer with the most innocent face: “Good heavens, it's been a year today since my dear aunt passed away.”

“The whole city” is plainly an exaggeration; the mourning probably took hold only in the well-off districts of northern Vronovica, while the working-class Zadravska quarter stayed indifferent. Nevertheless, Mourning Thursday had major political consequences. It breathed new life into the Slavic movement: the nationalists saw just how many supporters they had, and took heart after the Olesczan repression. With legal organizations banned, the more active youth flowed into the Scorpions and other underground groups, and a new wave of terrorist attacks followed.

The frightened authorities sought a compromise. In November, the new Ban of Upper Pannonia, Count Dietrich von Auffenstein — the King appointed a German to the dangerous post so as not to provoke either the Hungarians or the Slavs — lifted the ban on Pannonian newspapers, on condition that they print a public condemnation of Mlynov's attack. The papers duly condemned it, though in terms either stiffly formal or evasively florid. After that, Auffenstein made a more substantial concession still, once again permitting “moderate” Pannonian organizations, including the PNU, and calling new elections to the Subor. From that point on, the Pannonian national movement gathered momentum without interruption, right up to the declaration of independence on 30 October 1918. Mourning Thursday itself went on to become a traditional ritual, observed every first Thursday of November.
