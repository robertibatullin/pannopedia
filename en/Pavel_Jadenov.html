Pavel Jadenov

Pavel Jadenov (Zgurian and Deglian: Pavel Jadenov, Glagolitic: ⰒⰀⰂⰅⰎ ⰡⰄⰅⰐⰑⰂ; 1920–1996) was an officer of the Ministry of People's Security (Naprava ob oxrone naroda, NON) of the People's Republic of Upper Pannonia. As head of its Economic Intelligence Directorate, he played a prominent part in preparing the post-socialist market reforms.

Origins

On his voyage around the world in 1884–1887, the Pannonian aristocrat and traveler Adalfrid Attila Bregovi acquired several “living specimens of primitive races” for his human zoo at Gnilovody. Among them were two Melanesian teenagers from the Solomon Islands, a boy named Jaden and a girl named Tegu. Most of the “specimens” died during the voyage or soon after reaching Pannonia, but Jaden and Tegu survived and became a couple. They lived at Gnilovody as zoo exhibits, in a traditional hut, farming in their accustomed way as far as the local microclimate allowed. Winters, however, left them with nothing to do, and they keenly missed the extended family and clan of their homeland. Both soon took to drink and died young.

Of their children, only a daughter, Karolyna Jadenova (1895–1944), survived. She was taken in at an early age by Marta, wife of Otto Ostrovit Bregovi, Adalfrid Attila's son and heir. Karolyna became Marta's trusted and devoted maid and stayed at her side all her life. Marta took a personal interest in her future and even tried to find her a husband; a surviving letter from Marta to her husband complains how hard it was to find a suitable match for Karolyna, whose unusual looks many found off-putting. Karolyna never married, but in 1920 she had an affair with a Senegalese rifleman attached to the French military mission and bore him a son, Pavel.

Childhood and youth

Like his mother, Pavel Jadenov grew up in the Bregovi household as a ward and future servant. In 1938 he was conscripted into the Royal Army. Near the end of his service, in autumn 1940, his regiment fought in the brief campaign against the Hungarian invasion (see King Drogan). After demobilization Pavel returned to the Bregovi household, where Otto Ostrovit hired him as a bodyguard.

His official biography claims that during the occupation Jadenov “fought in the Resistance under a false name” — the latter hardly plausible for the only black man in the country. Be that as it may, in 1944 Pavel and his mother were arrested, precisely on charges of “participation in the Resistance”, and sent to labor camps. Karolyna died there; Pavel survived until the People's Liberation Army freed the camp in autumn 1944. Soon afterwards he was drafted into the People's Liberation Army, and on the personal orders of Szamenicki he was transferred from there to the NON's “special detachments”.

Early years in the NON

As a member of a special detachment, Jadenov fought the “fascist bandits” — the guerrilla groups of the monarchist Resistance — and took part in the notorious burning of the village of Kreszevecka Rava in August 1946. He was then transferred to the Vronovica Investigations Directorate, where he worked on many political cases between 1947 and 1953. He had no professional training, but his menacing appearance and rough manners made him a natural for the bad cop in a good cop/bad cop routine. In its early years the Gowbka regime was relatively mild and preferred psychological pressure to physical. As the repressions grew in scale, however, there was less and less time for mind games: confessions had to be beaten out of victims quickly and without ceremony. Jadenov excelled at this too, earning a reputation as one of the NON's most feared interrogators and the nickname “the Black Devil”.

In the first postwar years the NON was staffed largely by Soviet specialists from the NKVD and SMERSH, who had also done much to build it. The fall of Beria in Moscow in 1953 gave Szamenicki a pretext for a sweeping purge of “Beria's men”, and with it a chance to rid himself of his Soviet minders. The purge left many vacancies to be filled with loyal people. In this shake-up Pavel Jadenov, with his clean record, was promoted to head of the Economic Intelligence Directorate, a department mainly engaged in technological espionage.

Connections

Having grown up in the household of a prominent aristocrat, Pavel knew many members of pre-communist Upper Pannonia's highest elite. To them he had been nothing more than an exotic servant, but the change of regime reversed the roles. Otto Ostrovit Bregovi emigrated with his family in 1944 and died in 1946; Marta followed him soon after. Their son Svjetovit, a sculptor and the new head of the house, asked the communist authorities for permission to return home, renouncing his princely title. Pavel personally vouched for his loyalty to Szamenicki, and permission was granted. Svjetovit Bregovi went on to become an eminent official sculptor of the People's Republic and president of the PRUP Academy of Arts.

In 1949 Jadenov married another aristocrat, Dora Olesczan (1925–1989), daughter of Count Justus Olesczan. The family had traditionally been pro-Hungarian, and paid dearly for it: Count Justus was shot in 1946, and his wife Irena was sent to a camp, where she died in 1948. Their two elder sons managed to emigrate, but Dora was left behind in Pannonia, an orphan with no means of support and the dangerous status of a daughter of enemies of the people. On her side the marriage was probably one of necessity, yet it proved lasting. The couple stayed together for the rest of their lives, and acquaintances credited Dora with giving Pavel, in time, the polish and manners of a cultured man, though he never received a higher education.

Economic Intelligence Directorate

Jadenov's personal ties to the old aristocracy, including its émigré branch, served him well in his new post. Economic intelligence called for a network of agents in Western business, and Jadenov had the right connections and used them skillfully. In 1961 a Center for the Study of Contemporary Capitalism was set up under his supervision at the Institute of Socialist Economy. The center supplied the Economic Intelligence Directorate (EID) with analysis and sent scholars to conferences abroad. Many of them worked directly for the EID and used these conferences to recruit Western experts and businessmen as agents.

The EID was effective, supplying Pannonia's growing industry with many important technologies; the country's industrial and construction boom of the 1950s and 1960s owed it a great deal. The Center for the Study of Contemporary Capitalism, for its part, brought together the strongest economists in the country, so it is hardly surprising that they were the first to notice the warning signs. After Vaclav Bluxa took over in 1974, the center became the main think tank of market-minded economists who called for loosening the planned economy along Hungarian and Yugoslav lines. In confidential memos to the EID, it warned of an approaching crisis and argued that reform was necessary. Jadenov passed these findings up the chain in toned-down form, but Gowbka refused to hear of any revision of the foundations of central planning.

Later years

In 1990, after the fall of the communist regime, the EID was dissolved as part of a drastic downsizing of the NON, and the 70-year-old Jadenov retired. Around the same time, revelations appeared in the press about his unsavory role in the events of 1946–1953. Jadenov flatly denied torturing detainees, while at the same time pleading that “times were harsh, and the survival of the state was at stake”. No investigation followed and he was never punished. Instead he remained a respected figure and advised many of the fledgling private companies of the privatization era. In 1991 he moved to Switzerland, where he lived in an exclusive retirement home. He took no part in the civil war, made no comment on it, and kept no contact with Pannonian politicians of the period. He died in 1996.

Pavel and Dora's only child, a daughter named Hanna, was born in 1950. She trained as an actress, but her unusually dark skin kept her from leading roles on the traditional Pannonian stage of the day. In 1969, while still a student, she married Berislav Zslutovodski, a student of stage directing. He went on to make his name as an experimental director, founding his own theater studio and staging avant-garde productions in which an actor's skin color did not matter — and there Hanna could shine in leading roles. As of 2026, the couple jointly run the Municipal Theater in the Zgurian zone of Vronovica.
