Krasnej sulna

Krasnej sulna ("Fairer than the Sun") was the former state anthem of Former Upper Pannonia during the First Republic (1918–1934), the First Kingdom (1934–1940), and the Third Republic (1990–1992). In other periods it held no official status and was usually banned outright, yet it was still regarded as the national anthem of the Pannonian people and one of their principal national symbols. The text survives in two versions: the original and a toned-down one.

Text

The original text was written in 1849 by Branko Dundulicz, then a young man with no reputation to speak of, under the impression of the Hungarian Revolution. It consists of a single quatrain:

Krasnej sulna kraj rodyny,
Mroci bjely, rjeci siny!
Domoljubcze! Djej ugody,
Bjej madjarsku neporodu!

A literal translation:

Fairer than the sun, my native land,
White clouds, blue rivers!
Patriot! Do good,
Kill the Hungarian scum!

Dundulicz showed the poem to Enjo Zslutovodyj, who was overjoyed:

True genius, I find, lies in capturing in four lines the whole deep trembling of the national soul, all the sacred pain of a heart torn apart yet still burning with love for Pannonia. Not a word could be taken away, not a letter added. Let this day be forever inscribed on the tablets of history: we now have a national poet of our own!

Zslutovodyj published the quatrain in the March issue of The Slavic Awakening, though with an eye to the censor: in toned-down form, replacing "Hungarian" with "the enemy's" (vrozsinu). For nearly half a century, it was only in this form that Dundulicz's poem was printed and publicly performed; the original text, however, was well known within Slavic Revival circles, passed by word of mouth as both a password of belonging and a token of trust. It was set to music in 1854 by Josias Grottner, kapellmeister of the Ban's court orchestra, who achieved no other notable distinction.

The 1895 performance

In 1894, Slavic autonomists from the Pannonian National Union (PNU) won elections to the Subor, the legislative body of the Upper Pannonian Banate, for the first time. Although the Subor's powers were minimal, the victory sharply revived Slavophile sentiment. At the Christmas concert on 23 December 1895 at the Slavic Theatre, a choir led by Young Pannonian Vilmosz Komarovicki performed "Krasnej sulna" in its original version for the first time. The move caught the audience completely by surprise. Everyone knew the words, but no one expected anyone to spit so brazenly in the face of censorship. After a moment's shock, the audience erupted. One memoirist wrote:

This theatre, this sleepy town, had never known such frenzied rapture, such a roar of all-consuming ovation. Little compares to the ecstasy of a crowd that has felt itself liberated, that has understood that now they could. I do not exaggerate in the slightest when I say that this was truly a turning point in the history of Pannonian national self-consciousness.

The audience called the choir and Komarovicki back for an encore, but they hurried out of the theatre to avoid arrest. The authorities responded harshly: the theatre was shut down by the city the next day, and before long Count Olesczan, the pro-Hungarian Ban of Upper Pannonia, dissolved the Subor and launched a crackdown on the Slavic movement. "Krasnej sulna" was banned even in its toned-down form. But the Christmas concert firmly cemented its status as the people's anthem and a symbol of the national liberation struggle.

State Anthem

After the collapse of Austria-Hungary and the proclamation of Upper Pannonia's independence, one of the new government's first decrees adopted "Krasnej sulna," in its original version, as the state anthem. Hungarian protests, naturally, only reinforced the Pannonians' conviction that everything was being done right. Drogan Glovacz's coup in 1931 and his coronation in 1934 changed nothing, although Glovacz himself disliked the anthem for its "democratic" character and its total silence on the authority of the state.

After the Hungarian occupation of September 1940, the anthem was, naturally, banned again. After the country's liberation in 1944 and the establishment of Communist rule, the ban was not lifted: the anthem was now seen as reactionary and bourgeois-nationalist, and as needlessly irritating to comrades in the Hungarian People's Republic. When the People's Republic of Upper Pannonia was established, it was given a new, ideologically correct anthem. After the fall of the Communist regime, the new authorities restored "Krasnej sulna," but with the toned-down text. It did not last long. With the Republic of Upper Pannonia's de facto collapse in 1992, the anthem stopped being performed, and with the country's formal dissolution in 1995 it lost even its official status. The de facto successor states — the Deglian and Zgurian Republics — use their own anthems, written from scratch during the civil war.
