Opisthonomics

The Opisthonomics (Ancient Greek Ὀπισθονομικοί, from ὄπῐσθεν “backward, back” and νόμος “law”) were followers of Aristeas of Chalcidice, a Byzantine theologian of the late 6th and early 7th centuries. His teaching was condemned as heresy by the local Council of Thessalonica in 609 and is known only from the council's decrees; none of his own writings survive.

Aristeas was an ordinary parish priest on the Chalcidice peninsula. He held that the Book of Revelation was not the work of the apostle John, taught dubious doctrine, and should be dropped from the New Testament. In its place he proposed an eschatology of his own: the events of biblical sacred history would repeat in reverse order. The Creation, the Flood, the Exodus, the coming of Christ, and the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem would be mirrored by the rebuilding of the Temple, the coming of the Antichrist, the return of the Jews to Egypt, a new Flood, and the end of the world. Aristeas also altered the liturgy at will. At baptisms, for instance, he held people under water so long that many began to choke.

The council anathematized his teaching and exiled Aristeas to Illyricum, where imperial rule was then collapsing under the pressure of the Avars and Slavs. Neither he nor the Opisthonomics are mentioned again. However, late-18th-century entries in the Klykov Paschalion reproduce his eschatology exactly, which suggests that the teaching reached the monastic community of Klykov monastery, took root there, and survived for more than a thousand years.
