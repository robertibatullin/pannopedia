Branko Dundulicz

Branko Dundulicz (Zgurian; Deglian: Branko Dundulič; Glagolitic: ⰁⰓⰀⰐⰍⰑ ⰄⰖⰐⰄⰖⰎⰊⰝ; c. 1830 – after 1887) was an Upper Pannonian poet, universally regarded as the greatest national poet and the creator of the literary Pannonian language and its poetics.

Biography

Dundulicz's biography is a mystery. No one ever met him in person; he communicated with the world exclusively through letters, addressed to a poste restante box at Vronovica's central post office. A deaf-mute servant on a black horse delivered and collected the correspondence, proving his identity with a broken seal — one half in his own keeping, the other held at the post office, a common method of identity verification at the time. The servant's name was never learned. Anyone who tried to follow him home was met with a drawn weapon, and more than one such attempt proved the threat was not idle.

Dundulicz was never found, so everything known about him comes from his own letters. By his account, he was ethnically Croatian, descended from Grenzer who had settled on the Pannonian military frontier in the early eighteenth century. His ancestors, he claimed, had served in a regiment of Deglian Haiduks, and he considered himself a Deglian. He explained his secretive life in romantic, Rousseauian terms: he lived alone in the Nexozsi Vrjexi, wandering the forests and living off the hunt, avoiding cities and their crowds and bustle so as not to trouble his soul or muddy the wellspring of inspiration that flowed from sublime contemplation of Mother Nature. He called himself the last of his line, denied having any living relatives, and flatly refused every request for a personal meeting. After years of fans pleading for a portrait, he finally sent, in 1877, a crude pencil sketch — the only known likeness of him, and the basis for countless portraits since imagined by generations of artists.

Works

Dundulicz's first publication, in March 1849, was the quatrain "Krasnej sulna" — later the state anthem of the First Republic — which made him famous within the narrow circle of Slavophile Pannonian intellectuals. He would later speak of it dismissively, as a youthful exercise. Through the 1850s and 1860s he regularly published poems, ballads, and longer verse narratives in The Slavic Awakening and, later, other Pannonian-language periodicals. His poems were popular enough that readers bought these publications largely for their sake; many admitted they had learned to read the Glagolitic script for the sole purpose of reading Dundulicz. His verses were set to music and performed in domestic salons, and eventually on the professional stage as well.

His best-known poems from this period include the elegy On the Ruins of Drozsin Castle (1854), with its oft-quoted line "our land is full of dark secrets," and the programmatic sonnet The Captive Mouflon (1866), an allegory for the fate of the heroic Pannonian people under the Hungarian yoke. After this poem was published, the Dinaric mouflon became a national symbol of Upper Pannonia, and it still appears on the coat of arms and flag of the Deglian Republic today. Recurring themes across his work were the beauty of untamed nature, the noble simplicity of Deglian highlanders, and a heroic past.

Dundulicz's talent did not fade in the 1870s, but his popularity began to wane: his poems grew stale, came to seem old-fashioned, and his whole persona as nature's romantic son took on a faintly comic, dated air. The Pannonian national movement was drifting toward a more progressivist, liberal-reformist outlook, and Dundulicz no longer fit the mold. He began to be parodied. The poet took it badly.

In 1883 he published the poem Branko the Deglian, the first overtly polemical work of his career. Its hero, an obvious authorial alter ego — a noble highlander and son of untouched nature — is set against the vain, hollow townsfolk of the Zgurians. Politically, the poem read as a conservative manifesto: it glorified tradition, denounced progress, and warned against reckless reform and the breaking of old ways. The forest hermit had struck precisely the nerve of his era's controversies, and the poem became the subject of heated debate in the Pannonian press. Censorship forbade open political discussion, so conflicts between political positions took the form of literary quarrels instead. It was in this very controversy that the Zguro-Deglian split first surfaced openly and took on ideological shape: Dundulicz's supporters proudly claimed the name Deglian, his opponents just as readily identified as Zgurian — both sides with little regard for their actual ethnic origins.

Dundulicz himself declined to engage with the debate he had started, and did not even respond to a Young Pannonian Sandor Ljux's comedy Dumbness Is Not A Virtue (1886), which parodied and mocked both his poem and himself more mercilessly than ever before. He published less and less, and only old-fashioned nature lyrics by that point. Some connoisseurs consider the poems of this period his finest. After 1887 his correspondence stopped, and he is presumed to have died that year.

Theories about his identity

In Pannonian literary scholarship, the Dundulicz question occupies the same place as the Shakespeare authorship question in English studies. The mystery of his identity has kept many scholars up at night, and the official story of the forest hermit satisfies almost no one. It is widely assumed that "Branko Dundulicz" is a pseudonym, and that the Croatian origin was invented specifically so that he could not be linked to any Pannonian family.

Even during Dundulicz's lifetime, many believed that he himself was the supposedly deaf-mute servant through whom the correspondence was conducted. Sandor Ljux was a proponent of this theory, and in 1880 he attempted to unmask the servant. He wrote:

Tired of his pretending, I shouted after him: "Tell your master, my good man, that he has written himself out entirely, and that in his latest ballad only four lines are any good — and those are stolen!" At that, this supposed deaf-mute turned in his saddle, and his eyes flashed with such fury that it erased my last shred of doubt.

But the servant's identity is likewise unknown, leaving the question open. Judging from his poems and letters, Dundulicz was an educated man, versed in the classical languages as well as Church Slavonic. On this basis, some believe he was a priest, forced to conceal his literary activity as supposedly incompatible with holy orders. Historians have proposed several candidates — parish priests from mountain districts whose lifespans fit — but reliable evidence for any of them is lacking.

One of the most popular theories identifies Dundulicz with Baron Peter Zorza (1828–1887). The dates fit perfectly. As a young man, Peter, the second son of the head of House Zorza, was preparing for the priesthood; he studied at the Academy of Sciences, moved in the same circles as the future fathers of the Slavic Revival, and shared their interests. After the untimely death of his elder brother Andrasz, he became heir and later head of the house, and spent nearly the rest of his life confined to the family's Zorzsin castle in the Nexozsi Vrjexi. Literary and other intellectual pursuits were discouraged in House Zorza, giving Peter good reason to hide his writing. However, samples of Peter's own handwriting have survived, and they do not match.

Another, even more romantic theory credits Dundulicz's poems to Ilona Esterhazy (1830–1888), born Bregovi, a student of Enjo Zslutovodyj and the addressee of his love poetry. The dates fit, and her closeness to the early Slavic Revival literary circle is well documented, but Ilona lived in Hungary and never once visited the mountains of Upper Pannonia, which Dundulicz spent his life praising and clearly knew intimately.
