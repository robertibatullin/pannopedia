Ervin Zsladov

Ervin Mixalov Zsladov (Zgurian: Ervin Mixalov Zsladov, Deglian: Ervin Mixalov Žladov, Glagolitic: ⰅⰓⰂⰊⰐ ⰏⰊⰢⰀⰎⰑⰂ ⰆⰎⰀⰄⰑⰂ; 1931–1999) was an Upper Pannonian politician and the last president of the Republic of Upper Pannonia before its collapse and civil war.

Early career

Zsladov was born in 1931 to a Zgurian working-class family in Novokopy. Part of his schooling fell under the Hungarian occupation (1940–1944). His official biography credits him with “taking part in the Resistance”, though by the standard of official biographies, practically the entire population of the country took part in it. At any rate, he welcomed the Red Army's liberation of Pannonia and soon joined the Young Fighters' League (Mlodoborska liga, ML), the youth wing of the Communist Party of Upper Pannonia (CPUP). During his military service (1949–1951) he made a name for himself as an ML activist and caught the eye of the party leadership. After demobilization he was admitted to the CPUP and given a full-time party job. He proved a capable, diligent, and conscientious functionary, ready to carry out any order from above without asking awkward questions. This served his career well. In 1957 he was transferred to the capital and joined the CPUP City Committee's Directorate for Mass Events, where he recruited extras for street propaganda pageants with hundreds of participants. In November 1964, during celebrations of the twentieth anniversary of liberation, he managed to please the Supreme Leader himself, President Severin Gowbka, by lining up young beauties from a gymnastics team in front of his reviewing stand. Gowbka, a great admirer of female beauty, noted the quality of the personnel work, and Zsladov was promoted to the Sixth Directorate of the Presidential Office, which specialized in precisely this line of work.

U6

Officially, the Sixth Directorate (Szesta uprava, or U6) was responsible for “recruiting temporary personnel to perform specific tasks in support of the needs of state bodies.” In practice, it supplied Gowbka with occasional mistresses — several dozen a year — drawn from ML activists, servicewomen of the NOR, sportswomen, and the like. Its recruiters, Zsladov among them at first, toured army units and sports clubs, where personal files recorded each woman's physical measurements. They went through the files, picked suitable candidates, called them in for confidential talks, and told them something along these lines: “You should understand that Comrade Gowbka, for all his superhuman talents, is still a living man of flesh and blood. Tirelessly giving all his strength, sparing nothing in the service of the people and the party, he does occasionally need simple human rest. Would you, Comrade, be willing, on a strictly voluntary basis, to take part in measures to provide for the Supreme Leader's leisure?”

Many years later, after the fall of the Communist regime, an interviewer asked Zsladov whether coercion had ever been used. He only laughed. Almost everyone agreed gladly, he said, and the few who refused were not punished in any way. Those who accepted were sent to Zeleny dvor (Green Court), Gowbka's country residence. There they were housed in a dormitory, where a staff female instructor prepared them and taught them the necessary skills. On paper, this was a business trip “at the disposal (rozporjud) of the Presidential Office” — hence the bureaucratic slang for these women, rozporjuzsi (disposal women). Once the “business trip” lasting up so several weeks were over, they were rewarded with coveted goods unobtainable in ordinary shops.

In this delicate post, Zsladov showed the same diligence, the same eye for personnel, and the necessary lack of scruples. A scandal broke in 1968, when one of the rozporjuzsi complained to Gowbka that the heads of U6 were abusing their position and freely helping themselves to what was meant for the Supreme Leader alone. Enraged, Gowbka dismissed the directorate's entire leadership and sent them to the camps. Zsladov, who had done his job honestly and never abused his position, was appointed the new head. He thus gained an office at the Green Court and a place in the leader's innermost circle, if in a dubious and disreputable capacity. He held this risky and tempting post faithfully for ten years; none of his predecessors had lasted more than two.

In parliament

As Gowbka aged, his needs naturally diminished. In March 1978 — by an odd coincidence, a few days before the suicide of his son Boris — the president abolished the Sixth Directorate by decree and appointed Zsladov deputy chairman of the Legislative Assembly, the country's parliament. This steep rise surprised many and gave birth to the wildest theories. In the smoking rooms of the Presidential Office and the Legislative Assembly, people whispered that Zsladov had found Gowbka one of the rozporjuzsi with near-miraculous gifts, who had supposedly restored the Leader's youth and nearly given him a heart attack. Whatever the truth of this, the strange appointment may have had other motives. For many years the Legislative Assembly had been chaired by Miljana Gowbka, the Supreme Leader's wife. She knew about the Sixth Directorate and tolerated it, but such a deputy could hardly have been to her liking. The appointment was an open humiliation for Miljana and probably reflected Gowbka's unease at her growing authority and influence.

As deputy chairman, Zsladov dealt with purely technical matters. The post carried no political weight, and the parliament itself was politically powerless: its role was to pass unanimously the laws drafted in the real centers of power — the party's Central Committee, the Presidential Office, and the Ministry of People's Security (NON). Everyone understood the rules of the game. But one day the game came to an end.

Head of state

Gowbka died suddenly on 10 November 1989, amid a deepening economic crisis, mass discontent, and the collapse of socialist regimes in neighboring countries — according to legend, a few hours after being told of the fall of the Berlin Wall. His death was kept secret for three days. His widow Miljana and her relatives fled to Libya. To his own utter astonishment, Zsladov automatically became chairman of the Legislative Assembly and thus, under the 1979 Constitution, acting head of state in the absence of a president.

Zsladov had never been known for willpower, initiative, or adaptability — none of the qualities a head of state needs, least of all in a time of severe crisis. In the turbulent months after Gowbka's death, he simply drifted with the current, carrying out his duties as a formality and avoiding any decision of his own. This suited the Pannonian political elites perfectly, since nobody wanted a new Gowbka. With the CPUP's authority in ruins, the parliament found itself the only legitimate power in the country. Following the spirit of the times, it passed a series of radical measures aimed solely at calming the protesters in the streets: censorship was abolished, a multi-party system and private enterprise were allowed, the army was cut, and Vaclav Bluxa was appointed prime minister with a mandate for decisive market reforms. Zsladov's signature stood under every one of these revolutionary decrees, but his own initiative was minimal: as always, he simply did what was expected of him.

In private and in public alike, Zsladov admitted he could hardly wait for a presidential election that would let him retire. But no one intended to let him go; his weakness suited too many people. In January 1990 the Legislative Assembly extended his presidential powers until an election to be held under a new constitution. That constitution had yet to be adopted by the next Legislative Assembly. Elected in September 1990, the new assembly split into irreconcilable nationalist factions and could not even agree on a government, let alone a constitution. The crisis deepened, and the country was heading straight for civil war between its two ethnic communities. Zsladov stayed on as president, utterly bewildered and powerless to influence events.

In June 1991 he finally resolved to do something. Invoking a rather vague constitutional provision, he issued a decree dissolving the paralyzed parliament. For the first time in many months, the parliament united — to declare the decree illegal and demand the president's resignation. Zsladov probably took this with enormous relief. On 28 June he announced his resignation, acknowledging that central authority in the country had effectively ceased to exist, and left it to parliament to elect a new president or call an election. Parliament never managed to do either. Zsladov retired to his dacha and spent the rest of his life tending his garden, taking no part in the civil war or any of the other upheavals of those years. His dacha settlement lay deep in the Zgurian zone and was untouched by the fighting. The forgotten former president died in 1999.

Family

Zsladov married in 1964, as the Sixth Directorate did not employ bachelors. His marriage had the same indecent flavor as the rest of his service: the heads of U6 offered — or imposed — as a wife for their new recruit one of the rozporjuzsi, who had accidentally become pregnant by Gowbka. The boy, Sandor, born in 1965, was officially Zsladov's son, but his real parentage was hardly a secret to anyone. Gowbka took no interest in the child and played no part in his life. Sandor Zsladov studied at the Institute of Socialist Economics, where he was a student of Vaclav Bluxa and a supporter of his market reforms. In the 1990s he fought in the civil war on the Zgurian side. Afterwards he made a sharp political turn, joined the Zgurian Socialist Party (ZSP), and publicly declared himself Gowbka's son. Today the “son of two presidents”, Sandor Gowbka-Zsladov, remains politically active as a ZSP member of the Zgurian Legislative Assembly and an ardent advocate of restoring socialism and rehabilitating Gowbka. He has run for mayor of Vronovica several times, without success.
