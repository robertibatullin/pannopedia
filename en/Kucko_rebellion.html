Kucko rebellion

The Kucko rebellion (Zgurian and Deglian: Povust Kucka; Glagolitic: ⰒⰑⰂⰖⰔⰕ ⰍⰖⰜⰍⰀ) was a peasant revolt in Upper Pannonia in 1436. It is recorded in a single source, the so-called Olesczan redaction of the Bregovo Episcopal Chronicle: “In the same year [1436] one Kucko, a wandering singer, reviled the Prince-Bishop Djordji in his songs and incited the common folk to withhold their tithes, sowing disobedience in many parishes and stirring up no small unrest. The Prince-Bishop raised an army and chastised the disobedient, stripping many of their goods, enslaving them and selling them to the Saracens. Kucko himself he captured and put to a most cruel death: he had his sinews drawn out and ordered them played upon like the strings of a lyre until he gave up his soul to God. Thus did Prince-Bishop Djordji strike no little fear into the people and silence the boldest tongues.”

The Olesczan redaction was not published until 1772, and its authenticity was questioned from the start. Its publisher, and almost certainly its author, was the priest Emmanuel Olesczan. He was openly hostile to the Bregovis, the descendants of Prince-Bishop Djordji, and never missed a chance to reproduce — or invent — any evidence of their cruelty and misrule. For a long time, the story of Kucko was therefore dismissed as unreliable and drew little attention.

This changed after the Second World War, when the communist regime of Gowbka came to power. No self-respecting socialist state could do without a heroic popular uprising in its past. In 1949 the medievalist Svjetlan Milovanov — son-in-law of Krasimir Bluxa, brother of Upper Pannonia's first prime minister Zdjeslav Bluxa — published an article in the Pannonian Historical Review titled “The Peasant War of National Liberation Led by Kucko”. In his earlier work, Milovanov had rejected the Olesczan redaction as a forgery; now he argued that it was “at least a partly reliable source”. Drawing on a wide range of other sources, none of which actually mentioned Kucko, and on bold conjecture, he inflated the chronicle's brief entry into the full-blown story of a mighty popular movement that shook feudalism to its foundations. The party praised this timely article, and “the peasant war led by Kucko” was promptly written into the historical canon of the People's Republic, with a whole chapter in school textbooks. Milovanov's shaky conjectures acquired the force of established fact, and any doubts about the Olesczan redaction were forbidden.

Kucko became a true cult figure in 1957, when the folklorist Borivoj Zslutovodski published a cycle of epic songs about the rebellion, supposedly collected from the blind singer Alisza Braximov. Zslutovodski almost certainly wrote all of them himself. This did not stop him from claiming that the songs were very old — some even composed by Kucko himself — and that they preserved genuine historical information about the revolt. This discovery, too, came at just the right moment. The Pannonian people badly lacked a national epic, and here was one that was not only national but class-conscious and revolutionary as well. The Kucko Epic immediately entered the school literature curriculum. Braximov, who was illiterate and had a very shaky memory of “his” text, was proclaimed a living classic and admitted to the Writers' Union and the Academy of Sciences. The National Conservatory was named after Kucko, and in front of it rose the monument The Execution of Kucko by the leading national sculptor Svjetovit Bregovi, a distant descendant of Prince-Bishop Djordji. In 1960 Milovanov published a hefty monograph on “the peasant war led by Kucko”, now freely citing the Kucko Epic as a reliable historical source. In 1962 the national classic Dominik Raczev added a two-volume epic novel on the same subject, Strings of Wrath, in which Kucko had grown into a figure of European stature: a Renaissance humanist, a disciple of Jan Hus, and the leader of an international revolutionary and anticlerical movement. All three makers of the legend — Milovanov, Zslutovodski and Raczev — received the Gowbka Prize, and Braximov received it twice.

After 1989 the Kucko rebellion was quickly forgotten. It has been dropped from today's textbooks, the epic and the novel are no longer reprinted, and nearly all historians now regard the Olesczan redaction's account as an 18th-century fabrication.
