People's Liberation Army

The People's Liberation Army (Zgurian: Narodna Oslododja Racz; Deglian: Narodna Oslodođa Rač; Glagolitic: ⰐⰀⰓⰑⰄⰐⰀ ⰑⰔⰎⰑⰄⰑⰌⰀ ⰓⰀⰝ; commonly abbreviated NOR) was the armed force of Upper Pannonia during the Socialist era.

Formation (1942–1956)

The NOR began as the armed wing of the UP National Liberation Committee, the Soviet-sponsored body constituted in 1942 under the nominal chairmanship of Severyn Gowbka, with four NKVD officers attached to it. It was recruited from Pannonian émigrés and former prisoners of war and never grew beyond a reinforced battalion. Training took place at a camp outside Kuybyshev, on the pattern of the other Soviet-sponsored national formations of the war years: the company was the smallest establishment unit, each company had a Soviet instructor attached, and from 1943 a Pannonian political officer as well. That same year the formation received its colors from the Soviet command and was sworn in under a ceremonial borrowed from the Czechoslovak and Polish corps.

In autumn 1944 the NOR entered Upper Pannonia from the Romanian side, behind the advancing Soviet units. Its only genuine engagement came at Rudec, where a retreating Hungarian company held up the advance for a day; the NOR lost some forty men killed out of a strength of little more than six hundred. Vronovica was taken by the Red Army without it. None of this prevented later propaganda from crediting the liberation of the country almost entirely to the NOR, leaving the Red Army a secondary role — and, as the years passed, mentioning it less and less.

With the proclamation of the People's Republic in 1945, the formation — by then some eight hundred strong — began to be built up into a regular army on the Soviet model. Conscription was made universal, rifle divisions were raised to Red Army establishment, and the Vronovica Military Academy was founded to train officers; by 1950 the NOR numbered several tens of thousands. Small arms in the early years were captured stock or Soviet handouts, Mosin rifles and Degtyaryov machine guns, while heavy equipment, from T-34 tanks to artillery, came through Soviet military assistance. A Soviet military mission remained in the country until the mid-1950s.

Until 1948 the army's principal task at home was the destruction of the surviving royalist and nationalist bands, which had fought the Hungarian occupation during the war and refused to accept the new government. The NOR conducted the field operations; prisoners, investigation, and sentencing were left to the Ministry of People's Security (NON). Those three years — fighting an enemy scattered in small groups through forest and mountain, at home in the terrain and sustained by the local population — supplied the model for all later Pannonian military thinking, with the roles reversed.

Ranks were taken over from the pre-war army unchanged: the slavicized grades introduced by the Pannonian Legion outlived both the Legion itself and the Glovacz regime. After Stalin's death in 1953 the ladder gained one further rung at the top, the rank of Generalissimo, reserved for Supreme Leader Gowbka alone.

Reforms (1956–1968)

The cooling of relations with Moscow after 1956 and the turn toward Yugoslavia reached military policy as well. The Soviet mass regular army ceased to be the only available model, and the Yugoslav doctrine of all-people's defence gradually took its place. The first steps toward a militia army followed — known in Pannonian usage as “partisanization.”

The break with Yugoslavia in 1966 left the country without allies and forced a complete revision. The military doctrine of 1967 assumed for the first time that war would have to be fought against Yugoslavia and NATO at once and without Warsaw Pact support. Only one answer was available: an armed population and total partisan war.

The scheme ran as follows. The regular army holds the occupier for a week, exactly as long as the militia needs to mobilize. It is then destroyed, and the militia carries on the war at full strength across the whole territory. Depots of weapons, ammunition, food, and manufactured goods were laid down for this purpose, on the assumption that the economy would not function at all for a year, the entire population having gone over to partisan warfare. Occupation would in the end cost the enemy so much that he would prefer to leave. Losses of up to twenty percent of the population were held to be acceptable.

The break with the Warsaw Pact in 1968 required a further amendment. The Soviet bloc now joined the list of probable occupiers, which meant war against every neighbour without exception. Acceptable losses were raised to thirty percent, and the stockpiling norm from one year to two.

The NOR under the late People's Republic (1969–1989)

The 1968 doctrine shaped the NOR for the next two decades. Conscription applied to both sexes: two years for men, one for women, with deferment for pregnant women and mothers of small children. Men passed automatically into the territorial reserve on discharge; women entered it by choice. A proportion of conscripts of both sexes stayed on as long-service soldiers, and these made up the standing army, whose task was to hold the occupier through the first days of a war while the reserve mobilized.

The territorial reserve was organized by county (ujezd), each unit tied to the nearest base or underground depot of weapons, ammunition, and equipment. Reservists trained annually where they lived, and their units were as often as not commanded by local party officials and NON officers rather than professional soldiers.

Strength and structure

By 1985 some 72,000 people were under arms at any one time: 25,000 long-service soldiers and officers of the regular cadre, and roughly 47,000 conscripts of both sexes. The territorial reserve carried about 520,000 names, roughly one inhabitant in four.

The regular army was organized in five motor rifle brigades, one for each operational axis: Austrian, Hungarian, Romanian, and two Yugoslav ones, Serbian and Croatian. To these were added an artillery brigade, two engineer regiments, a signals regiment, and a training division through which every conscript passed. There were two armored units, independent battalions attached to the Hungarian and Romanian brigades, those being the only axes where the ground was considered fit for armor. No offensive formations were raised at all. A landlocked country had no navy either; river transport craft were attached to ground units.

Territorial defence was built parallel to the regular army and reported directly to the Ministry of People's Defence, bypassing district command. Each of the fourteen counties raised a brigade; each urban or rural district, a company. Company rosters were drawn up by address and place of work, so that neighbours and workmates served in the same platoon — the calculation being that such a unit would hold together after every form of central command had been destroyed.

Armament

The regular army had about 150 tanks: some 110 T-34-85s, delivered under Soviet military assistance and never withdrawn from service, and around 40 Chinese Type 59s bought in the early 1970s. The artillery remained largely post-war Soviet; anti-tank weapons and armored personnel carriers were bought mainly from Romania.

NOR aviation amounted to some fifty combat aircraft: 24 Chinese J-6 fighters, a dozen MiG-21s acquired through Egyptian intermediaries in the mid-1970s, and 12 Romanian IAR-93 ground-attack aircraft bought in 1983. Transport aviation consisted of An-2s, Mi-8 helicopters, and Romanian IAR-316s. All of it flew from the military airfield north of Vronovica — the one that became King Svetoplok International Airport after 1995. Air defence rested on two surface-to-air missile regiments with Chinese HQ-2 systems; HN-5 shoulder-launched missiles were issued down to company level.

Small arms were made at home. The standard weapon was the Model 1971 rifle, an unlicensed copy of the Chinese Type 56, itself a copy of the Soviet AK. It was built at one of the “numbered” plants in Gowbkaslava; by 1989 around 750,000 had been produced, more than the country had people liable for service. The same plant and another of the numbered works turned out cartridges, hand grenades, anti-personnel and anti-tank mines, 60mm and 82mm mortars, and demolition charges filled with explosive from the Gowbkaslava chemical works. In keeping with the doctrine that “the ground must burn under the occupier's feet,” mines and mortars were produced in unimaginable quantities: by the late 1980s more than a million mines lay in the depots.

Territorial units were armed more simply: Model 1971 rifles, Simonov carbines, Degtyaryov machine guns, mortars, rocket launchers, mines, and TNT blocks. They were entitled to no heavy weapons; mortars and rocket launchers stood in for artillery. Nor did they have transport of their own. Mobilization plans assigned each unit particular civilian vehicles, listed by owner and address, private Dravas with their trailers among them — the trailer counting as the platoon's establishment transport.

All of it was held in some 340 underground depots: in the karst cavities of the Nexozsi Vrjexi, in the worked-out coal seams at Starokopy, and in purpose-dug vaults at county and district centers. Under the 1968 norms each depot held two years' supply for independent operation — not only weapons and ammunition but food, fuel, medicines, uniforms, field printing presses, and radio sets.

Disintegration (1989–1991)

An army of this size had been a heavy burden even in good years; by the late 1980s, with the economic crisis deepening, it had become impossible to sustain. Cuts were announced immediately after Gowbka's death, and in November and December 1989 the Legislative Assembly passed a demilitarization package almost unanimously: conscription of women was abolished, men's service was reduced to eighteen months, and the regular cadre and state arms orders were cut several times over.

The fatal error lay not in the reform itself but in one apparently secondary provision. The depots and partisan bases were left with territorial defence instead of being transferred to the regular army's custody. At the same time territorial defence funding was cut to a minimum, and the annual musters that had maintained its discipline and its link to the army were abolished in the same economy drive.

The measures were popular at first. Then the cancellation of state defence orders ruined the large plants and put whole towns out of work, while the reduction of the cadre turned thousands of officers and sergeants with no civilian trade onto the street. The depots, meanwhile, passed into the uncontrolled hands of local authorities. Some began selling off the contents to criminal groups; others used them to arm militias of their own.

What remained of the army quickly lost any sense of what it existed for and split along ethnic lines with the rest of society. Clashes between Zgurians and Deglians in the ranks grew more frequent, and after several scandals the Ministry of Defence introduced separate service for the two communities in early 1991. Zgurian and Deglian units date from this decision. When paramilitary formations began the civil war in 1992, both took the side of their own communities, and the NOR ultimately ceased to exist.
