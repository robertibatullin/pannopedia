Zdjeslav Bluxa

Zdjeslav Gostyvojev Bluxa (Zgurian; Deglian: Zđeslav Gostyvojev Bluxa; Glagolitic: ⰈⰌⰅⰔⰎⰀⰂ ⰃⰑⰔⰕⰬⰂⰑⰟⰂ ⰁⰎⰖⰢⰀ; 1861–1925) was an Upper Pannonian Slavic Revival activist, politician, writer, traveller, inventor, underwater archeology pioneer, fringe scientist, healer, occultist, probably a spy, and the first Prime Minister of the independent Upper Pannonia. The son of the prominent Slavist Gostyvoj Bluxa, he combined, from a young age, an obsession with his own invented "Venetic theory" — that the Slavs had descended to Earth from Venus — with restless practical enterprise, and had a gift for converting one into the other: mystical experience into a healing method, and a healing method into political capital.

Over the course of nearly forty years he was expelled from Italy as an Austrian spy, arrested in Germany as a French one, and investigated in his homeland as a Russian one; he founded a secret cult, a clinical network, a newspaper, and a political committee; he was acquainted with Flammarion, William James, and Masaryk; and in 1918 he became head of the first government of independent Upper Pannonia. Forced out of office in 1924, he set off to find the remains of his invented civilization off the coast of New Guinea and vanished without a trace. A man who spent his life only loosely distinguishing truth from fiction left historians no way to do so in his own case.

Early years (1861–79)

Born in 1861, Zdjeslav and his twin brother Zbyslav were the eldest sons of Gostyvoj Bluxa — a prominent Slavic Revival activist — and Magrita Bregovska, an illegitimate daughter of the Pannonian nobleman Lajosz Leonard Bregovi. As a child he was curious, smart, active, and creative, already showing a talent for deception. When Zdjeslav was five, both brothers fell into the water from a boat; Zbyslav drowned, Zdjeslav survived. The loss left a permanent mark on him — by all accounts, he had been deeply attached to his twin.

Zdjeslav attended a classical gymnasium alongside his father's Slavic Sunday school. In 1877 he enrolled in the Pannonian Pedagogical Institute, which Gostyvoj Bluxa had co-founded with Dometius Bobov that same year. That year also brought the deaths of his father and Bobov — lifelong rivals and collaborators who killed each other in their final duel — leaving Zdjeslav, at sixteen, the head of the family. He took the responsibility seriously and remained devoted to his siblings and their children throughout his life.

Slavic Revival Movement (1879–84)

Two years later, in 1879, Zdjeslav became the informal leader of the Young Pannonians, a circle of Slavic Revival enthusiasts that had coalesced around the Pedagogical Institute. At its productive peak in the 1880s, the circle generated much of what became canonical in Pannonian art and literature, with members including novelists, composers, playwrights, and painters. The wider circle organized cultural events and supported its members' work; the inner circle formed a secret society centered on an cult of the Slavic goddess Mora, impersonated by Zdjeslav's sister Kvjetana. By all accounts, the cult was a pure invention by Zdjeslav Bluxa himself.

Through the Slavic movement Bluxa befriended his maternal uncle Adalfrid Attila, head of the House of Bregovi, a wealthy nobleman and an adventurous traveller who had explored the Malay Archipelago, Africa, and the Near East. Bregovi gave Bluxa access to the family library at Bregovo Castle, including the occult and alchemical manuscripts assembled by Gergej Gabriel — a former head of the house, a Freemason and Rosicrucian of notably mystical inclinations.

The reading fed directly into Bluxa's first publication 1. In 1882 he presented his pseudo-scientific Venetic theory inspired by theosophy, occult doctrines, pan-Slavic nationalism, and astronomy books by Camille Flammarion. The Veneds or Venets, a name for the proto-Slavs attested in ancient sources, were theorized to have been Slavic-speaking, proto-Glagolitic-writing superhumans from Venus. Bluxa inverted the conventional etymology: the goddess Venus was named after the planet, not the reverse — the planet's name being the Venets' own. These cosmic beings had "upbred" and "enlightened" the "subhuman" natives of Earth, built a great prehistoric civilization, and were ultimately destroyed by a flood — reflected in the legends of Atlantis and Vineta, the latter being, in Bluxa's reconstruction, the original form.

Travels (1884–8)

The theory's implications were immediately practical. In 1884, convinced that Venice had been a Venetic settlement, Bluxa attempted underwater photography in the Venetian lagoon. Italian authorities, suspecting Bluxa of being an Austrian agent spying on the Italian navy, arrested and expelled him. In 1884–7 he joined Adalfrid Attila Bregovi's expedition into the Pacific Ocean. Landing on the Ventanat Islands — small islets east of New Guinea — Bluxa declared them an Upper Pannonian possession on the grounds that a supposedly Venetic colony had once stood there.

Bluxa patented an improved underwater camera of his own construction — a device that Jacques Cousteau would assess positively decades later. In the New Guinea expedition, he used it to take photos of what he would present as submerged ruins. The images were blurred and inconclusive, but on returning to Vronovica in 1887 he mounted an exhibition of photographs and purported Venetic artefacts — all, as later investigation confirmed, forgeries.

Immersion theory and practice (1888–94)

Soon after return, in 1888, Bluxa published a philosophical treatise, Immersion as Spiritual Experience 2. The book described his perceptions during deep dives, which he interpreted as mystical visions rather than the hypoxia-induced hallucinations they more plausibly were. He argued that immersion to the edge of death enabled contact with the spiritual world, water being the element bridging the material and the immaterial. From this premise, Bluxa reinterpreted the Christian rite of baptism as well:

Eastern Christianity still preserves an older form of baptism by immersion; the deep pools of ancient baptisteries, and countless texts describing baptism as the death of the old man and the birth of the new, all point the same way: the original sacrament's essence lay in bringing the initiate to the brink of death by drowning. This is exactly what John the Baptist did to Jesus, and exactly what the early Church practiced, until the meaning was lost and an initiation meant to transform the person decayed into a purely symbolic ritual.

From there Bluxa extrapolated further: this experience could be shared collectively; the ancient Venets had deliberately submerged their civilization to attain supernatural powers; they had done the same on Venus before arriving on Earth; and the modern Venetian ruling class — crypto-Slavic in Bluxa's reading — might be engineering the slow sinking of their city with the same aim. Some readers were persuaded; among them, the Young Pannonian composer Komarovicki was moved to write his symphony The Drowned World.

Between 1888 and 1890, Bluxa toured Vienna, Prague, St. Petersburg, Moscow, and Warsaw, giving lectures and mounting exhibitions of his New Guinean "findings". Reception ranged from outright dismissal as a freak or con artist to genuine enthusiasm. He presented his Venetic theories to general audiences; for more select circles, he demonstrated his immersion technique as a path to mystical experience. After returning to Vronovica, he rented from Adalfrid Attila a small sanatorium in the Gnilovody hot spring resort for a symbolic price and made it a "spiritual healing center".

His "healing immersion" method — so-called baphotherapy, combining mineral baths, mild induced hypoxia, and "spiritual healing" — was in essence a radical variant of the hydropathy fashionable in the nineteenth century. It was probably not pure quackery: Bluxa's brother Krasimir, who had medical training, assisted him, and baphotherapy proved fairly effective against what medicine of the time diagnosed as "hysteria". Bluxa became a popular healer and gathered a following of devoted female patients who formed something close to a cult around him.

Emigration (1894–6)

At the same time, Bluxa promoted a pro-Slavic agenda: language rights and greater autonomy for Upper Pannonia within Hungary. He aligned himself with the Pannonian National Union (PNU), recently founded by his brother-in-law Zlotan Czigraj, husband of his sister Kvjetana. In autumn 1894, the PNU stood a good chance of winning a majority in the Subor — Upper Pannonia's legislative body, almost powerless and elected by the five percent of citizens who met its property qualification. A timely scandal broke: the anti-PNU press accused Bluxa of "immoral behavior" toward his female patients.

These accusations were not ungrounded. The "spiritual immersion" technique required the healer and patient to bathe together, nude, to ensure "full and unconstrained bodily contact with the aquatic element". Bluxa denied any closer bodily contact, but had to shut down his clinic and leave the country with Krasimir. The PNU won the election regardless.

In 1895, Bluxa moved to France, where he published translations of his works. In Paris, he met Camille Flammarion and tried to win him over to his theories, without success. Bluxa moved in the circle of the occultist Papus (Gérard Encausse), and discussed hypoxia and hallucination with the physiologist Charles Richet, whose own research into psychical phenomena inclined him to take Bluxa's immersion visions seriously. Bluxa gained a measure of fame thereafter, and opened a baphotherapy clinic near Paris, again with Krasimir as professional medical advisor.

Research and marriage (1897–1901)

In 1897, he received a letter from Vannes — a French city once founded by the Veneti, a Gaulish tribe. An anonymous local wrote:

The Law of the Initiated declares: the profane one who has learned the Mysteries must die, or be received into the Circle. Know that the Wise have decided your fate by a margin of a single vote. You, a profane man who stumbled upon the Mysteries of the Sons of Venus, have been judged worthy of Initiation. Here, in the City of the Veneti, you are awaited.

The letter was sealed with a "Venetic" symbol — one Bluxa had invented himself to decorate his fabricated exhibits. He believed it nonetheless, left the clinic in Krasimir's care, and set off for Vannes. There, he discovered that the letter had been written by a mentally unstable young woman, Julie Mirambeau, who had come to believe her own fantasies, inspired by Bluxa's writings.

Bluxa set out to cure Julie's "hysteria", and the two became involved. While in Vannes, he undertook yet another underwater exploration and tested a new invention he named the echolithoscope, a kind of primitive sonar intended to locate underwater stone constructions. The research was cut short when Julie's brother August, a French army lieutenant, demanded that Bluxa marry her.

Bluxa fled to Germany instead, and in 1899 mounted a new expedition near Danzig in search of Vineta, only to be arrested by German police, who accused him of spying for France on the German navy. He moved on to St. Petersburg, but in 1901 the Mirambeau siblings tracked him down there, and he was finally forced to marry Julie, who had already borne his son Camille in 1899.

In the Russian Empire (1901–5)

In St. Petersburg, Bluxa lectured to Vladimir Bekhterev's clinical circle on baphotherapy and struck up an alliance with the court physician Pyotr Badmaev, who blended Tibetan medicine with fashionable occultism among the capital's elite. The zoologist Nikolai Wagner, a longtime advocate of spiritualism among Russian academics, tested Bluxa's hypoxia-trance technique on volunteers from his own laboratory.

These connections proved useful. In 1903, Bluxa presented his echolithoscope to the Russian Navy's technical committee as a mine-detection device. He later defended himself:

All I wanted was funding to keep developing the echolithoscope, purely for underwater archaeology. The scientific world showed no interest, so I decided to interest the military instead. Who could have known it would go this far?

Tested successfully at Kronstadt in 1904, it was approved by the Navy Staff for the war against Japan. As the only person able to operate it, Bluxa was attached to the fleet as a civilian contractor and sailed with Admiral Rozhestvensky's squadron to the Pacific. His ship was hit at Tsushima; an excellent swimmer, he survived and pulled a sailor from the water, but was taken prisoner by the Japanese.

In Asia and America (1905–9)

In captivity, Bluxa appealed to the Austrian embassy, and Austrian diplomats pressed Japan to hand him over as an Austro-Hungarian subject wanted for questioning about his Russian Navy service. Released into Austrian custody in 1905, he was being escorted home under guard when, in Hong Kong, he somehow hypnotized his guards, robbed them, and slipped aboard the first ship bound for San Francisco.

In the US, Bluxa had already acquired a fan base. Charlotte Winthrop Peabody, a Boston heiress who had come to Gnilovody for "hysteria" treatment before the 1894 scandal, had followed his career ever since and financed the American editions of his books. She now arranged an honorary doctorate for him from the Medical School of Maine at Bowdoin College in Brunswick — a small institution later shut down for its low standards — and funded a new clinic that opened there in 1906, promoting it energetically among Boston society. Bluxa corresponded with the Harvard psychologist William James on the nature of hypoxia-induced visions, and befriended the psychical researcher Hereward Carrington, then preparing to bring the Italian medium Eusapia Palladino to America for testing.

It ended in tragedy: in 1909, one of his patients drowned mid-session during a baphotherapy treatment, and Bluxa had to flee the country. Return home was still barred by the unresolved suspicion of Russian espionage, so he went back instead to St. Petersburg, to a wife and son he had not seen in five years.

Again in Russia (1909–17)

In St. Petersburg he found that Julie had converted to Orthodoxy, joined Rasputin's inner circle of devoted women, and in 1906 had borne another son, Seraphim, by an unknown father. The marriage nonetheless held. Once news of the American scandal reached Europe, Bluxa abandoned medical practice altogether and turned his attention to politics.

In St. Petersburg he also found a growing Pannonian political diaspora. Hungarian authorities had dissolved the PNU-dominated Subor back in 1896. The repression against Slavic autonomists that followed had radicalized the movement and driven waves of political emigration — to France and Britain, but also to Russia, a longtime patron of Slavic movements within Austria-Hungary.

Bluxa fell in with these émigré circles, and in 1912 published a book on the prospects of Pannonian independence, this time free of mysticism or pseudoscience: a straightforward nationalist-democratic program. Grown more radical than in his youth, he called for the dissolution of the Habsburg Empire, an independent Republic of Upper Pannonia, and liberal internal policies including secular education and women's rights. The book established him as an influential figure among the diaspora.

When war broke out in 1914, the Russian government recognized Bluxa as a useful asset for undermining Austria-Hungary from within. It financed his newspaper For Free Pannonia and backed a political organization under his leadership, the Pannonian Committee in Petrograd, which from 1915 began recruiting Pannonian prisoners of war into what became the Pannonian Legion. During these years, Bluxa also made contact with the leaders of the Czechoslovak movement — Masaryk, Beneš, and Štefánik — and secured their backing for Upper Pannonian independence, a connection that would serve him well later on. After the February Revolution, Bluxa took his wife and children via neutral Sweden and Norway to England, then on to France.

Birth of the First Republic (1917–18)

In Paris, Bluxa reconnected with his brother Krasimir, who still ran a fairly popular hydrotherapy clinic, having long since dropped the original tenets of baphotherapy, and with his brother-in-law August, by now a colonel on the French General Staff. These connections let him resume political work, now under French patronage. Probably through French intelligence channels, he opened a secret correspondence with PNU figures still operating legally inside Upper Pannonia, among them his brother-in-law Zlotan Czigraj and Otto Ostrovit Bregovi, son of Adalfrid Attila. In the summer of 1918, with Austria-Hungary's collapse already looming, these and other figures formed the National Committee of Upper Pannonia. That autumn, when the collapse came, their moment arrived.

On 16 October 1918, Emperor Karl's manifesto authorized the monarchy's peoples, including the Slavic peoples of the Hungarian half, to form national committees. The already-functioning National Committee gained legal standing and, with it, the political weight of the country's most authoritative body. The Ban of Upper Pannonia, his authority in Budapest gone, resigned on 29 October and handed power to the Committee. On 6 November, the Committee declared Upper Pannonia's independence.

The Committee was not of one mind. Its Zgurian majority, Czigraj and Bregovi among them, pressed for full independence. Its Deglian minority wanted federation with the newly formed State of Slovenes, Croats and Serbs, uniting the South Slavic peoples of the former Austria-Hungary. A Hungarian population, excluded from the Committee altogether, demanded that the union with Hungary continue. Through November and December, clashes broke out across the country with demobilized Hungarian soldiers making their way home from the front.

In Paris, meanwhile, Bluxa lobbied the French government and American diplomats for recognition and support of Pannonian independence, and got it. In December 1918, he and August Mirambeau arrived in Vronovica. The Committee brought both men in, valuing above all their ties to Paris. On the eve of the 20 December session at which the Committee was to form a Provisional Government, Marta Bregovi — wife of Otto Ostrovit and a former patient of Bluxa's — wrote a note to her husband:

Remember, my sweet cucumber: when you speak up for Z., don't lean on the argument that he's a genius, a visionary, the nation's only hope — we both know it's true, but pressing that point will only anger his enemies and put off the undecided. Insist instead, as forcefully as you like — because it happens to be true — that Z. has an international reputation and strong connections with the French, the Americans, and the Czechoslovaks, and that right now foreign support is a matter of our nation's survival. In short: get him at least the Foreign Ministry, or I shall be angry and sulky.

The argument worked. Over the objections of skeptics who dismissed Bluxa as "a buffoon, a con man, and a spy for every intelligence service", the new cabinet handed him two key posts at once — Foreign Minister and Prime Minister. His first decree appointed Colonel Mirambeau commander of the Pannonian National Army, which had yet to be formed.

At the Head of Government (1919–24)

The young republic inherited a heavy burden. The economy lay in ruins, Hungary and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (the SHS) threatened it from outside, international recognition remained shaky, and there was no agreement even within the government itself: the Deglians still pressed for union with the SHS. The civil administration inherited from the empire kept running like a well-oiled machine, but the country had no army — one had to be improvised in a hurry from demobilized Pannonian soldiers of the former Austro-Hungarian army, most of them worn out after four years of war.

Under these formidable conditions, Bluxa, who had no administrative experience whatsoever, not only survived politically but scored real successes. Mirambeau quickly assembled a fighting core for the Pannonian National Army (PNA) and used it to put down the Hungarian uprisings. Another threat soon grew in its place: the Pannonian Legion, under Drogan Glovacz, crushed a communist uprising in the spring and summer of 1919 and remained the only genuinely disciplined and experienced military force in the country. Bluxa neutralized Glovacz without an open confrontation — in 1920 he was sent off on a military-diplomatic mission to Paris and London, and in his absence the Legion was folded into the PNA under Mirambeau, a far less dangerous figure, since a foreigner could not aspire to supreme power. The domestic situation stabilized. At the Paris Peace Conference, Bluxa pushed for international recognition of independence; the outcome was a compromise, with final recognition made conditional on a plebiscite.

The first parliamentary elections were held in 1920. The PNU won a majority, Bluxa stayed on as Prime Minister, and the presidency — purely ceremonial under the constitution — went to Czigraj. Bluxa immediately took up reforms in the nationalist-democratic spirit: secularizing education and extending the franchise. The program met resistance even within his own party — not everyone in the PNU shared Bluxa's radicalism — and the Church opposed secularization outright: Pannonian society at large turned out to be more conservative than its Prime Minister. Pannonian was made the official language, a "linguistic purification" campaign began, and state symbols were adopted. The promised plebiscite was held in 1920 and confirmed the choice of independence, a victory for Bluxa and the PNU.

Bluxa relied on a dense network of support inside and outside the PNU — people bound to him by kinship, old friendship, or the sectarian ties of Venetic-theory adepts and baphotherapy practitioners. That was enough to hold on to power, but not enough to win broad popular support. Bluxa's cabinet, absorbed in nation-building and cultural-reform projects, handled the economy poorly. Voters grew steadily more disillusioned and drifted to the Social Democrats. In the 1924 elections the PNU held onto first place but lost its majority. To stay in power, it had to form a coalition with the more conservative Christian Socialists, who made Bluxa's own resignation, as too much of a radical, a condition of joining. So in February 1924 Bluxa's premiership ended, and with it his political career.

The Last Expedition (1924–25)

Bluxa took his removal from office hard: his own party surrendered him for the coalition deal, and the ungrateful Pannonians, to whom he himself had given the vote, had withdrawn their trust in him. But he was not a man built for depression. He was still full of energy and in excellent physical shape for a man of sixty-three. He announced publicly that he was leaving politics to return to invention and to what he called his scientific research.

In the summer of 1924, Bluxa left for Australia with a small group of assistants, including his son Camille. In Cairns he bought a small lugger, named it Venus, and began preparing for an expedition. He intended to return to the waters off New Guinea and search the Ventanat Islands again, this time with an improved echolithoscope and modern diving equipment. He claimed he expected to find serious, conclusive evidence of the Venetic civilization on the floor of the Pacific.

On 25 April 1925, at the tail end of the storm season, the Venus left Cairns, crossed the Coral Sea on a favorable trade wind, and put in at Port Moresby on 2 May to obtain official clearance from the Australian administration of Papua for a passage through the eastern archipelagos and to buy the latest Admiralty charts. On 18 May the Venus called at Samarai, the "capital" of eastern New Guinea, a tiny island crowded with warehouses and the offices of pearl and copra traders — where the crew needed to hire local Papuan pilots and sailors who knew the local currents. On 6 June the Venus put to sea. She was never seen again.

The archipelagos east of New Guinea are a formidable maze of volcanic islands, tidal currents, and coral banks absent from any chart; tropical waters where a hull can foul with barnacles past the point of steerage within two weeks; a breeding ground for blackwater fever, the deadliest form of malaria; and, at the time, islands inhabited by uncontacted and often hostile Papuan and Melanesian peoples. The Venus carried no radio — the small vessel, already overloaded with diving and echolithoscope equipment, had no room left for the additional bulky apparatus and lead batteries. In short, the lugger could have been lost for any of a hundred reasons. It is somewhat stranger that not even wreckage of the Venus was ever found.

Conclusion

A deeply contentious figure in life, even within his own party, Bluxa was canonized almost immediately after his death as a father of the republic and one of the central figures of the Pannonian national pantheon. Everything that came before — the Venetic theory, baphotherapy, the espionage and medical scandals — was glossed over in this version of his biography, or recast as the eccentric enthusiasms of a future statesman. As early as 1926, the street in the Second District of Vronovica where Bluxa was born was renamed in his honor, and a statue of him went up in 1927.

Under Glovacz's dictatorship and monarchy, the former Prime Minister became a figure written out of the record. Glovacz was now the sole father of independent Upper Pannonia, and there was no room beside him for anyone else. King Drogan's own memoirs, and the histories published under his reign, mentioned Bluxa only in passing, lumped together with the other state-mongers who had nearly wrecked the republic. The statue was left standing, though, and the street kept its name — it took the Hungarian occupiers, bent on erasing all memory of Pannonian independence, to finally remove them. Under Communists, Bluxa was cast as a narrow bourgeois nationalist, one of those responsible for crushing the Pannonian Soviet Republic — in short, a villain there too.

The post-Communist authorities tried to rehabilitate Bluxa as a hero again, looking for ideological grounding in the democratic era of the First Republic. The Zgurian-Deglian split buried that search. Zgurians still hold Bluxa in high regard, if with some reservations about the "darker chapters" of his biography; Deglians see him instead as the archetype of the despised "Zgurian clan" — a network of politicians of Zgurian origin, bound by kinship and shared ideology, who imposed their own — Zgurian — interests on the young republic under the guise of national ones.

Biographers aiming for neutrality tend to describe Bluxa as a gifted mystifier who was handed one chance to turn his powers of persuasion to the political stage, and who made the most of it. Whether this amounted to statesmanship or just another successful con remains hard to say. The disappearance of the Venus in 1925 still invites romantic speculation to this day. Bluxa had, after all, spent his life teaching that death by water could be a passage rather than an ending; he could hardly have chosen a more fitting end for himself.
