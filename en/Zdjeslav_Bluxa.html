Zdjeslav Bluxa - DRAFT

Zdjeslav Gostyvojev Bluxa (Glagolitic: ⰈⰌⰅⰔⰎⰀⰂ ⰃⰑⰔⰕⰬⰂⰑⰟⰂ ⰁⰎⰖⰢⰀ; 1861-1924) was an Upper Pannonian Slavic Revival activist, politician, writer, traveller, inventor, underwater photography pioneer, fringe scientist, occultist, cult leader, probably a spy, and the first Prime Minister of the independent Upper Pannonia. Early years

Born in 1861, Zdjeslav and his twin brother Zbyslav were the eldest sons of Gostyvoj Bluxa — a prominent Slavic Revival activist — and Magrita Bregovska, an illegitimate daughter of the Pannonian nobleman Lajosz Leonard Bregovi. As a child he was curious, smart, active, and creative, already showing a talent for deception. When Zdjeslav was five, both brothers fell into the water from a boat; Zbyslav drowned, Zdjeslav survived. The loss left a permanent mark on him — by all accounts, he had been deeply attached to his twin.

Zdjeslav attended a classical gymnasium alongside his father's Slavic Sunday school. In 1877 he enrolled in the Pannonian Pedagogical Institute, which Gostyvoj had co-founded that same year. That year also brought the deaths of his father and Dometius Bobov — lifelong rivals and collaborators who killed each other in their final duel — leaving Zdjeslav, at sixteen, the head of the family. He took the responsibility seriously and remained devoted to his siblings and their children throughout his life.

Two years later, in 1879, Zdjeslav became the informal leader of the Young Pannonians, a circle of Slavic Revival enthusiasts that had coalesced around the Pedagogical Institute. At its productive peak in the 1880s, the circle generated much of what became canonical in Pannonian art and literature, with members including novelists, composers, playwrights, and painters. The wider circle organized cultural events and supported its members' work; the inner circle formed a secret society centered on an cult of the Slavic goddess Mora, impersonated by Zdjeslav's sister Kvjetana. By all accounts, the cult was a pure invention by Zdjeslav Bluxa himself.

Through the Slavic movement Bluxa befriended his maternal uncle Adalfrid Attila, head of the House of Bregovi, an adventurous traveller who had explored the Malay Archipelago, Africa, and the Near East. Bregovi gave Bluxa access to the family library at Bregovo Castle, including the occult and alchemical manuscripts assembled by Gergej Gabriel — a former head of the house, a Freemason and Rosicrucian of notably mystical inclinations.

The reading fed directly into Bluxa's first publication 1. In 1882 he presented his pseudo-scientific Venetic theory inspired by theosophy, occult doctrines, pan-Slavic nationalism, and astronomy books by Camille Flammarion. The Veneds or Venets, a name for the proto-Slavs attested in ancient sources, were theorized to have been Slavic-speaking, proto-Glagolitic-writing superhumans from Venus. Bluxa inverted the conventional etymology: the goddess Venus was named after the planet, not the reverse — the planet's name being the Venets' own. These cosmic beings had "upbred" and "enlightened" the "subhuman" natives of Earth, built a great prehistoric civilization, and were ultimately destroyed by a flood — reflected in the legends of Atlantis and Vineta, the latter being, in Bluxa's reconstruction, the original form.

The theory's implications were immediately practical. In 1884, convinced that Venice had been a Venetic settlement, Bluxa attempted underwater photography in the Venetian lagoon. Italian authorities, suspecting Bluxa of being an Austrian agent spying on the Italian navy, arrested and expelled him. In 1885–1886 he joined Adalfrid's expedition into the Pacific Ocean. Landing on the Ventanat Islands — small islets east of New Guinea — Bluxa declared them an Upper Pannonian possession on the grounds that a supposedly Venetic colony had once stood there.

Bluxa also brought an improved underwater camera of his own construction, taking photos of what he would present as submerged ruins. The images were blurred and inconclusive, but on returning to Vronovica in 1887 he mounted an exhibition of photographs and purported Venetic artefacts — all, as later investigation confirmed, forgeries.

In 1888 Bluxa patented his underwater camera — a device that Jacques Cousteau would assess positively decades later — and published a philosophical treatise, Immersion as Spiritual Experience 2. The book described his perceptions during deep dives, which he interpreted as mystical visions rather than the hypoxia-induced hallucinations they more plausibly were. He argued that immersion to the edge of death enabled contact with the spiritual world, water being the element bridging the material and the immaterial, and that this experience could be shared collectively. From there he extrapolated further: the ancient Venets had deliberately submerged their civilization to attain supernatural powers; they had done the same on Venus before arriving on Earth; and the modern Venetian ruling class — crypto-Slavic in Bluxa's reading — might be engineering the slow sinking of their city with the same aim. Some readers were persuaded; among them, the Young Pannonian composer Komarovicki was moved to write his symphony The Drowned World.

Between 1888 and 1890 Bluxa toured the Slavic cultural capitals — Prague, St. Petersburg, Moscow, Warsaw — giving lectures and mounting exhibitions. Reception ranged from outright dismissal as a con artist to genuine enthusiasm. He presented his Venetic theories to general audiences; for more select circles, he demonstrated his immersion technique as a path to mystical experience.
