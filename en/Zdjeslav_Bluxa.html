Zdjeslav Bluxa - DRAFT

Zdjeslav Gostyvojev Bluxa (Glagolitic: ⰈⰌⰅⰔⰎⰀⰂ ⰃⰑⰔⰕⰬⰂⰑⰟⰂ ⰁⰎⰖⰢⰀ; 1861-1924) was an Upper Pannonian Slavic Revival activist, politician, writer, traveller, inventor, underwater archeology pioneer, fringe scientist, healer, occultist, cult leader, probably a spy, and the first Prime Minister of the independent Upper Pannonia. Early years

Born in 1861, Zdjeslav and his twin brother Zbyslav were the eldest sons of Gostyvoj Bluxa — a prominent Slavic Revival activist — and Magrita Bregovska, an illegitimate daughter of the Pannonian nobleman Lajosz Leonard Bregovi. As a child he was curious, smart, active, and creative, already showing a talent for deception. When Zdjeslav was five, both brothers fell into the water from a boat; Zbyslav drowned, Zdjeslav survived. The loss left a permanent mark on him — by all accounts, he had been deeply attached to his twin.

Zdjeslav attended a classical gymnasium alongside his father's Slavic Sunday school. In 1877 he enrolled in the Pannonian Pedagogical Institute, which Gostyvoj Bluxa had co-founded with Dometius Bobov that same year. That year also brought the deaths of his father and Bobov — lifelong rivals and collaborators who killed each other in their final duel — leaving Zdjeslav, at sixteen, the head of the family. He took the responsibility seriously and remained devoted to his siblings and their children throughout his life.

Slavic Revival Movement

Two years later, in 1879, Zdjeslav became the informal leader of the Young Pannonians, a circle of Slavic Revival enthusiasts that had coalesced around the Pedagogical Institute. At its productive peak in the 1880s, the circle generated much of what became canonical in Pannonian art and literature, with members including novelists, composers, playwrights, and painters. The wider circle organized cultural events and supported its members' work; the inner circle formed a secret society centered on an cult of the Slavic goddess Mora, impersonated by Zdjeslav's sister Kvjetana. By all accounts, the cult was a pure invention by Zdjeslav Bluxa himself.

Through the Slavic movement Bluxa befriended his maternal uncle Adalfrid Attila, head of the House of Bregovi, a wealthy nobleman and an adventurous traveller who had explored the Malay Archipelago, Africa, and the Near East. Bregovi gave Bluxa access to the family library at Bregovo Castle, including the occult and alchemical manuscripts assembled by Gergej Gabriel — a former head of the house, a Freemason and Rosicrucian of notably mystical inclinations.

The reading fed directly into Bluxa's first publication 1. In 1882 he presented his pseudo-scientific Venetic theory inspired by theosophy, occult doctrines, pan-Slavic nationalism, and astronomy books by Camille Flammarion. The Veneds or Venets, a name for the proto-Slavs attested in ancient sources, were theorized to have been Slavic-speaking, proto-Glagolitic-writing superhumans from Venus. Bluxa inverted the conventional etymology: the goddess Venus was named after the planet, not the reverse — the planet's name being the Venets' own. These cosmic beings had "upbred" and "enlightened" the "subhuman" natives of Earth, built a great prehistoric civilization, and were ultimately destroyed by a flood — reflected in the legends of Atlantis and Vineta, the latter being, in Bluxa's reconstruction, the original form.

Travels

The theory's implications were immediately practical. In 1884, convinced that Venice had been a Venetic settlement, Bluxa attempted underwater photography in the Venetian lagoon. Italian authorities, suspecting Bluxa of being an Austrian agent spying on the Italian navy, arrested and expelled him. In 1885–1886 he joined Adalfrid Attila Bregovi's expedition into the Pacific Ocean. Landing on the Ventanat Islands — small islets east of New Guinea — Bluxa declared them an Upper Pannonian possession on the grounds that a supposedly Venetic colony had once stood there.

Bluxa patented an improved underwater camera of his own construction — a device that Jacques Cousteau would assess positively decades later. In the New Guinea expedition, he used it to take photos of what he would present as submerged ruins. The images were blurred and inconclusive, but on returning to Vronovica in 1887 he mounted an exhibition of photographs and purported Venetic artefacts — all, as later investigation confirmed, forgeries.

Immersion theory and practice

Soon after return, in 1888, Bluxa published a philosophical treatise, Immersion as Spiritual Experience 2. The book described his perceptions during deep dives, which he interpreted as mystical visions rather than the hypoxia-induced hallucinations they more plausibly were. He argued that immersion to the edge of death enabled contact with the spiritual world, water being the element bridging the material and the immaterial, and that this experience could be shared collectively. From there he extrapolated further: the ancient Venets had deliberately submerged their civilization to attain supernatural powers; they had done the same on Venus before arriving on Earth; and the modern Venetian ruling class — crypto-Slavic in Bluxa's reading — might be engineering the slow sinking of their city with the same aim. Some readers were persuaded; among them, the Young Pannonian composer Komarovicki was moved to write his symphony The Drowned World.

Between 1888 and 1890, Bluxa toured Vienna, Prague, St. Petersburg, Moscow, and Warsaw, giving lectures and mounting exhibitions of his New Guinean "findings". Reception ranged from outright dismissal as a freak or con artist to genuine enthusiasm. He presented his Venetic theories to general audiences; for more select circles, he demonstrated his immersion technique as a path to mystical experience. After returning to Vronovica, he rented from Adalfrid Attila a small sanatorium in the Gnilovody hot spring resort for a symbolic price and made it a "spiritual healing center".

The "immersion healing" technique — combining mineral water treatment, mild induced hypoxia, and "spiritual therapy" — proved effective against what was then diagnosed as "hysteria". Bluxa became a popular healer, building something like a cult among his devoted patients, most of them women.

Emigration

At the same time, Bluxa promoted a pro-Slavic agenda: language rights and greater autonomy for Upper Pannonia within Hungary. He aligned himself with the Pannonian National Union (PNU), recently founded by his brother-in-law Zlotan Czigraj, husband of his sister Kvjetana. In autumn 1894, the PNU stood a good chance of winning a majority in the Subor — Upper Pannonia's legislative body, almost powerless and elected by the five percent of citizens who met its property qualification. A timely scandal broke: the anti-PNU press accused Bluxa of "immoral behavior" toward his female patients.

These accusations were not ungrounded. The "spiritual immersion" technique required the healer and patient to bathe together, nude, to ensure "full and unconstrained bodily contact with the aquatic element". Bluxa denied any closer bodily contact, but had to shut down his clinic and leave the country. The PNU won the election regardless.

In 1895, Bluxa moved to France, where he published translations of his works. In Paris, he met Camille Flammarion and tried to win him over to his theories, without success. Bluxa moved in the circle of the occultist Papus (Gérard Encausse), and discussed hypoxia and hallucination with the physiologist Charles Richet, whose own research into psychical phenomena inclined him to take Bluxa's immersion visions seriously. He also crossed paths with the Polish Socialist Party emigres Bolesław Limanowski and Stanisław Mendelson, though his monarchist sympathies confined these acquaintances to café argument. Bluxa gained a measure of fame thereafter, and opened an immersion clinic near Paris.

Research and marriage

In 1897, he received a letter from Vannes — a French city named after the Veneti, a Gaulish tribe. An anonymous local claimed that Bluxa had rediscovered a genuine, still-surviving Venetic tradition, and invited him to join its secret society. Bluxa believed it and travelled to Vannes, only to find that the sender was a delusional young woman Julie Mirambeau who had come to believe her own fantasies, inspired by Bluxa's writings.

Bluxa set out to cure Julie's "hysteria", and the two became involved. While in Vannes, he undertook yet another underwater exploration and tested a new invention he named the echolithoscope, a kind of primitive sonar intended to locate underwater stone constructions. The research was cut short when Julie's brother August, a French army lieutenant, demanded that Bluxa marry her.

Bluxa fled to Germany instead, and in 1899 mounted a new expedition near Danzig in search of Vineta, only to be arrested by German police, who accused him of spying for France on the German navy. He moved on to St. Petersburg, but in 1901 the Mirambeau siblings tracked him down there, and he was finally forced to marry Julie, who had already borne his son Camille in 1899.

In the Russian Empire

In St. Petersburg, Bluxa lectured to Vladimir Bekhterev's clinical circle on immersion therapy and struck up an alliance with the court physician Pyotr Badmaev, who blended Tibetan medicine with fashionable occultism among the capital's elite. The zoologist Nikolai Wagner, a longtime advocate of spiritualism among Russian academics, tested Bluxa's hypoxia-trance technique on volunteers from his own laboratory.

These connections proved useful. In 1903, Bluxa presented his echolithoscope to the Russian Navy's technical committee as a mine-detection device — later admitting that his real aim had been financing for underwater archaeology. Tested successfully at Kronstadt in 1904, it was approved by the Navy Staff for the war against Japan. As the only person able to operate it, Bluxa was attached to the fleet as a civilian contractor and sailed with Admiral Rozhestvensky's squadron to the Pacific. His ship was hit at Tsushima; an excellent swimmer, he survived and pulled a sailor from the water, but was taken prisoner by the Japanese.

In Asia and America

In captivity, Bluxa appealed to the Austrian embassy, and Austrian diplomats pressed Japan to hand him over as an Austro-Hungarian subject wanted for questioning about his Russian Navy service. Released into Austrian custody in 1905, he was being escorted home under guard when, in Hong Kong, he somehow hypnotized his guards, robbed them, and slipped aboard the first ship bound for San Francisco.

In the US, Bluxa had already acquired a fan base. Charlotte Winthrop Peabody, a Boston heiress who had come to Gnilovody for "hysteria" treatment before the 1894 scandal, had followed his career ever since and financed the American editions of his books. She now arranged an honorary doctorate for him from the Medical School of Maine at Bowdoin College in Brunswick — a small institution later shut down for its low standards — and funded a new clinic that opened there in 1906, promoting it energetically among Boston society. Bluxa corresponded with the Harvard psychologist William James on the nature of hypoxia-induced visions, and befriended the psychical researcher Hereward Carrington, then preparing to bring the Italian medium Eusapia Palladino to America for testing.

It ended in tragedy: in 1909, one of his patients drowned mid-session, and Bluxa had to flee the country. Return home was still barred by the unresolved suspicion of Russian espionage, so he went back instead to St. Petersburg, to a wife and son he had not seen in five years.

Again in Russia

In St. Petersburg he found that Julie had converted to Orthodoxy, joined Rasputin's inner circle of devoted women, and in 1906 had borne another son, Seraphim, by an unknown father. The marriage nonetheless held. Once news of the American scandal reached Europe, Bluxa abandoned medical practice altogether and turned his attention to politics.

In St. Petersburg he also found a growing Pannonian political diaspora. Hungarian authorities had dissolved the PNU-dominated Subor back in 1896. The repression against Slavic autonomists that followed had radicalized the movement and driven waves of political emigration — to France and Britain, but also to Russia, a longtime patron of Slavic movements within Austria-Hungary.

Bluxa fell in with these émigré circles, and in 1912 published a book on the prospects of Pannonian independence, this time free of mysticism or pseudoscience: a straightforward nationalist-democratic program. Grown more radical than in his youth, he called for the dissolution of the Habsburg Empire, an independent Republic of Upper Pannonia, and liberal internal policies including secular education and women's rights. The book established him as an influential figure among the diaspora.

When war broke out in 1914, the Russian government recognized Bluxa as a useful asset for undermining Austria-Hungary from within. It financed his newspaper For Free Pannonia and backed a political organization under his leadership, the Pannonian Committee in Petrograd, which from 1915 began recruiting Pannonian prisoners of war into what became the Pannonian Legion. After the February Revolution, Bluxa took his wife and children via neutral Sweden and Norway to England, then on to France, where he continued his political work, now under French sponsorship.
