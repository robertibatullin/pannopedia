Advent of Freedom

The Advent of Freedom (Zgurian and Deglian Advent slobody, Glagolitic ⰀⰄⰂⰅⰐⰕ ⰔⰎⰑⰁⰑⰄⰬ) was the fall of the Communist regime in Former Upper Pannonia in November and December 1989. It lasted only six weeks, partly overlapping with the Catholic season of Advent, hence the name. Yet it was crowded with events and shaped the country's future.

Background

By the end of Severin Gowbka's half-century in power, the economy of the People's Republic of Upper Pannonia (PRUP) was in prolonged decline. Real wages were falling, queues for bread and meat had become routine, and petrol and consumer goods could only be had through connections. Discontent first broke into the open in Gowbkaslava, where steelworkers and chemical workers went on strike repeatedly in 1988–1989. The authorities alternately punished the strikers and bought them off, but offered no change.

Meanwhile, Eastern Europe was in upheaval. In Poland, the Round Table talks led to semi-free elections, and in August the non-communist Tadeusz Mazowiecki became prime minister. Hungary dismantled its border fence with Austria, and thousands of East Germans poured through to the West; mass demonstrations filled the streets of Leipzig and Berlin, and in October Erich Honecker resigned. The Pannonian media said nothing, but Hungarian television could be received in the north of the country: the language was incomrehensible but the footage spoke for itself. At night, homemade shortwave sets picked up Pannonian-language broadcasts of Radio Free Europe and Voice of America.

Everyone expected change, including most party officials, who could see that the old course led nowhere. The one exception was Gowbka himself. At seventy-five, the Leader had no intention of either reforming or retiring. He still personally struck the word “reform” out of party documents and insisted that construction of the People's Palace go on. The Politburo dared not contradict him: the fate of Ljubovan Jazvjec, head of the capital's party organization, dismissed for a careless remark, was a lesson to all.

10-13 November: Death of the Leader

On the evening of 10 November 1989, Gowbka suffered a massive heart attack at the Green Court (Zeleny dvor), his private estate outside Vronovica — according to legend, a few hours after being told of the fall of the Berlin Wall. His guards reported the death first to the Minister of State Security, Jaromir Doszev, who ordered it kept secret. Under the PRUP Constitution, presidential powers passed automatically to the Leader's widow, Miljana Gowbka, as chairwoman of the Legislative Assembly. Aware of how unpopular she was, she made no attempt to hold on to power and wrote her letter of resignation that same night.

On the morning of 11 November, the Politburo met in secret: fifteen spineless functionaries used to carrying out Gowbka's will without question. The Leader had left no instructions for the event of his death, and they were at a loss. They invited the disgraced Jazvjec, the most popular politician in the country, restored him to the Politburo, and offered him the party leadership, but he declined on grounds of ill health. No new general secretary was chosen; having no better option, the Politburo agreed that the party would be run collectively. With Miljana Gowbka gone, the formal head of state was now her deputy, Ervin Zsladov, an apparatchik nobody had heard of, not even a Politburo member.

The situation was dangerous, he said: the socialist bloc was collapsing and the country was restless. Reform was unavoidable, but the party had to keep power, and the sensible way to do so was to step into the shadows and let state bodies act without visible party oversight. Gowbka's death should stay secret until Vronovica was fully under control; there should be no state funeral, and the cult of personality, which everyone was tired of, should be wound down quickly. Nobody objected.

On 12 November, Gowbka was buried in secret, with only his relatives present; the burial site remains unknown. The media reported that the Leader was ill, while the NON tightened security around government buildings in the capital. Rumors of his death leaked out all the same, and columns of military vehicles in the streets only lent them weight. On 13 November, Miljana Gowbka, her nephew Karol, her daughter-in-law Monika (widow of Boris Gowbka), and her granddaughters Bettina and Lucia left for Libya for good. The rumors grew more insistent, and people gathered in the streets demanding the truth.

14-24 November: First Days of Freedom

On the morning of 14 November, the authorities finally made it official: Gowbka was dead, Miljana Gowbka had resigned, and there would be no new general secretary — the party would be led collectively. The duties of parliamentary chairman and president passed to Zsladov. In his first address to the nation, he vaguely promised “long-overdue” political and economic reforms and a readiness to “listen to society.” One day of mourning was declared; the state funeral was cancelled, supposedly “at the family's request.”

People poured into the streets, and not in a mourning mood. Strangers congratulated each other with tears of joy. In places the celebrations turned into riots: posters and portraits of Gowbka were torn down everywhere, monuments defaced, shop windows smashed, and policemen attacked; a random passer-by was beaten up after being mistaken for Miljana Gowbka. The hotheads set out to storm the Grod, the NON headquarters, but Jazvjec came out to the crowd and calmed it, preventing bloodshed. Crowds of would-be emigrants massed at the Hungarian and Yugoslav borders. The border guards did not dare to shoot and, under pressure, let everyone through.

On 15 November, Zsladov signed his first decrees. They called an extraordinary session of the Legislative Assembly for 25 November and set up a Legislative Committee to draft reform laws, which included, besides party apparatchiks, popular reformist intellectuals such as Vaclav Bluxa. Political prisoners were amnestied, and inmates of psychiatric hospitals were released according to a list drawn up by international human rights organizations; among those freed was the Deglian nationalist Kosta Raczev. The decrees abolished censorship and travel restrictions and legalized the private press, private trade, and small businesses. For a while, this calmed the streets.

On the same day, the CPUP Central Committee held an extraordinary plenum. Gowbka was honored with a minute's silence, and after that his name was not mentioned again, neither in praise nor in condemnation.

Over the following days, slogans quoting Gowbka and all but the largest of his monuments were removed across the country in an orderly fashion. Local authorities began renaming streets and towns, and the Leader's name gradually disappeared from the map. In a new decree, Zsladov abolished the NON, the country's most feared and hated institution, splitting it into intelligence, counterintelligence, and several other services.

The same days, Zsladov telephoned foreign heads of state, starting with Gorbachev, promising democratic reform and a return to normal relations; Ceaușescu did not take the call.

Meanwhile, the country saw a boom in street rallies, petty trade, and the independent press, helped by the fact that paper was still sold at state prices. There was no regulation whatsoever: people sold whatever they had wherever they could, and printed and said whatever they liked. After half a century of dictatorship, they were drunk on freedom. Among dozens of other papers, Raczev's Deglian Herald appeared — a modest sheet that drew attention only through its publisher's famous name.

25 November — 5 December: Reforms Begin

The extraordinary session of the Legislative Assembly opened on 25 November. The deputies were the same ones elected under Gowbka, who until then had dutifully rubber-stamped the party's decisions: mostly party apparatchiks and mid-level officials, with a sprinkling of workers and peasants for show. Zsladov proclaimed the dawn of an era of democratic renewal, and the Legislative Committee introduced its first bill, hastily drafted in ten days, on the reform of the army.

By 1 December the bill had passed almost unanimously, with only minor amendments. Conscription of women was abolished, men's service was cut to eighteen months, and the regular army was to be reduced in stages. Amendments to the Criminal Code passed at the same time: political articles were repealed, private enterprise was decriminalized, and everyone convicted under these articles who had not already been freed by the amnesty was released immediately.

From 1 to 5 December, parliament debated the 1990 budget — openly, for the first time; under Gowbka, the budget had been secret and approved by the government without any input from deputies. The Assembly adopted amendments put forward by Bluxa on behalf of the Legislative Committee, sharply cutting state military procurement, spending on the army and the former NON, and funding for the People's Palace. The planned economy and price controls, however, were kept for a transitional period.

The extraordinary 17th Congress of the CPUP opened on 5 December. The party renamed itself the Socialist Party and promised a “reassessment of Gowbka's legacy” and broad democratization. Nobody objected. Everyone could feel which way the wind was blowing, and even the staunchest hardliners lacked the courage to go against it.

6-8 December: The Alphabet Question

On 6 December, the Legislative Assembly passed a new electoral law introducing a multi-party system and freedom of campaigning; early elections under the new rules were set for October 1990. The same day, a bill was introduced for a gradual switch to the Latin alphabet — bluxovica — as a symbol of breaking out of cultural isolation and returning to Europe.

9-24 December: Change Gathers Pace

Meanwhile, new parties of every stripe sprang up everywhere: dozens of democratic ones, from moderate to radical, and nationalist ones, such as Raczev's Deglian People's Front, which demanded cultural and linguistic autonomy for the Deglians. The economy still ran on central planning and fixed prices, and so shortages persisted, only partly eased by private trade. The market itself was practically unregulated, and business disputes were increasingly settled by force. On 20 December, thousands of officers of the NOR and the former NON, discharged under the cuts, received their last pay; nobody knew what they would do next.

On 24 December, the Legislative Assembly appointed Bluxa prime minister with a mandate for decisive market reforms. Bluxa promised a short, hard period, after which the market would sort everything out. Parliament approved his program and went into recess. So ended the honeymoon of Upper Pannonian democracy.

The Advent of Freedom was a time of optimism and hope for quick renewal. But the same weeks saw the first cracks of ethnic division: the alphabet question carried Zgurian–Deglian tension into politics. Had the economic reforms succeeded, rising prosperity might have defused it. That did not happen.
