Visiting Former Upper Pannonia

This article covers the practical side of entry procedures for foreign nationals — and foreign nationals only — into Former Upper Pannonia. Entry procedures for holders of Upper Pannonian passports (typically bought online) are covered in a separate article and are not addressed here. The procedures described below were current as of publication (August 2026); individual rules can and often do change unpredictably, though the overall approach has remained more or less stable since the early 2000s.

General principles

Former Upper Pannonia (FUP) is a territory in Central Europe that has had no internationally recognized government or state status since 1995. In practice it is divided between two unrecognized (and mutually unrecognized) de facto states — the Zgurian Republic (ZR) and the Deglian Republic (DR). Under the Lausanne Agreements that ended the 1992–1995 civil war, the only internationally recognized institution is the United Nations Observer Mission for Former Upper Pannonia (UNOMFUP), headed by the UN High Commissioner. This administration, which directly controls the Neutral Zone between the DR and the ZR, serves as the diplomatic and legal interface between the two de facto states and the international community.

Anyone hoping to enter FUP should understand that, as a rule, neither the UN administration nor either of the de facto states is glad to see them. The UNOMFUP website warns that in FUP, no internationally recognized institution guarantees a foreigner's civil rights or safety, and that visiting is not advisable. In the DR, which has been under permanent martial law since 1999, any foreigner is treated as a potential spy. The ZR authorities pursue a more open policy, but after a wave of exposés in the early 2000s uncovered corruption in the republic, they have been deeply suspicious of journalists, bloggers, and overly curious foreigners in general. These attitudes have shaped the visa policies of all three administrations for more than twenty years.

Entering the Neutral Zone

The Neutral Zone has no external border of its own; the only way in is by air, through King Svetoplok International Airport in Vronovica. Entry requires a visa issued by the UNOMFUP. There is no tourist visa — only diplomatic, humanitarian, work, or business visas are issued. Since the UN does not deal with private individuals, no country has a consulate or visa center where you could apply for one directly. In theory, you could approach your own country's foreign ministry, which would then take the matter up with the UN. In practice, though, this channel has never actually been tested, and it isn't clear that anyone has ever gotten a visa this way.

In practice, you should be invited by the UNOMFUP itself or by a diplomatic, humanitarian, or commercial organization based in the Neutral Zone. This is the usual route for staff of international organizations, journalists, and businesspeople. In this case the visa is processed directly in FUP, a confirmation document (accreditation letter) is mailed to you, and the visa is stamped into your passport at the airport on presentation of that document; the wait is usually a few months. Even this route can end in refusal, though, since there is a whole market in invitations from fake humanitarian organizations, and the UNOMFUP accordingly keeps a blacklist of organizations whose invitations don't count. The list changes constantly, is never published, and is evidently riddled with errors — an invitation from a perfectly legitimate organization is often refused, while one from an obvious shell outfit sails through. There is reason to suspect that every consular officer in the UNOMFUP keeps their own personal version of the blacklist.

Entering the Zgurian Republic

As an unrecognized state, the Zgurian Republic maintains no diplomatic missions abroad. Its visa can only be obtained at the border, at one of three land crossings — with Hungary, Austria, or Slovenia. This is a considerably easier and faster process, though it is still not an automatic stamp. You must fill out a lengthy paper form, pay a visa fee (an unpredictable 100 to 150 euros), and wait while border guards check your details against their databases. The wait runs from one to four hours, not counting time spent queuing or filling out the form. The Zgurian authorities evidently keep their own blacklists, populated by anyone who has ever written anything negative about the Zgurian Republic — or anything positive about the Deglian Republic — in the media or on social networks, plus many people caught up at random. Unofficial estimates put the refusal rate at 10 to 15 percent. Reasons for refusal are never given, and the visa fee is not refunded.

In 2025, Zgurian Foreign Minister Rosczislav Vloszek (son of Ljubodar) triumphantly announced the launch of a mobile app ZgurPass that would let travelers apply for a visa online in advance and cross the border without waiting. The app was developed by the ZR's largest IT company owned by Zarjana Vloszek-Drogoslavova (daughter of Rosczislav); development took two years and cost 1.5 million euros, funded by a UNOMFUP grant. The app was buggy and unreliable, often slowing down and complicating border crossings rather than speeding them up, and the personal data it collected quickly ended up in the hands of cybercriminals. Within a few months the EU banned ZgurPass over its unsafe handling of personal data, and the app remains unavailable. In 2026, Vloszek-Drogoslavova announced work on ZgurPass 2.0, powered by AI, and requested a 6 million euro grant for it; the project's fate remains undecided.

The visa is valid for 60 days, and re-entry requires a gap of at least 30 days. The stamp usually goes on a loose sheet rather than in the passport itself, to avoid spoiling it with the seal of an unrecognized state — but this is far from guaranteed and depends on the border guard's mood. A simplified entry procedure applies to residents of the border areas, as well as to the special economic zone of Gnilovody, a resort the Zgurian authorities are eager to draw tourists to. Entry to Gnilovody is visa-free, but foreigners are barred from leaving it for the rest of the country.

Entering the Deglian Republic

Entry procedures for the Deglian Republic are stricter: the visa must be obtained in advance, and only on the strength of an invitation from a government or commercial organization accredited by DR counterintelligence. Like the ZR, the DR has no consulates or visa centers abroad, so the process starts with corresponding with the inviting organization to secure that invitation. The application itself is then mailed to the Deglian foreign ministry; once an accreditation letter arrives, you present it at one of the land crossings with Romania, Serbia, or Croatia. The process resembles entry into the Neutral Zone, except that tourist visits are permitted. A typical waiting time is two to three months, 10 to 20% applications refused. The visa is valid for 60 days, extendable by 30, and re-entry requires a gap of at least 30 days.

Unlike the ZR, every foreigner entering the DR is formally placed under counterintelligence surveillance. That means that once you're across the border — and in this case the visa, incidentally, is always stamped directly into the passport — you can expect an interview with a counterintelligence officer, a search of your phone and belongings, and, in suspicious cases, a full body search. Your phone is required to have an app installed that tracks your location (officially) and, presumably, quite a bit more. You are assigned a counterintelligence handler who calls you daily to ask where you've been, what you've done, and who you've met. If they forget to call, you're obligated to call in yourself before the end of the day. Violations are punished with fines and deportation, followed by a lifetime ban from the DR.

Intrusive as it is, this counterintelligence attention can occasionally work in your favor, because DR intelligence also takes an interest in foreigners. That interest usually takes the form of recruitment attempts, and since Deglian intelligence operates on a shoestring budget, recruitment tends to mean blackmail. Casual new acquaintances may steer you into some minor offense — a small bribe, illegal currency exchange, sex with someone almost of legal age — and then offer you a choice: spy for Deglian intelligence, or face a long stretch in a Deglian prison. Your counterintelligence handler can actually save you here, since blackmail is itself a crime, and DR counterintelligence and intelligence have a long-standing rivalry that leaves counterintelligence more than happy to catch the other service breaking the law. Naturally, you'll have to leave the country as soon as possible, and returning is strongly discouraged even in the unlikely event you'd want to.

Moving between the zones

There are no direct crossings between the ZR and the DR; all travel between the two republics passes through the Neutral Zone. A foreigner holding a UNOMFUP visa may travel freely across all of FUP — not just the Neutral Zone where they entered, but both republics as well. This is a valuable privilege, but worth noting: these are exactly the foreigners Deglian intelligence is most eager to recruit, and they have no counterintelligence handler to bail them out (though the UNOMFUP can be called on, and sometimes it helps).

A ZR or DR visa is valid only in the corresponding republic and grants no access to the Neutral Zone. Every crossing point into the zone is guarded from both sides. Local residents are usually waved through without a document check, but anyone who looks or sounds like a foreigner will be checked without exception. Passing for a local is possible, but if you manage to slip into the Neutral Zone on a Deglian visa and from there into the ZR, and you're caught once inside the ZR, expect immediate deportation and a lifetime blacklisting. The reverse — getting caught in the DR on a Zgurian visa — carries far graver consequences.
