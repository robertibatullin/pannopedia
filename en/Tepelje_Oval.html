Tepelje Oval

Tepelje Oval (Zgurian: Tepeljski oval; Deglian: Tepeĺski oval; Glagolitic: ⰕⰅⰒⰅⰎⰠⰔⰍⰊ ⰑⰂⰀⰎ) is a locality in Former Upper Pannonia near the village of Tepelje, now within the Zgurian Republic. It is an oval area running north to south, 8.5 km long and 4.9 km wide. It is widely believed to be an anomalous zone, though no one agrees on what exactly makes it anomalous.

On 8 May 1973, engineering units of the People's Liberation Army arrived in Tepelje without warning and began putting up a wire perimeter west of the village. Nothing unusual had preceded the move. No one explained anything to the locals: the soldiers themselves knew nothing, and the officers cited secrecy. The perimeter followed no natural, administrative, or historical boundary. It enclosed part of Tepelje's farmland, hills covered in beech forest, a stretch of country road, a roadside chapel, and a livestock burial ground — nothing of any evident value or scientific interest.

Access to the fenced area was forbidden on pain of death, and the perimeter was closely guarded by state security (NON) troops, but no work of any kind went on inside it, or at least none visible from outside. A group of hikers who happened to be inside the zone were detained by the soldiers when they came out to the checkpoint and driven away; what became of them afterward is unknown. The state gradually paid Tepelje's residents compensation for the confiscated land, a process that dragged on for years and cost the villagers no small struggle with the bureaucracy.

In 1990, after the fall of the Communist regime, amid a sweeping reorganization and downsizing of the NON, the guard was withdrawn and the area opened to the public. Over the following decades the Tepelje Oval was thoroughly investigated, but no structures, unusual objects, or natural anomalies were ever found there. The NON's files declassified to date refer to the Oval only as “protected natural zone 11450,” and shed no further light on the mystery — just repetitive guard reports on minor incidents, like random hikers or livestock approaching the perimeter.

According to the recollections of an anonymous NON officer, recorded in the early 1990s, “Zone 11450” was the personal project of Boris Gowbka, deputy minister of state security and son of Supreme Leader Severin Gowbka. Boris alone knew the Oval's secret, and he took it to his grave when he died — or was killed — in March 1978. That is where the known facts end.

Rumors about the “zone” started at once, and theories multiplied: a secret government bunker or nuclear test site was being built near Tepelje; a gold deposit had been found; a terrible epidemic had broken out; an alien ship had crashed; a rift had opened into a parallel universe; an ancient evil had awoken. Once it became clear that nothing was being built inside the Oval and that all the activity amounted to guarding the perimeter, the rational theories had to be dropped, leaving only the fantastical ones. These were what the many enthusiasts who flocked to the “zone” after it opened in 1990 set out to test — and, as noted, they found nothing. This has not stopped legends of the Tepelje Oval's wonders, mysteries, and healing or harmful properties from persisting, growing, and accumulating detail.

In 2016, PAPER, the research center at FUPFOR headquarters, mounted its own expedition to the Oval. It was the initiative of General Dougherty, the peacekeeping mission's new commander and an enthusiast of anomalous phenomena. The results have not been made public.

The zone is uninhabited today. The former fields and dirt road have been completely overgrown by birch and alder forest with dense, near-impassable undergrowth. Most of Tepelje's residents had left the village by the early 1990s, and its last old-timers died out in the 2000s. Its houses stand abandoned, slowly falling into ruin. The former guard barracks now serves as a temporary base for the tourists and researchers who occasionally visit the Oval.
