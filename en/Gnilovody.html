Gnilovody

Gnilovody (Zgurian and Deglian: Gnilovody; Glagolitic: ⰃⰐⰊⰎⰑⰂⰑⰄⰬ) is a natural region and municipality in Former Upper Pannonia, occupying a valley studded with hot mineral springs. It has no continuous urban fabric; instead, a scatter of spa-resort complexes and their attendant settlements fills the valley floor. The municipal territory covers 11.5 km², with a permanent population of about 10,000, though the number of resident holidaymakers at any given time ranges from 20,000 to 50,000. The name — literally “rotten waters” — comes from the reek of hydrogen sulfide given off by the sulfurous springs. Gnilovody is the administrative seat of Gnilovody County, the westernmost county of the Zgurian Republic, bordering Austria and Hungary.

Nature

Geology and microclimate

Gnilovody occupies an endorheic valley in hill country, roughly 10 km long and up to 2 km wide at its broadest. The valley is a down-dropped block of crust formed along a tectonic fault. Millions of years ago a shallow, warm Pannonian Sea covered the area and left behind thick beds of limestone, with a sulfur-rich gypsum layer underneath. Vertical displacement along the fault tilted the block, raising the southern side relative to the northern and exposing the gypsum layer along the southern slope.

Surface groundwater percolates through the limestone to a depth of 1–2 km, where geothermal heat warms it and dissolves calcium carbonate; it then rises back to the surface along fracture lines. Some water penetrates further, into the gypsum layer, where high temperature and pressure — aided by sulfate-reducing bacteria — trigger a reaction that releases hydrogen sulfide. This sulfur-laden water likewise rises to the surface, emerging on the southern side where the fault has exposed the gypsum bed.

The result is two distinct sets of springs: numerous calcium-magnesium-carbonate springs on the northern, limestone slope, and sulfurous ones on the southern slope, both ranging from 40°C to 75°C. Their waters converge in the valley center to form Lake Gnilovody, itself endorheic, fed by gentle runoff down the slopes. As the carbonate spring water cools and meets the air, calcium carbonate precipitates out, building cascades of travertine terraces as picturesque as those at Pamukkale or Plitvice. The hydrogen sulfide from the sulfurous springs partly evaporates and is partly broken down by thiobacteria, so the lake itself ends up fresher and less mineralized than the springs that feed it. Its water temperature holds steady year-round between +22°C and +28°C.

The lake bed is blanketed in several meters of therapeutic thermal mud — a peaty sapropel. Every autumn the surrounding vegetation sheds huge quantities of organic matter — leaves, needles, stems — which the oxygen-starved, sulfide-rich depths never fully decompose. Instead it slowly “cures,” turning into a soft, oily black or dark grey silt laced with precipitated calcium, gypsum crystals, and organic acids.

The constantly heated water turns the lake into a giant thermal battery. In winter, once air temperatures drop below freezing, thick fog rolls off the lake and blankets the entire valley; the fog layer acts as insulation, trapping infrared radiation. In summer the valley instead swelters under extreme humidity.

Flora and fauna

The thermal springs have given rise to a distinctive ecosystem thick with endemic species. The banks of the carbonate streams, built up from pale, porous travertine, are carpeted in vivid moss; blue-green algae tolerant of the hot water tint it turquoise. The sulfurous springs support extremophile bacteria: their beds and streamside rocks are coated in slick bacterial mats ranging in color from toxic yellow and bright orange to blood red and emerald green. Snottites — pale, ropy strands of slime formed by bacteria that convert hydrogen sulfide into sulfuric acid — hang from overhanging rocks and driftwood. The resulting acidification leaves the soil around these streams too caustic for ordinary plants; native sulfur crystallizes on the bare ground and rocks at the water's edge, forming brittle lemon-yellow crusts that faintly smoke on frosty days.

In the shallows, the lake bed is carpeted with beds of submerged plants such as eelgrass and water milfoil. The water's high concentration of carbon dioxide from the springs drives explosive plant growth, producing dense underwater jungles. Where the water deepens and light grows scarce, plants give way to bacterial and algal mats — velvety carpets of filamentous green and blue-green algae along with filamentous sulfur bacteria, which knit together into a dense “cushion” that binds the mud in place.

Before human intervention, the lakeshore was dense with reed beds, white water lilies bloomed on the water, and relict ferns such as the marsh fern grew a little further back on the banks. Grass snakes, frogs, and newts thrived in what was, for them, a permanent springtime. The lake held an endemic carp species adapted to the warm, faintly sulfurous water, while beech forest covered the valley slopes between the thermal streams.

In the 1870s Adalfrid Attila Bregovi, the wealthy traveler who owned most of the valley's land, decided to turn it into an open-air “tropical area” and introduced a wide range of tropical and subtropical species collected on his expeditions. Many of the imports — birds and orchids among them — died out the first winter, unable to withstand the cold air, but the aquatic and shoreline species adapted, producing a bizarre hybrid biocenosis.

Bald cypress (Taxodium distichum), imported from North America, took to the shoreline perfectly; the lake is now ringed by a forest of these towering conifers, their breathing roots — pneumatophores — jutting up out of the water. They shed their needles in winter, but even bare, their massive trunks look monumental looming out of the fog. In the shallows, Indian lotus (Nelumbo nucifera) has crowded out the native water lilies, spreading in summer into vast stands of huge leaves and pink blossoms. Further from the water, on ground kept warm and dry from below, dense bamboo groves have taken hold.

Poeciliid fish — guppies and mosquitofish, released both for mosquito control and for decoration — have multiplied beyond counting; they feed on insect larvae and now anchor the food chain. Cichlids such as tilapia, large African fish that owe their survival to the lake's steady warmth, have become the dominant species at its center, wiping out the native endemic fish entirely in the process. Red-eared sliders have likewise survived in the warm water, though in winter they have to crowd into the thermal springs themselves, since the outside air is too cold for them.

Native predators — herons, otters, kingfishers — thrive on the fish-rich, never-freezing lake, which supplies them with food year-round. But they compete with a handful of introduced predators that have likewise held on: the Burmese python (Python bivittatus), which gets through winter by retreating into deep burrows or the unfrozen lake bottom, and the desert monitor (Varanus griseus), which simply hibernates.

The single most vivid presence in the local ecosystem is the Japanese macaque (Macaca fuscata). In summer they play in the surviving beech groves; in winter they barely leave the warm pools and travertine basins, sitting neck-deep in the water for hours amid thick clouds of steam. They have learned to dig up lotus roots, catch frogs, and dive for cichlids, but they much prefer the easier pickings offered by people — begging, stealing, rummaging through trash, or raiding orchards. Tourists find them charming and adorable on first encounter; the feeling rarely survives a second one.

History

The Gnilovody valley has been inhabited since deep antiquity, as shown by archaeological finds preserved in the lake mud; its oxygen-free environment keeps not just bone but skin and wood in good condition. The earliest finds date to the early Neolithic. A medieval legend places St. George's slaying of the dragon at this very spot: the sulfurous springs, it holds, rise from the rotting flesh of the dragon buried deep beneath the lake, while the carbonate springs are the tears of the princess he rescued. A monastery of St. George was already standing on the lake's southern shore by the eleventh century; the Bregovo prince-bishops later granted it rights over the entire valley.

The healing properties of the thermal waters were likewise recognized early. In the medieval period they were held to be miraculous and credited to St. George, and the valley drew pilgrims from across the Kingdom of Hungary and beyond. The Ottomans, who took Gnilovody after the Battle of Mohács, also venerated St. George — as Jirjis, in the Islamic tradition — and valued the springs' medicinal properties; the monastery kept its privileges and went on attracting pilgrims, Muslims among them. But the valley sat squarely on the Austro-Ottoman frontier and was repeatedly ravaged by the armies of both sides, falling into decline until it was all but depopulated by the close of the seventeenth century.

After the Austrian reconquest of Upper Pannonia, Gnilovody became part of the County of Upper Pannonia. The monastery of St. George was restored, but the crown parceled out part of its lands to secular landowners, the noble House of Bregovi among them. A fortress was built on the southern shore above the sulfurous springs: Zsluty Vody (“Yellow Waters”). Through the late eighteenth and first half of the nineteenth century, the Bregovis — never ones to shy from commerce — built modern spa establishments on their holdings. The monks of St. George, with only their medieval infirmaries to offer, could not compete; they gradually sold off most of their lands to the Bregovis, keeping only the central monastery at Svjutodjordjevo.

By the second half of the nineteenth century the Bregovis owned nearly the entire valley, which is what let Adalfrid Attila build his tropical park. Gnilovody grew into a sprawling, profitable resort dotted with private villas, sanatoriums, and every kind of pleasure establishment, and stayed that way until the Communists nationalized it in 1945. The large sanatoriums became state health resorts for the working masses, while the villas turned into dachas and rest homes for the nomenklatura.

After the fall of the Communist regime, Gnilovody briefly became a battleground of privatization, complete with armed turf wars, and then a combat zone during the civil war itself. Zgurian forces had secured firm control of it by 1994, but the resort trade only began to recover after the 1995 ceasefire. Several more rounds of privatization and ownership shuffling later, most of the sanatoriums ended up in the hands of various members of the Vloszek family.

Current state

Thirty years after the civil war, Gnilovody is a thoroughly developed, well-run resort. Eager to draw foreign tourists, the Zgurian authorities secured it “simplified access zone” status — the only part of Former Upper Pannonia any foreigner can enter without a visa or special permit, a paid reservation at any sanatorium, spa, or hotel in the valley being all that's required. Direct buses run daily from Eisenkirchen and Kecskehegyszentmiklós to the bus station in Zsluty Vody, the municipality's main transport hub and administrative center.

Visitors are offered the full range of spa treatments, mineral water for drinking, outdoor hot-spring bathing year-round, boat trips on the lake, and tours of the valley's singular “tropical” forest. Swimming in the lake itself is not advised, given the deep bottom mud and the risk of python attack. Because Gnilovody has to compete with equally well-developed resorts just across the border in Hungary, its owners employ every dubious trick in the book to lure tourists in; every major hotel and sanatorium runs a casino and an “adults only” zone.

Because the Vloszeks have every interest in keeping tourist spending inside Gnilovody rather than anywhere else, there is no free exit from the valley. It is ringed by a wire fence, and Zgurian border guards posted at every way out turn back anyone carrying a foreign passport; the only way for a foreigner to leave is the scheduled bus to Eisenkirchen or Kecskehegyszentmiklós. The arrangement draws frequent complaints from tourists who say they “feel a bit like inmates of a concentration camp.”

Review sites carry plenty of other complaints. Tourists rave about the scenery while noting that the quality of service falls well short of the prices, and that the services on offer are often outright fraudulent, if not predatory. Guests are pestered by macaques that roam freely and show no fear of people; unsettled by monitor lizards and pythons that regularly wander onto hotel grounds; annoyed by pushy vendors and prostitutes; and terrified by the local gangsters for whom Gnilovody is a favorite getaway — one whose idea of relaxation always seems to involve raucous parties, blaring music, gunfire into the air, and hooliganism of the most outrageous kind. The hotel owners and municipal administration, for their part, appear to spend far more time and effort scrubbing such reviews off the internet than fixing the underlying problems.
