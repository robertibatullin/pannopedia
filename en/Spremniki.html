Spremniki

Spremniki (lit. “those who are ready” or “those prepared”; masc. sg. spremnik, fem. sg. spremnica) are a militarized nationalist youth subculture of Former Upper Pannonia, with both Zgurian and Deglian branches. They emerged from the late socialist period, reached their peak during the civil war of 1992–1995 and its immediate aftermath, and have declined substantially since approximately 2005, yielding ground in the urban milieu to the sedmaki subculture.

Origins

The spremniks grew from two overlapping late-socialist sources: official paramilitary youth organizations and the football ultra movement.

The official organizations — which included a formation for teenagers commonly known as the Future Partisans — combined political instruction with physical training, fieldcraft, and ceremonial drill. They also maintained traditions of communal living and collective discipline inherited in part from the “House of Childhood” system: the state-organized upbringing of orphans that had produced a cohort thoroughly habituated to closed-community existence. As the Communist regime weakened through the 1980s, the official organizations retained their structures while their ideological content emptied out. The space was filled by ethnic nationalism.

The football connection was less institutional but equally formative. The ultra groups attached to the Zgurian Sokol and the Deglian Dynamo clubs provided the stadium confrontations, the color symbolism, and the territorial discipline that the emerging spremniks absorbed and amplified. Alongside both, informal fight clubs — semi-secret organized combat groups — were operating in both communities and supplied a third strand of recruits.

The term spremniks appears in both communities from the late 1980s. By 1992, when the civil war began, the subculture was already sufficiently established to supply organized, trained personnel to both sides.

Appearance

The spremniks maintain a uniform appearance, treated as a matter of collective discipline rather than personal expression. Both men and women wear hair closely cropped or fully shaved; a beret at the prescribed angle; a tight olive tank shirt; military-style camouflage trousers; heavy ankle boots; and accessories in the national colors of their community — blue and white on the Deglian side, red and white on the Zgurian. A combat knife worn visibly on the belt is mandatory. The body is typically tattooed with national symbols (Zgurian falcon, Deglian mouflon, Glagolitic Z or D characters), mottos and emblems of individual spremnik groups.

Lifestyle

The spremnik personal regime is ascetic. The list of prohibited substances is absolute: alcohol, tobacco, narcotics, and any stimulant including energy drinks. Physical fitness is an explicit collective value; both men and women train regularly in combat sports, distance running, and calisthenics, and are expected to maintain muscular physiques.

Within the community, collective naturism — Freikörperkultur — is practiced and encouraged for both sexes. It is strictly an internal matter and never displayed publicly. For outside observers encountering this combination for the first time — the martial exterior, the knives, the nationalist iconography, the nude communal exercise — the effect can be disorienting. Spremniks do not acknowledge any incongruity. They cite Spartan practice as the relevant precedent. The actual root is more prosaic: the miljaniczi communes in Houses of Childhood operated under similar norms of communal physical life for ideological and logistical reasons, and the early spremniks — many of them products of those institutions — inherited those norms alongside the rest of the package.

Ideology

The spremniks are routinely characterized as neo-Nazis. They reject the label, and their objections have some substance, though they do not fully survive examination.

The nationalism is ethnic rather than racial in the classic sense: its target is defined by Pannonian community membership, not by any physical distinction that could be reliably made, since Zgurians and Deglians are physically indistinguishable. Non-Slavic minorities — Roma, Jews, Muslims — are regarded with suspicion or low-level hostility, but they are not the defining ideological enemy and organized violence against them is not a spremnik practice. The canonical enemy is the other Pannonian community: Zgurians for Deglian spremniks, Deglians for Zgurian.

This enmity presents a practical problem. UN police enforce a standing prohibition on entry to the Neutral Zone for individuals displaying identifiable spremnik markers — first of all tattoos — without requiring any further documentation or pretext. A Deglian spremnik who wishes to confront his Zgurian counterpart is effectively prevented by institutional geography. The enemy actually encountered is therefore a sedmak of the same ethnicity: someone whose undisciplined lifestyle represents, in the spremnik analysis, the precise failure of national character that the subculture exists to correct. This confrontation is conducted with considerable energy.

Gender and sexuality

The treatment of gender and sexuality within the spremniks is consistently noted by outside observers and difficult to reconcile with the neo-Nazi comparison.

Women are full members of spremnik groups, with no formal distinction of role or rank. They train alongside men, participate in the same physical ordeals, and hold positions of authority within group structures. The explicit rationale is that the national struggle requires every capable person. The informal tone of address between male and female members is, by the standards of comparable subcultures elsewhere, notably unpatronizing. Such attitudes most likely originate from the PRUP army, where conscription service was mandatory for men and women alike.

Same-sex relationships, between both men and women, are accepted within the subculture without social comment or organizational sanction. The framing is not progressive. Spremniks have nothing to do with LGBTQ+ subculture and generally despise it. This is yet another Spartan reference to the military utility of close bonds among fighters. The miljaniczi precedent is also cited, with some accuracy — long institutional separation of the sexes in the commune system had produced similar outcomes by practical circumstance rather than ideological choice.

The result is a subculture whose external presentation is aggressively nationalist and whose internal social arrangements are, by regional standards, quite liberal.

Brand prohibitions

The standard Zguro-Deglian color prohibition is observed by spremniks with stricter consistency than in the general population. Deglian spremniks refuse any product, item of equipment, or piece of clothing coloured red-and-white; Zgurian spremniks apply the mirror prohibition to blue and white. The restriction extends from consumer goods into combat gear, tool handles, webbing, and digital interfaces. The full elaboration of prohibited brands — sportswear, fast-food chains, and technology brands — parallels the general Zguro-Deglian system, applied without exception. Unlike sedmaki, the ascetic lifestyle eliminates alcohol, tobacco, and energy drink brand loyalties entirely.

Relations with civilians

The spremniks' relationship with the civilian population of their own community is consistently ambivalent, and the ambivalence is structural rather than incidental.

On the enforcement side, spremniks treat violations of community cultural codes as a collective concern rather than a private matter. A café or kiosk found to be stocking brands subject to the color prohibition — Coca-Cola in the Deglian zone, or Oreo biscuits in the Zgurian — can expect a visit, followed by the removal or destruction of the offending stock if the owner does not act first. The visit is rarely announced. Establishments that have received one visit generally do not require a second.

On the protection side, spremniks are the most reliable source of physical intervention against sedmak violence in areas where they maintain a presence. The sedmaki subculture produces high rates of street harassment, assault, and opportunistic crime; the formal authorities respond inconsistently. Spremniks respond reliably. A woman being followed or assaulted by sedmaks in a street where spremniks are present will be defended; the sedmaks will not be warned first. This function is valued by civilians who would not otherwise express any sympathy for the subculture.

The result is a community posture toward the spremniks that is widespread but rarely articulated honestly: tolerated because useful, not liked because dangerous, and deferred to in specific practical circumstances by people who would describe themselves as having nothing to do with them.

Decline and current status

The spremniks were most numerous and influential during the civil war and the decade following the 1995 ceasefire. The practical demand for organized, ideologically committed young people willing to bear arms was at its height during the war; the aftermath sustained the subculture through continued territorial tension, particularly on the Deglian side following the 1999 coup and the imposition of permanent martial law.

The decline from approximately 2005 reflects the growing dominance of the sedmaki subculture, which offered urban youth a recognizable identity without the demands — dietary, physical, ideological, organizational — of spremnik membership. The sedmaki also developed a cultural presence in music, nightlife, and street commerce that the spremniks, by ideological disposition, did not. The spremniks had always defined themselves implicitly as a wartime emergency formation; the ceasefire, once it became permanent, made sustained recruitment progressively harder to justify.

The subculture continues to exist in both zones, with influence exceeding its membership numbers. Several nationalist political organizations and veterans' groups maintain effective spremnik memberships and spremnik values in institutional form. The Military Academy of the Deglian Republic, housed in the former Dom Djectva, trains officer candidates in an environment sharing significant features with spremnik culture — a continuity that, on both sides, is regarded as natural.
