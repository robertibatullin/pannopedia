Domenyk Raczev — DRAFT

Domenyk Janoszev Raczev (Zgurian; Deglian: Domenyk Janošev Račev; Glagolitic: ⰄⰑⰏⰅⰐⰬⰍ ⰡⰐⰑⰞⰅⰂ ⰓⰀⰝⰅⰂ; 1909–1974) was an Upper Pannonian writer, one of the classic authors of Upper Pannonian socialist realism and a lifelong theorist and practitioner of collective literary creation — the idea that group authorship could reforge a wayward criminal individual into a true creator and a builder of socialism.

Early years (1909–1932)

Domenyk Raczev was born in Vronovica into an educated and fairly well-off bourgeois family — his father owned a small musical instrument factory. His parents were of Deglian origin, and Janosz Raczev was sympathetic to Deglian nationalism. Domenyk himself leaned left from a young age, and as a student at the New University (1927–1932) belonged to a semi-clandestine Communist circle, though he never joined the Communist Party itself.

It was around this time that Raczev developed an interest in avant-garde literature. Following the fashionable trends of the day, he founded the “First Vronovica Brigade of Literary Labor,” a circle of like-minded students who set out to practice collective authorship. The Brigade quickly fell apart over incompatible writing styles, personal quarrels, and disagreements about Trotsky, but despite the failed experiment, Raczev kept his faith in collective literary work for the rest of his life.

Emigration (1932–1945)

Although Domenyk had never been politically active, the rise of Drogan Glovacz's dictatorship put him at risk: the repression of leftists was brutal and indiscriminate, and it often fell on peripheral sympathizers just like him. He left for Paris as soon as he graduated in 1932. There he published a collection of poems at his own expense — or rather his parents' expense, since he does not appear to have held any job. The handful of reviewers who noticed it offered grudging praise for his skillful adaptation of Brecht's and Mayakovsky's techniques to Pannonian, but the poems brought Raczev no recognition beyond the small circle of émigré intellectuals.

Recognition came after Raczev traveled to the USSR in 1933 and published a documentary account of his impressions the following year. The itinerary was extensive: the young writer visited not only Moscow and Leningrad but also the construction site of the White Sea–Baltic Canal, the factories of Magnitogorsk, and collective farms in Ukraine. Who arranged and paid for the trip is unknown, but the resulting book was rapturous, praising everything he saw — from the carefree life of Ukrainian kolkhoz workers to the reforging of prisoners at Belbaltlag. He appears to have genuinely and quite sincerely taken every Potemkin village his Soviet guides showed him at face value.

Books of this kind were still a novelty at the time. Translated into French, English, and Russian, On the Roads of Red Russia brought Raczev a measure of fame — and cost him his relationship with his father. Janosz sent his son a furious letter disowning him as a “traitor and a Moscow spy.” The rift may well have been forced on him by the political climate of the Kingdom of Upper Pannonia after 1934, but whatever the cause, Domenyk stopped receiving money from home, and the book's royalties were not enough to live on. For the first time in his life, he struggled to find work, eventually landing a job in London as a translator for an international finance firm with a branch in Upper Pannonia.

In 1936, Raczev moved to England, where he combined his work as a translator with occasional publications in the émigré press. After the outbreak of the Second World War, his company shut down its Upper Pannonian operations. Exactly how Raczev supported himself in the following years is unclear — official biographies pass over the question in silence. He probably kept working as a Pannonian translator, but now for British intelligence or counterintelligence. Domenyk maintained no ties with the Pannonian government-in-exile, which he considered fascist, though he was friendly with British left-wing writers, including George Orwell. In 1944 he enthusiastically welcomed the Red Army's liberation of Upper Pannonia, and shortly after the war ended, in the summer of 1945, he returned home — “to serve the liberated Pannonian people with his pen.”
