Domenyk Raczev

Domenyk Janoszev Raczev (Zgurian; Deglian: Domenyk Janošev Račev; Glagolitic: ⰄⰑⰏⰅⰐⰬⰍ ⰡⰐⰑⰞⰅⰂ ⰓⰀⰝⰅⰂ; 1909–1972) was an Upper Pannonian writer, one of the classic authors of Upper Pannonian socialist realism and a lifelong theorist and practitioner of collective literary creation — the idea that group authorship could reforge a wayward criminal individual into a true creator and a builder of socialism.

Early years (1909–1932)

Domenyk Raczev was born in Vronovica into an educated and fairly well-off bourgeois family — his father owned a small musical instrument factory. His parents were of Deglian origin, and Janosz Raczev was sympathetic to Deglian nationalism. Domenyk himself leaned left from a young age, and as a student at the New University (1927–1932) belonged to a semi-clandestine Communist circle, though he never joined the Communist Party itself.

It was around this time that Raczev developed an interest in avant-garde literature. Following the fashionable trends of the day, he founded the “First Vronovica Worker Brigade of Literary Labor,” a circle of like-minded students who set out to practice collective authorship. The Brigade quickly fell apart over incompatible writing styles, personal quarrels, and disagreements about Trotsky, but despite the failed experiment, Raczev kept his faith in collective literary work for the rest of his life.

Emigration (1932–1945)

Although Domenyk had never been politically active, the rise of Drogan Glovacz's dictatorship put him at risk: the repression of leftists was brutal and indiscriminate, and it often fell on peripheral sympathizers just like him. He left for Paris as soon as he graduated in 1932.

There he became friends with the sculptor Svjetovit Bregovi, the Czech Communist journalist Egon Erwin Kisch, and the German émigré writer Anna Seghers, and published a collection of poems at his own expense — or rather his parents' expense, since he does not appear to have held any job. The handful of reviewers who noticed it offered grudging praise for his skillful adaptation of Brecht's and Mayakovsky's techniques to Pannonian, but the poems brought Raczev no recognition beyond the small circle of émigré intellectuals.

Recognition came after Raczev traveled to the USSR in 1933 and published a documentary account of his impressions the following year. The itinerary was extensive: the young writer visited not only Moscow and Leningrad but also the construction site of the White Sea–Baltic Canal, the factories of Magnitogorsk, and collective farms in Ukraine. Who arranged and paid for the trip is unknown, but the resulting book was rapturous, praising everything he saw — from the prosperous life of Ukrainian kolkhoz workers to the reforging of prisoners at Belbaltlag. He appears to have genuinely and quite sincerely taken every Potemkin village his Soviet guides showed him at face value.

Books of this kind were still a novelty at the time. Translated into French, English, and Russian, On the Roads of Red Russia brought Raczev a measure of fame — and cost him his relationship with his father. Janosz sent his son a furious letter disowning him as a “traitor and a Moscow spy.” The rift may well have been forced on him by the political climate of the Kingdom of Upper Pannonia after 1934, but whatever the cause, Domenyk stopped receiving money from home, and the book's royalties were not enough to live on. For the first time in his life, he struggled to find work, eventually landing a job in London as a translator for an international finance firm with a branch in Upper Pannonia.

In 1936, Raczev moved to England, where he combined his work as a translator with occasional publications in the émigré press. After the outbreak of the Second World War, his company shut down its Upper Pannonian operations. Exactly how Raczev supported himself in the following years is unclear — official biographies pass over the question in silence. Domenyk maintained no ties with the Pannonian government-in-exile, which he considered fascist, though he was friendly with British left-wing writers, including George Orwell, Stephen Spender, and Naomi Mitchison. In 1944 he enthusiastically welcomed the Red Army's liberation of Upper Pannonia, and shortly after the war ended, in the summer of 1945, he returned home — “to serve the liberated Pannonian people with his pen.”

Early years in the People's Republic (1945–1950)

Upon his return, Raczev promptly joined the Communist Party of Upper Pannonia (CPUP) and threw himself into public life, founding the Union of Communist Writers and securing official backing for it. Yet this was purely his own initiative — one that the Communist government of Severyn Gowbka merely looked on with favor. In the early postwar years, Raczev was nowhere near being the regime's State Writer No. 1: he simply lacked the literary weight for that role. A book of poems, a documentary travelogue, and a handful of journalistic pieces — however loyal — were nowhere near enough. Far more distinguished writers, such as Helena Prox and Nobel Prize nominee Viktor Kostanj, had likewise taken a pro-Communist stance, and, unlike Raczev, who had spent the war years sitting out an ambiguous existence in England, they had stayed in the country and supported the Resistance.

That very ambiguity, however, worked in his favor. In 1950, a group of former Resistance fighters — monarchists and supporters of the London government-in-exile — attempted to assassinate Gowbka. The plot failed and triggered a wave of mass repression, aimed above all at former Resistance members. Writers were among the casualties of this campaign, including Prox and Kostanj, both sent to the Mjedno concentration camp. For Domenyk Raczev, this was the turning point — and he seized it brilliantly. He secured an audience with Gowbka and laid out his plan: to establish an “institution for reforging criminal writers through collective literary labor for the benefit of the people” — in other words, a writers' sharashka.

The Beksziczi camp and the zenith of literary fame (1950–1964)

Gowbka knew little about literature. By his own admission, reading put him to sleep. But he knew that Stalin — his idol at the time, whom Gowbka even imitated in appearance — attached enormous political importance to fiction. Raczev's proposal sounded bold, revolutionary, properly Communist. Gowbka gave the necessary orders, and the work got underway. Over the course of 1950, an abandoned noble estate called Beksziczi, south of Vronovica, was converted into a camp, and around a hundred imprisoned writers and journalists were transferred there, from a list drawn up by Raczev himself and cleared with the Ministry of People's Security (NON). Kostanj and Prox were among them.

According to the memoirs of Beksziczi inmates — and a great many of them wrote memoirs — conditions at the camp were practically a “health resort”: decent food, lax discipline, and no physical labor. The writers' only obligation was to write. They were organized into brigades, each assigned a single project, typically a novel. Raczev set the themes, reviewed the output, and did the final editorial pass himself. The most important, “flagship” novels were published under his own name; lesser ones appeared under the names of other members of the Writers' Union presidium — loyal Raczev cronies who, as a rule, had never written a line in their lives. Authors who fell short of their daily character quota, or turned in substandard work, faced transfer to an ordinary camp — the harshest punishment there was. Viktor Kostanj, for instance, went on strike in 1963 and was transferred to the dreaded “land reclamation” labor camp of Czistovodno in Xljubiscza, where he died that same year.

In this fashion, Raczev “wrote” a thick novel every year or two. Together they formed a sprawling epic covering the whole history of Upper Pannonia from the Middle Ages to the present era of socialist construction — naturally, in full accordance with the Marxist-Gowbkaist view of history. For three key novels in the series — Smoky Dawn on Gowbka's heroic revolutionary youth, Winners Don't Cry on the liberation of Upper Pannonia by the People's Liberation Army under Gowbka's command, and Turbine Symphony on the construction of the Gowbka Hydroelectric Plant, conceived by Gowbka and built under Gowbka's leadership — Raczev received the Gowbka Prize three times, from Gowbka's own hands. Critics hailed him as “the Pannonian Gorky.” Raczev himself never thought of himself as either a plagiarist or an exploiter of involuntary labor. Judging by his suicide note, he was genuinely proud of what he had done, regarding himself as a benefactor of imprisoned writers and the leader of a creative collective. Raczev funneled most of his enormous royalties into the camp's treasury, which paid for decent food for the inmates, the books and periodicals they needed for their work, and even the occasional touring musician or actor.

Closure of the camp (1964)

Raczev ran the literary side of Beksziczi and officially held the title of “production director,” giving him decisive influence over the inmates' daily and working routine — but the camp itself still belonged to NON, and its guards were state security officers. Surviving declassified records suggest he was in constant conflict with them. Both sides bombarded Gowbka with complaints: Raczev argued that writers were sensitive souls who needed delicate handling, while the NON men complained about his permissiveness and reported that the prisoners feared nothing and were having subversive conversations. Eventually the Supreme Leader grew tired of the squabbling. He saw little propaganda value in Raczev's output anyway: abroad, the novels were a laughingstock, and at home people read them only out of duty.

There was also a more compelling reason to shut the camp down. By then, inmates had begun to be released: Helena Prox, for one, was freed in 1960 after serving most of her ten-year sentence at Beksziczi. Strict secrecy notwithstanding, not everyone kept quiet, and rumors that “Doko” (Domenyk's diminutive) “owned a plantation of slave writers” spread ever further through literary and journalistic circles. News of Beksziczi's “health-resort” regime emboldened writers on the outside — the prospect of a prison term no longer seemed quite so frightening. NON's own reports from the early 1960s noted writers and journalists growing bolder and more outspoken by the year, and set against news of “thaw”-era developments in the USSR and elsewhere in the Communist bloc, this looked alarming. It was time to end the experiment before it spun out of control. In 1964, as part of a general crackdown following yet another assassination attempt, Gowbka ordered Beksziczi closed. The inmates were sent to serve out the rest of their sentences to Mjedno and other ordinary camps. The camp's buildings were demolished in the 1970s during the construction of the Seventh District.

Last years (1964-1972)

Raczev regarded Beksziczi as his life's work, and the closure hit him hard. The “Pannonian Gorky” himself faced no punishment: he kept every post, honor, and title he held, and “his” books went on being reprinted, translated, and taught in schools and universities — but his literary career was over. Aside from speeches at Writers' Union congresses, he never published another new text under his own name; perhaps this was a quiet form of protest. He did not, however, shirk his duties as a Party member, head of the Writers' Union, academician, and so on. Ill-suited to private life, he threw himself entirely into administrative and organizational work, which he treated with the utmost seriousness.

In the early 1970s, his health began to fail. Medical ethics of the time forbade doctors from disclosing a terminal diagnosis to the patient, so his doctor fed him some reassuring lie — forgetting that a Communist writer had a classical gymnasium education and could read Latin, and carelessly left his medical chart open on the desk. That was how Raczev learned his real diagnosis: inoperable prostate cancer with bone metastases. Years of slow, agonizing decline lay ahead of him. He decided to spare himself the suffering.

On 16 August 1972, in the study of his spacious apartment in the Sixth District, Raczev wrote a lengthy suicide note that amounted to a fairly candid memoir of his entire life. He set down the whole history of Beksziczi, his conflicts with NON, and his complicated relationship with Gowbka. The one thing he left out was what he had done in London between 1940 and 1945; he mentioned only, in passing, that he had “made his small contribution to the fight against fascism.” He asked forgiveness from the Party and from Gowbka personally for his “cowardly” exit, confessing that he had shown “a weakness unworthy of a Communist, giving in to his fear of physical pain.” Once he finished writing, he took poison. The official verdict cited only a “sudden death.” The national classic was buried with extraordinary pomp. The monument at Central Cemetery was carved by Raczev's old friend Svjetovit Bregovi — his last work; the sculptor died the following year.

Personal life and family

As a true Communist, Raczev held that private life counted for nothing next to labor for the common good. He scorned any excessive attention to personal feelings or domestic comfort as “philistinism,” threw himself entirely into work to the very end, and even took a certain pride in an ascetic lifestyle that shaded into outright unkemptness. According to the memoirs of Beksziczi inmates, he was solicitous toward them — always ready to hear their concerns and to shield them from the guards' abuses — yet just as ready to demand the work plan be met, and he sent anyone he judged unfit for the task off to an ordinary camp without a moment's hesitation.

Raczev is known to have had a few romances as a student and during his years abroad, though his suicide note does not mention them at all, focusing entirely on his political and literary career. In 1947, already pushing forty, he married his twenty-five-year-old secretary, Boleslava Husarova. There was probably little passion in the marriage — the couple addressed each other formally in their correspondence and discussed nothing but practical matters — but Boleslava proved the ideal writer's wife: she ran the household, handled all their private affairs, raised the children, and, above all, kept the archive in immaculate order. She preserved and meticulously catalogued not only every manuscript produced by the Beksziczi inmates but all the family correspondence, and even copies of her own reports on her husband to NON, whose informant she had been from the very start. The vast archive became available to researchers after Boleslava's death in 1992 and has still not been fully studied.

The Raczevs had two children: a daughter, Ursula (b. 1948), and a son, Konstantyn (“Kosta,” 1950–1995). Neither brought their father much joy. In his eyes, Ursula grew up a typical “philistine,” a golden-youth socialite indifferent to public affairs. In 1969 she married the fashionable novelist Ljubodar Vloszek, whom Raczev despised as a “hack scribbler” and regretted he could no longer send off for “re-education” to Beksziczi. The marriage did not last, in any case — the couple divorced in 1971 — and after her father's death Ursula lived as a wealthy heiress, keeping up an active social life and marrying more than once.

Kosta was even more of a disappointment. He showed a rebellious streak already in school, mocking his father's novels, and by his teens had refused to join the Young Fighters' League and was openly calling himself an anti-Communist. On a couple of occasions Raczev had to pull strings to get his son out of police custody — and, by his own account, only because his wife insisted; left to himself, he would have shipped the wayward boy off for “re-education” without a second thought. Raczev did not live to see the next chapter of Kosta's life — genuine dissidence, psychiatric confinement, and a meteoric political career in post-Communist Upper Pannonia. Had he known what was coming, he might well have killed his son with his own hands.
