Zguro-Deglian cultural differences

The worst offence you can make in the presence of a Zgurian or a Deglian is to say that the two peoples are essentially the same — sharing a language, a religion, a material culture, and an indistinguishable appearance. This observation is objectively true and is received as a grave insult by both. Each nation insists that it alone is the authentic continuation of the Pannonian Slavic tradition and the true heir of Great Moravia. The other group, in each telling, is descended from late Croatian or Slovene settlers who appropriated a language and a heritage that was never theirs. Neither claim has any basis in the historical record.

Origins of the Zguro-Deglian split

The names themselves are the first clue. Neither Zgurian nor Deglian is a Slavic word, and neither appears in any document before the mid-eighteenth century — a remarkable gap in a region with a continuous Glagolitic scribal tradition going back several centuries.

The most plausible account is reconstructed from Austrian military archives. Two Italian military contractors were active in Prince Eugene of Savoy's campaigns against the Ottomans in the early eighteenth century: Candido Sguro of Naples and Massimo Degliano of Genoa. Each raised a company of Slavic-speaking irregulars and led them in Habsburg service. This is attested in reliable Austrian documents. What came next has to be inferred.

After the war, these mercenaries were settled as Grenzer — frontier guards — along the Upper Pannonian segment of the new military frontier. They kept their commanders' names as unit designations. Over time, the name became an identity. Frontier soldiers enjoyed higher status than the surrounding peasantry, and the name carried a certain prestige; local Pannonian speakers in the surrounding countryside gradually began to adopt the same designation for themselves.

By the mid-eighteenth century, Austrian military correspondence refers to "Sgur Hussars" and "Deglian Haiduks" as distinct formations with recognizable home territories. By the early nineteenth century, Slavicized forms of both names — zgurcy in the north-west and degljane in the south-east — had entered the vocabulary of ethnic identification. The boundary between the two groups was never clean: they were thoroughly intermixed across the Pannonian interior, with family ties, shared parishes, and common markets crossing whatever line the ethnographers of the period tried to draw.

The conclusion is unavoidable: the Zguro-Deglian split is recent and essentially accidental. It was not the expression of a pre-existing cultural difference but its starting point. Both communities ended up with separate names and very little else to tell them apart — and, once those names became politically charged, with strong reasons to invent as much distinguishing content as possible. The origins of the quarrel between them are treated in a separate article. Here it is enough to note that the thinness of the underlying difference has produced an unusual amount of effort to assert it.

Differences

The differences that exist between the two cultures are real in the sense that people consistently act on them. They are not real in the sense of reflecting any deep historical divergence. Since the late Socialist era, both communities have devoted considerable effort to identifying, developing, and where necessary inventing markers that would make visible a distinction history had not provided.

Colors

The most conspicuous and pervasive difference derives from the rival football clubs. The Zgurian club, Sokol, plays in red and white. The Deglian club, Dynamo, plays in blue and white. This sporting allegiance has generated a system of color prohibitions that extends well beyond the stadium into every corner of daily life.

For a Zgurian, the combination of blue and white is the mark of the enemy. Individual colors are unremarkable; their conjunction is an affront. The prohibition has migrated from clothing to packaging, to digital interfaces, to the colors of household objects. Its current roster includes Facebook, Telegram, Nivea, Oreo biscuits, and — most colorfully — the Smurfs, a Belgian animated franchise whose small blue white-hooded protagonists have lent their name to one of the more popular Zgurian slurs for Deglians, smurfy. BMW is a notable exception from this blue-and-white blacklist.

For a Deglian, the tabooed combination is red and white. The corresponding prohibited list includes Coca-Cola, Nintendo, and Colgate — all identifiable by their red-and-white branding. A Deglian household typically keeps none of these. None are for sale in the Deglian Republic, though not officially prohibited.

Harder to avoid entirely are Adobe and YouTube, both carrying prominent red-and-white branding. Third-party utilities exist that render PDF icons in blue and white rather than the standard red. A mobile application called YouBlube, which recolors the YouTube interface throughout, is among the most-downloaded apps in the Deglian zone; it has been removed from the Google Play Store on multiple occasions and continues to circulate through informal channels.

Drinks

Separate from the football-derived prohibitions, and possibly older, is the question of rakia. Zgurians drink slivovica, the plum brandy. Deglians drink gruszevica, the pear brandy. The historical origin of this divergence is unknown; it appears in the earliest systematic ethnographic accounts of both communities and cannot be traced to any particular event. A Zgurian who drinks gruszevica is understood to be making a cultural statement, generally a hostile one. A Deglian offered slivovica at a wedding will decline on any plausible grounds.

At mixed-community social occasions — which occur more frequently than either side admits — the standard solution is to offer both, clearly labeled, on separate tables at opposite ends of the room. The host who simply puts out whatever is in the house will find the evening difficult to remember fondly.

Sedmaki and spremniki

Among the sedmaki and spremniki subcultures, the taboo system is substantially more elaborate and more strictly enforced. The full account of sedmak or spremnik brand prohibitions — extending to sportswear, fast-food chains, and technology brands, with Deglian and Zgurian representatives maintaining rigorously separate allegiances — is covered in the respective articles: Sedmaki, Spremniki. It is enough to note here that for both groups, the color prohibitions are not social conventions but articles of faith, and violations are treated accordingly.

Social enforcement

The question of enforcement is complicated by a consistent asymmetry between the treatment of outsiders and insiders.

A foreign visitor who arrives in a Zgurian district wearing blue and white, or in a Deglian district wearing red and white, will not be attacked. He will, within a short distance of the boundary, be approached by a passerby — typically an older resident, though age is not a requirement — and informed, with varying degrees of courtesy but consistent clarity, that he has made a mistake he should not repeat. The correction is offered as a genuine public service.

For a person known to belong to the other community, the same appearance carries a different weight and invites a different response. Wearing the wrong colors in the wrong district is read not as ignorance but as provocation. The social consequences range from collective cold-shouldering — being served last in shops, receiving no eye contact, finding that taxis do not slow down — to direct confrontation. Physical violence is not the default outcome but is not unusual.

The enforcement mechanisms are entirely informal. There is no institution whose mandate includes policing color prohibitions. What functions instead is social memory. A shopkeeper who sells blue-and-white wrapping paper in the Zgurian zone will lose customers without anyone explaining why. The most reliable enforcement mechanism is the extended family: a young person who is seen in the wrong colors will hear about it from every relative present at the next family meal, each of them independently, and at length.

One category of transgression is handled differently: a member of one's own community who adopts the enemy's color scheme is treated not as a fool but as a traitor. The practical distinction matters. A Deglian who accidentally buys red-and-white kitchen tiles without noticing the combination will receive patient correction and some sympathy. A Deglian who is seen to drink a Coca-Cola in public will receive neither.
