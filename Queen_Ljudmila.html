Queen Ljudmila

[REFINE]

Ljudmila Droganova Glovacz (Zgurian; Deglian: Lüdmila Droganova Glovač; Glagolitic: ⰎⰣⰄⰏⰊⰎⰀ ⰄⰓⰑⰃⰀⰐⰑⰂⰀ ⰃⰎⰑⰂⰀⰝ) (born January 10, 1941) is the only child of Queen Adele and the last claimant to the Pannonian throne. Born in wartime London and raised in New York by Pannonian émigré royalists, she spent most of her life distancing herself from her dynastic inheritance before becoming, in middle age, an active figure in international advocacy on Pannonian human rights. Origins and early life (1941–1952)

Ljudmila was born in London on January 10, 1941, officially as the posthumous daughter of King Drogan I. The record places the birth less than nine months after the King's assassination in April 1940; rumors attributing paternity to Randall Massey — Queen Adele's English secretary and probable British intelligence officer — were never officially addressed.

After Queen Adele's death in September 1952, the émigré community proclaimed the eleven-year-old Ljudmila Queen in exile. The Regent Council — a committee of five former officials managing the dynasty's legal affairs — arranged her transfer to the United States, where the Pannonian community was larger and the FBI agreed to provide protection against possible NOON assassination attempts.

In New York (1952–1959)

Ljudmila was adopted by Joshua Baumgarten, a distant maternal relative, whose parents had emigrated from Austria-Hungary in the early twentieth century. Joshua owned a fabric wholesale business in Queens; his wife Linda taught secondary school mathematics. They cared for their adopted daughter and never missed a chance to boast: "You know, our sweet Mila is the Queen of Upper Pannonia. Not kidding."

Ljudmila attended the Brearley School on a scholarship arranged by the émigré community. The FBI kept watch throughout the decade. Told about the surveillance at fourteen, she found it more embarrassing than reassuring.

The Pannonian émigré community organized around the Regent Council's American branch and several cultural associations. They needed Ljudmila as a symbolic center. She found the role increasingly unconvincing and later described Sunday visits to exile clubs — anthem renditions, speeches about the liberation of the homeland — as occasions of polite dread.

Yale and the rejection of royal identity (1959–1963)

Ljudmila, who insisted on being called Mila Baumgarten, was admitted to Yale in 1959 to read English literature. At Yale she avoided Eastern European student associations and declined invitations to speak on Pannonian affairs from groups of both left and right. She contributed reviews to a student literary magazine.

Her position, stated to that magazine in early 1959, was unambiguous: "I'm tired of this Disney fairytale shit about kings and princesses. My official biological father was a Fascist dictator, and my mother hated him. Not a kind of legacy to be proud of. Am I a Pannonian? Yep. Am I a Queen of Pannonia? Nope." She added that Gowbka's regime had strong popular support and the whole royalist movement was pointless and hopeless. The Regent Council issued a notice that Her Majesty's views did not represent those of the legitimate government.

The People's Republic was delighted. In 1960, Miljana Gowbka — wife of the Supreme Leader — wrote Ljudmila a personal letter offering a Category I apartment in Sockostur, a seat in the Legislative Assembly, and a New University professor position, on condition of formal abdication. Ljudmila refused even a visit: "Sorry, I don't want to be a propaganda asset." She explained to the Regent Council that abdication would disappoint the Baumgartens and the many émigrés who had supported her. Without much respect for the title she considered pointless, she nevertheless felt morally bound to people who evaluated it differently.

Ljudmila graduated in 1963 and took a job as editorial assistant at Meridian House, a small literary publisher in lower Manhattan specializing in translated fiction.

Return to Pannonian affairs (1963–1969)

Meridian House published Central and Eastern European writers, which drew Pannonian literature into Ljudmila's professional orbit. She began correspondence with Pannonian writers in exile in Paris and Vienna.

Gowbka's growing insanity drew her attention from the mid-1960s. The 1968 denunciation of the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia she found revealing. "He is calling Brezhnev an imperialist," she wrote to a Parisian émigré that autumn. "He may be correct. It changes nothing about what he is."

By then she had resumed contact with the exile community, attending cultural events while avoiding political ones. She revived ties mainly with a younger generation of émigrés — those who had fled in the late 1940s — rather than the aging Regent Council.

In 1969, she became the founding director of the Queen Ljudmila Foundation, a humanitarian NGO incorporated in New York. Its stated mission was documenting human rights conditions in Upper Pannonia and supporting Pannonian refugees. It claimed no political goals at first.
