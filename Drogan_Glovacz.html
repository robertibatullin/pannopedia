Drogan Martonov Glovacz (Zgurian; Deglian: Drogan Martonov Glovač; Glagolitic: ⰄⰓⰑⰃⰀⰐ ⰏⰀⰓⰕⰑⰐⰑⰂ ⰃⰎⰑⰂⰀⰝ; 1888–1940), also known as King Drogan the First (Krol Drogan Prjevy), was a Upper Pannonian military officer who rose from prisoner of war to Field Marshal, seized dictatorial power in 1931 as the leader of the UP National Empowerment Movement, and was crowned king in 1934. Early life and career (1888–1914)

Drogan Glovacz was born in 1888 in Przemyśl, a garrison town in Austrian Poland, where his father Marton, a Deglian, served as an artillery officer. His mother Katalina was Zgurian. He attended the Theresian Military Academy in Wiener Neustadt and was commissioned in 1909 as Leutnant in Infantry Regiment Nr. 77, a Galician unit garrisoned in Sambor. By 1913 he had risen to Oberleutnant. He later claimed lifelong Pannonian patriotism; his service record suggests nothing of the kind.

World War I (1914–1916)

In August 1914, Regiment Nr. 77 deployed to Galicia. Glovacz fought in the opening campaign, retreating with the Austro-Hungarian forces following the fall of Lemberg in September. He took part in the Gorlice-Tarnów offensive of May 1915, the combined German-Austro-Hungarian push that drove Russian forces back hundreds of kilometers, and was promoted to Hauptmann in its aftermath. The Brusilov Offensive of June 1916 destroyed the regiment's position near Lutsk. Glovacz was taken prisoner on 28 June 1916, one of some 400,000 Austro-Hungarian soldiers captured in that campaign.

Pannonian Legion (1916–1922)

Captured in June 1916, Glovacz was recruited into the Pannonian Legion (Pannonska Legya) from a POW camp near Penza that autumn. In early 1917, he arrived at the Caucasian front. He commanded a battalion under Colonel Antal Zsigmondov through the attritional operations southeast of Erzurum until the Erzincan armistice of December 1917. By Glovacz's own later account, the year in Transcaucasia was Zsigmondov's choice, not his. Serving the Georgian Democratic Republic as an auxiliary security force along the Kutaisi-Batumi corridor while waiting for an evacuation route was, in Glovacz's telling, Zsigmondov's preference for comfort over risk.

In August 1918, Zsigmondov was killed in a patrol skirmish near Kutaisi. Glovacz assumed command and terminated the Georgian arrangement within days. After the Armistice of Mudros opened the Turkish Straits in October, British vessels carried the Legion to Trieste. The SCS government denied railway access, hostile to Pannonian independence. Glovacz marched overland: four firefights with SCS border detachments, then a five-day midwinter crossing of the Nexoszi Vrjexi range. Six were killed; five died of exposure. The Legion entered Vronovica in late February 1919. The march was subsequently named the Pannonian Anabasis and became the central myth of the Glovacz regime, progressively embellished until it bore little resemblance to events. Zsigmondov's name did not appear in any version of it.

The 1919 Bolshevik insurrection gave Glovacz his first exercise of force within Pannonia. Inspired by Béla Kun's Hungarian Soviet Republic, Communist activists rose in Vronovica's Zadravska industrial quarter and the northern mining towns in April; three Third International delegates — Erwin Bach, Raphael Schwach, and Erzsebet Karácsony — coordinated the rising and proclaimed a Pannonian Soviet Republic. Hungarian minority supplied irregular volunteers and weapons from across the border. The Legion suppressed the rising sequentially — border formations, then mining towns, then the Zadravska factories — through May and into summer. Military tribunals produced executions numbering in the dozens; Bach, Schwach, and Karácsony were among those shot. The broader labour movement was left intact, ensuring the Communists would remain a live threat to be dealt with again later. Glovacz's subsequent propaganda reframed the events as the defeat of a full Hungarian military invasion. The truth — that he had used an unaccountable force to suppress a domestic workers' uprising — served him less well.

By 1920 he was the decisive power in a country whose government could not function without him and could not comfortably function with him. The independence plebiscite of 1922 gave the parliamentary coalition its pretext: with UP's status confirmed, the Legion was dissolved and its men discharged or absorbed into the regular army. Glovacz accepted the rank of General and a ceremonial post. He was thirty-four years old and willing to wait.
