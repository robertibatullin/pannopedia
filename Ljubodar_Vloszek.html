Ljubodar Vloszek

Ljubodar Laslov Vloszek (Deglian: Lübodar Laslov Vlošek; Glagolitic: ⰎⰣⰁⰑⰄⰀⰓ ⰎⰀⰔⰎⰑⰂ ⰂⰎⰑⰞⰅⰍ; 1939–2001) was an Upper Pannonian writer and politician. Through a combination of political loyalty, facility with popular narrative, and a well-placed pen, he became the People's Republic's most commercially successful author and, after a timely defection and a career in émigré politics, the first President of the Zgurian Republic.

His place in the public record is contested. The claim, sustained by Vloszek himself for the rest of his life, that he had been a covert dissident from the beginning was received enthusiastically by the Western press and with more measured scepticism by his Pannonian contemporaries. His presidency (1996–2001) was marked principally by the systematic advancement of relatives and clients through the civilian institutional apparatus — a pattern that had, by his death in office, displaced the wartime military commanders from the influence they had held in 1995.

Early Life (1939–1957)

Vloszek was born in 1939 in the Third District of Vronovica, the fourth of six children. His father held a minor clerical position in the Ministry of Radio and Telegraphy; his mother did not work outside the household. The family was lower middle-class — respectable, but not resourceful.

The Hungarian occupation ended the father's employment; Slavic ministerial staff were systematically dismissed, Laslo Vloszek among them. The family survived on reduced means, supplemented by whatever the extended family could provide. Vloszek later claimed both parents had participated in the Communist Resistance; no documentary evidence supports the claim.

After liberation, Laslo Vloszek was restored to state employment within months — whatever sympathies the family had held, they proved compatible with the new order. He converted his given name from the Hungaricized Laslo to the Slavic Vlodislav, a change legally obligatory since 1945 and one the family appears to have embraced willingly. The Vloszeks were, by all available evidence, genuinely pro-Communist.

Ljubodar attended secondary school in the Third District, joining the Communist Youth Union at fifteen. He was not academically distinguished but noted for verbal aptitude and a marked interest in writing.

Education and Early Career (1957–1965)

Vloszek completed mandatory military service in 1959 and enrolled at the Journalist Faculty of the New University. His studies were competent rather than notable; he graduated without distinction in 1964, having already begun contributing to several Vronovica newspapers, and continued in this capacity after graduation.

It was during this period, according to later rumors, that Vloszek was approached by NOON and enrolled as a civilian informant. He resolutely denied this in his memoirs, and no confirming documentary evidence has been published. His later career suggests intimate NOON connections, and if he had actually been offered collaboration, he would likely not have declined, being a discreet Communist and a patriot eager to serve his country.

"Secret Diary of Queen Adele" (1965)

Vloszek's first significant work was not fiction — or did not claim to be. In 1965 he published what he presented as the recently discovered personal diary of Queen Adele, supposedly found among papers in a private collection. The document was a fabrication — a fact apparent from the very beginning, though never officially acknowledged during the People's Republic period.

The "diary" depicted Adele as a promiscuous adventuress whose intimate relations had encompassed multiple men and women and, in certain passages, some animals; as a meticulous spy working simultaneously for British, American, and Soviet intelligence services; and as a cynical manipulator whose charitable public image concealed crimes against the Pannonian people.

An abridged domestic edition with censored passages was published in Upper Pannonia1; the complete version appeared in Vienna through a press whose close ties to NOON's foreign operations directorate were poorly concealed2. The domestic edition ran to multiple printings, was classified as an authenticated historical document, and was cited by Gowbka himself in a 1968 speech — rendering its official authenticity irrefutable.

The Radlov Series (1968–1975)

Adventures of Captain Radlov (1968)

In 1968 Vloszek published his fiction debut: a spy novel featuring NOON counter-intelligence operative Captain Radlov3. The plot concerns a conspiracy by the automotive industries of three former Axis nations — West German BMW, Italian Ferrari, and Japanese Mitsubishi — to steal secrets from the Gowbkaslava Automobile Plant. For this they send three agents: a former SS officer, a mafioso, and a ninja. Radlov defeats all three, exposes the conspiracy, and marries a beautiful blonde engineer from the Drava 67 design team.

The novel was well-constructed entertainment: plotting competent, pace reliable, politics embedded in the action rather than delivered as separate lectures — a distinction that set it apart from most PRUP fiction. It sold very well and made Vloszek, at twenty-nine, the most prominent popular writer in Upper Pannonia.

Adventures of Major Radlov (1970)

The 1970 sequel4 sent the promoted Radlov to the United States, where he encountered American society assembled in full accordance with PRUP ideological expectation: magnates in top hats conducting business from mahogany boardrooms; Prohibition-style gangsters in fedoras, armed with Tommy guns; Ku Klux Klan lynchers active on the streets of New York City; and an oppressed proletariat on the verge of socialist revolution. The principal antagonists were the CIA hitman John Killer and the seductress Victoria Secret, whose names the author evidently regarded as self-explanatory.

Adventures of Major Radlov exceeded its predecessor in sales. It was subsequently translated and published in the United States by a small Californian Maoist press5, making Vloszek one of the very few PRUP authors to achieve Western distribution.

Adventures of Colonel Radlov (1975)

The third volume dispatched the now-Colonel Radlov to the Moon, with a mission to prevent a Selenite invasion6. Vloszek's engagement with astronomy and astronautics proved uneven, and the novel attracted considerable criticism for scientific errors of a fairly elementary kind. Sales fell short of the first two volumes, and domestic reviews — never hostile to a loyal author without cause — were noticeably cold.

Vloszek had by this point outlined a fourth volume, Adventures of General Radlov, in which the series would have culminated with a mission to the Sun. He did not write it.

Personal Life (1960–1977)

The first Radlov novel was dedicated "to my wife Slavica"; the second, "to my beloved wife Ursula"; the third, "to my precious angel Isabelle, the most admirable woman in the world." This succession of dedications traced Vloszek's rise through the social hierarchy.

His first wife, Slavica, had been his university classmate — an unremarkable journalist. The success of the first Radlov novel made Vloszek wealthy; authors' royalties were one of the few legal paths to private fortune in the Socialist world, the PRUP being no exception. He divorced Slavica, providing generous alimony, and married Ursula Raczeva, daughter of the patriarch of Pannonian Socialist-Realist literature: Domenik Raczev, Chairman of the PRUP Union of Writers, three-time Gowbka Prize laureate, and "Pannonian Gorky."

The second Radlov novel made Vloszek the officially wealthiest man in Upper Pannonia and introduced him into the highest elite circles. Among those who sought his company was Boris Gowbka, known as Borko — the supreme leader's only son and deputy chief of NOON. The two became close friends. In 1973, Vloszek divorced Ursula and married Isabelle Ryzsak, the established mistress of Borko. This marriage was, as Vloszek later confessed, designed to conceal Borko's affair with Isabelle by presenting Vloszek as a sham husband; the arrangement was not, however, entirely fictitious. Borko, Vloszek, and Isabelle constituted what was in effect a ménage à trois, drawing the two men into still closer friendship and mutual confidence. This relationship proved fatal for Borko and Isabelle, and, in a sense, for Vloszek himself.

"Vronovica in 2027" (1977)

In 1977 Vloszek published Vronovica in 20277, a utopian science fiction novel commissioned by Boris Gowbka and dedicated to him. Its publication marked a turning point in Vloszek's career.

Vronovica in 2027 depicts the city as the capital of a communist world. Proletarian revolutions have swept the remaining capitalist governments; the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia, conceding the superior clarity of Marxist-Gowbkaist doctrine, have voluntarily amalgamated with Vronovica at the ideological centre. The city has been rebuilt as a showpiece of the socialist future: marble skyscrapers rise above kilometre-wide avenues lined with flowering lime trees; above the rooftops, Drava 2025 flycars on atomic energy trace orderly lanes through the clear air. Every citizen occupies a private apartment at one room per person, receives goods by pneumatic post free of charge, and watches colour TV broadcasts. No one waits in a line; no one lacks anything.

Throughout the novel, colossal monuments to Severyn Gowbka line the city's boulevards, and he is invoked in every chapter as "the greatest leader and founder of our state." Yet the supreme authority of 2027 is never Gowbka himself. The ruling figure appears only as a leader "whose face and voice so closely resemble those of our greatest founder" — a formulation passed without remark by the censor's office. Severyn Gowbka read it differently.

Gowbka's response came at the October 1977 Central Committee Plenum, denouncing the novel as "ideological blindness" and "a grotesque caricature of the Communist future." The book was removed from sale the same day; Vloszek was expelled from the Union of Writers and his titles struck from official catalogues. Simultaneously, a NOON task force began systematic arrests among Boris Gowbka's associates. Isabelle Ryzsak was among those detained; none reappeared. Boris Gowbka died of a cardiac arrest in March 1978. The official death certificate recorded a natural cause, and no independent physician examined the body.

Vloszek concluded that his own arrest was a matter of time. In April 1978 he crossed into Austria through a minor mountain checkpoint, reportedly after paying the border patrol three months of average wages, and presented himself to the Radio Liberty offices in Munich. His statement, broadcast within the week, was sensational: he confirmed that the Secret Diary of Queen Adele had been fabricated on NOON instructions, issued a public apology to Queen-in-exile Ljudmila, and announced that both the Radlov series and Vronovica in 2027 had been composed as covert parodies concealing their subversive content beneath a surface of loyal entertainment. He declared himself a lifelong dissident who had at last escaped the country he had always opposed. The Western press received the revelation with enthusiasm and the minimum of scepticism.

Emigration (1978–1990)

Radio Liberty

Vloszek's statement earned him immediate engagement at Radio Liberty's Pannonian-language service. His broadcasts covered political developments inside the People's Republic, literary commentary, and accounts of his PRUP experience. He proved a skilled broadcaster: the same narrative gift that had carried his fiction made his programmes engaging, and his insider knowledge gave them a credibility that émigré journalism rarely achieved.

The broadcasts also established his characteristic public posture in emigration. He presented his years in the PRUP apparatus not as collaboration but as infiltration: every loyalty performed, every prize accepted, every dedication inscribed in a Radlov novel had been, he maintained, a calculated act of concealment by a secret opponent of the regime. The argument was not entirely implausible, but required his audience to extend considerable charity regarding the Secret Diary of Queen Adele. Most Western listeners were prepared to do so.

"Adventures of Inmate Radlov" (1981)

In 1981 Vloszek published Adventures of Inmate Radlov8, recycling the series' protagonist in radically altered circumstances. Radlov, now a PRUP corrective labour camp prisoner, wages a one-man resistance campaign, uncovers a conspiracy by which the commandant sells political prisoners to Chinese pharmaceutical companies as experimental subjects, and escapes to deliver evidence to a Radio Liberty correspondent. The political valences were precisely inverted from the original series; the plotting and characterisation were not otherwise improved. Western reviewers, grateful for the ideological gesture, received it warmly. Pannonian émigré readers were more divided.

The novel was dedicated "to my beloved Carolyn, whose clear eyes saw what I could only conceal." The dedicatee was Carolyn Whitmore, a Cornell Slavic Studies graduate twenty years Vloszek's junior, whom he had met at a Washington symposium on Eastern European dissident literature in 1979 and married the following year. Whitmore was a vigorous human rights activist with connections in the State Department's cultural programmes office, and an ardent admirer of Vloszek's writing; she was among the first Western advocates of the thesis that the Radlov series had always been a brilliant work of deliberate parody. Vloszek found this interpretation congenial and did nothing to discourage it.

Queen Ljudmila Foundation

Vloszek's émigré standing was consolidated through the Queen Ljudmila Foundation, a Washington-registered cultural organization funded substantially by the US State Department. Vloszek first received Foundation grants in 1983 and was appointed to its advisory board in 1985, a position that conferred institutional standing and a modest but reliable income.

He used this position in ways that foreshadowed his later practice. By the mid-1980s several relatives had received Foundation grants for émigré ventures — applications Vloszek had facilitated privately. The pattern attracted no comment; the sums were small, and subsidising émigré activity was the Foundation's stated purpose. The habit of converting institutional access into family benefit was nevertheless established.

Standing by 1990

By the close of the 1980s Vloszek had accumulated a reputation that was, by the standards of exile politics, substantial. He was the best-known Pannonian writer in the Western world and the leading intellectual voice of the Pannonian democratic opposition, according to the émigré press and Western commentary alike. When the collapse of Communism in 1990 brought the prospect of return into view, he was as well-positioned as any figure in the émigré opposition.

Post-Communist Career (1990–2001)

Return to Upper Pannonia (1990)

Vloszek returned to Vronovica in the spring of 1990 after twelve years in emigration. His confiscated property was restored and his banned works rehabilitated. Several newly formed democratic parties approached him simultaneously, competing for the credibility his association would confer.

He stood for parliament in autumn 1990 as the leading candidate of the Pannonian Democratic Renovation Party. The nationalist parties each captured approximately forty-five per cent of seats, leaving the PDRP's seven per cent in the position of kingmaker. Vloszek entered parliament as a minor but arithmetically indispensable figure — a role that suited his actual talents considerably better than the disinterested moral authority he had cultivated in emigration.

The Civil War (1992–1995)

When armed conflict broke out in 1992, Vloszek — Zgurian by origin — positioned himself as a neutral conciliator above ethnic loyalties. The logic was sound regardless of motive: a man with connections to both the Zgurian establishment and the Western institutions required to broker any settlement was more valuable as a mediator than as a partisan.

His most significant initiative of the period was the 1994 project to restore the Pannonian monarchy. Vloszek served as the principal intermediary between Queen-in-exile Ljudmila and the network of political and military figures inside the country whose cooperation any restoration required. The negotiations drew on a breadth of personal contacts — across factional lines and across decades — that suggested to some observers his old NOON connections had never been entirely dormant. The project collapsed with the assassination of Queen Ljudmila in late 1994. Vloszek left the country within days, citing credible threats from factions on both sides.

Zgurian Presidency (1996–2001)

After the 1995 ceasefire Vloszek returned and resumed his career within the Zgurian Republic. Effective power resided with the military commanders who had emerged from the war; those commanders, however, required a presentable civilian figurehead for negotiations with Western governments. Vloszek — possessed of Western credentials, an American wife with State Department connections, and a reputation, however assembled, as a democratic intellectual — was an obvious candidate. The Zgurian parliament elected him President in 1996.

The office carried no executive authority. Vloszek made no attempt to acquire any directly. What he acquired instead was leverage. The warlords needed his cooperation — his signature, his ceremonial appearances, his telephone calls to foreign officials — and leverage was tradeable. Over the following five years he placed relatives, in-laws, and clients in positions of commercial and bureaucratic influence: directorial posts, trade licences, procurement committees. The terms of exchange were understood by both sides and rarely made explicit. In at least one documented instance he reportedly offered to intercede with international war crimes investigators on a military commander's behalf in exchange for the appointment of a nephew to the chairmanship of the Vronovica Trade Chamber.

Vloszek died in office in April 2001. By that point his extended family — children from his first and fourth marriages, nephews, and more distant connections — occupied a disproportionate share of the Zgurian Republic's civilian institutional positions. The process had, gradually and without any single decisive moment, displaced the wartime commanders from the corridors of influence they had held in 1995. Vloszek left no political programme and no durable party. He left the Vloszeks.

Legacy

Vloszek's literary reputation survives in ambiguous form. The Radlov novels remain in print as period artefacts of People's Republic popular culture. The political inheritance is more legible. The Vloszeks — children, nephews, in-laws, and more distant connections installed during the presidency — retained influence well past his death, forming a recognisable network within the Zgurian Republic's civilian administration and commercial sector. In this sense Vloszek succeeded in building something durable, though not the thing his public persona had promised.

The question that has animated the most sustained historical debate — whether his subsequent career as dissident and president represented a genuine reversal or merely the same adaptive intelligence applied to a changed environment — is unlikely to be settled. The relevant archives, to the extent they survived the civil war, remain classified in both successor states.
